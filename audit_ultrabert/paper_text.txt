Abstract
Recent bidirectional encoders have been modernized with innovations from causal large language models (LLMs). However, the pretraining recipe itself remains inherited from small-scale conventions, leaving encoders underoptimized, with no single model performing competitively across both classification and retrieval tasks. In this work, we investigate how far encoder performance can be pushed by revisiting core design choices in training, revealing three key findings: (1) Encoders saturate on downstream tasks beyond a few trillion tokens, indicating that additional compute past this point does not help. (2) Across model sizes, lower masking ratios benefit classification while higher ones benefit retrieval, contradicting prior work suggesting larger ratios for larger models. (3) Distillation with pruning improves classification at low masking ratios but degrades retrieval at high ones. Guided by these findings, we present UltraBERT, a single encoder that assigns separate LM heads to low and high masking ratios within a batch and restricts the distillation objective to the low ratio. To our knowledge, UltraBERT is the first encoder to achieve state of the art on both classification and retrieval tasks, reaching 91.0 on GLUE and 47.7 on BEIR. Together, these results show how far encoder performance can be pushed and provide a principled basis for compute allocation, masking ratio design, and knowledge transfer in bidirectional models.
1
Introduction
Bidirectional encoders remain essential for non-generative applications, even amid the rapid progress of decoder-only large language models (LLMs). Their modest inference cost allows them to embed or score large volumes of text efficiently, making them the backbone of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipelines
(
Lewis et al., 2020
)
, quality filtering of pre-training data
(
Penedo et al., 2024
)
, and reward modeling for reinforcement learning
(
Gisserot-Boukhlef et al., 2026b
)
.
While decoders owe much of their popularity to their generalization to unseen tasks, fine-tuned encoders have often been shown to match much larger decoders on well-defined tasks at a fraction of the cost
(
Bucher and Martini, 2024
;
Edwards and Camacho-Collados, 2024
;
Zhang et al., 2025b
)
.
Recent work has increasingly modernized encoders with innovations from decoder-only LLMs
(
Portes et al., 2023
;
Warner et al., 2025
;
Breton et al., 2025
)
, adopting architectural advances such as rotary position embeddings
(
Su et al., 2024
)
, FlashAttention-2
(
Dao, 2024
)
, along with token scaling
(
Sardana et al., 2024
)
.
However, the pre-training recipe itself remains inherited from small-scale conventions. In particular, the masked language modeling (MLM) objective, which is specific to encoders, is still configured with lessons from prior studies under limited token budgets
(
Wettig et al., 2023
;
Gisserot-Boukhlef et al., 2026a
)
, where convergence speed is entangled with final performance.
This core component trades off classification against retrieval, leaving encoders trained with a single configuration specialized, with no single model performing competitively on both.
In this work, we begin by revisiting encoder pretraining at scale, and reveal three key findings. (1) Training encoders on up to 8.8T tokens, we find that downstream performance saturates around 1–2T tokens even as training and validation losses keep decreasing, unlike decoders, which continue to improve. This indicates an upper bound that additional tokens cannot raise. (2) At saturation, where convergence speed no longer confounds the comparison, classification consistently favors lower masking ratios across model sizes, contradicting prior work suggesting larger ratios for larger models, whereas retrieval favors higher ones. (3) Unlike in decoders, pruning and distillation from a stronger teacher does not uniformly raise encoder performance: it improves classification at a low masking ratio but degrades retrieval at a high one.

Guided by these findings, we present UltraBERT, which reconciles the two masking regimes within a single encoder rather than settling for a single configuration. Observing that the two regimes shape the LM head into distinct geometries,
we propose
LM head decoupling
, which mixes 5% and 50% masked sequences within each batch while assigning a separate LM head to each ratio.
Furthermore, we restrict distillation to the low-ratio sequences, while training the high-ratio ones with standard MLM.
Starting from a 1.3B teacher, we obtain 380M and 150M models through pruning and distillation, trained on 2T tokens with native 32K context.
Across classification and retrieval, where the strongest baseline differs, UltraBERT achieves state of the art on both, reaching 91.0 on GLUE
(
Wang et al., 2018
)
and 47.7 on BEIR
(
Thakur et al., 2021
)
.
Our 150M model matches RoBERTa-large
(
Liu et al., 2019
)
on classification with 2.3
fewer parameters, and outperforms Ettin-encoder-400M
(
Weller et al., 2026
)
on retrieval at 2.6
smaller size. Together, these results redefine what a single encoder can achieve, pushing encoder pretraining beyond its presumed limits.
2
Related Work
Modernized encoders
Since BERT
(
Devlin et al., 2019
)
and RoBERTa
(
Liu et al., 2019
)
, the development of encoders has been slower than that of decoder-only LLMs. Recent work has revived encoders by bringing in innovations from LLMs, such as RoPE, GLU, and alternating local and global attention, along with trillions of training tokens
(
Portes et al., 2023
;
Nussbaum et al., 2025
;
Zhang et al., 2024
;
Warner et al., 2025
;
Breton et al., 2025
;
Weller et al., 2026
)
. However, the pre-training recipe itself, in particular the MLM objective and its masking ratio, has been inherited from the conventions of the BERT era. As a result, each of these encoders excels on one side only, with none performing competitively across both classification and retrieval.
Masking ratio and pre-training objectives
The masking ratio of MLM has been revisited in several studies, which report that ratios higher than the original 15% are beneficial, especially for larger models
(
Wettig et al., 2023
;
Ankner et al., 2024
;
Yang et al., 2023
;
Dong et al., 2025
;
Gisserot-Boukhlef et al., 2026a
)
. However, these findings are based on budgets of 10B–100B tokens, where the effect of the masking ratio is entangled with convergence speed. Another line of work replaces MLM with Replaced Token Detection (RTD)
(
Clark et al., 2020
;
He et al., 2023
)
, which is considered superior on classification tasks but is known to degrade retrieval
(
Rep et al., 2024
;
Warner et al., 2025
)
. This comparison, however, has always been made at the conventional 15% masking, without varying the corruption level. In contrast, our work revisits both questions after reaching saturation with trillions of tokens, on both classification and retrieval tasks.
Encoders for retrieval
Bidirectional encoders are also widely adopted for information retrieval. Bi-encoders are trained to encode a given text into a single vector, which is aligned to be close to those of similar texts
(
Reimers and Gurevych, 2019
)
. They usually start from MLM pre-training to acquire basic linguistic knowledge, and then go through contrastive fine-tuning
(
Wang et al., 2022
;
Li et al., 2023
;
Nussbaum et al., 2025
;
Zhang et al., 2024
)
. While the fine-tuning stage has been extensively optimized, the pre-training recipe of the backbone determines the upper bound of the resulting model. Encoders designed for retrieval, however, tend to fall behind on classification.
Knowledge distillation for language models
Knowledge distillation for encoders has been proposed to match various targets: masked token reconstruction logits
(
Sanh et al., 2019
;
Jiao et al., 2020
)
, hidden state vectors
(
Sanh et al., 2019
;
Jiao et al., 2020
)
, self-attention scores
(
Jiao et al., 2020
;
Wang et al., 2020
)
, and Gram matrices of attention vectors
(
Wang et al., 2020
;
Wang et al., 2021
)
. Meanwhile, recent LLMs usually adopt logit-based distillation to develop small language models
(
Gemma Team et al., 2024
;
Gemma Team et al., 2025
;
Amini et al., 2025
;
Meta AI, 2024
)
. In particular,
Muralidharan et al. (2024)
and
Liu et al. (2026)
propose
Cascade Distillation
, where a small model is initialized from the pruned weights of its parent and then distilled from a stronger teacher. However, whether these approaches transfer to encoders, and how they interact with the masking ratio, remains unexplored.
3
Exploring the Pre-Training Recipe for Stronger Encoders
In this section, we examine the core design choices of MLM pre-training under controlled settings. We train encoders at three scales (150M, 380M, and 1.3B) with RoPE and alternating local and global attention, similar to
Warner et al. (2025)
. All experiments use the same dataset mixture and tokenizer. Following the evaluation protocol of
Warner et al. (2025)
, we fine-tune and evaluate on GLUE
(
Wang et al., 2018
)
and SQuAD 2.0
(
Rajpurkar et al., 2018
)
for classification, and fine-tune on MS MARCO
(
Nguyen et al., 2016
)
and report the average nDCG@10 on BEIR
(
Thakur et al., 2021
)
for retrieval. Further details of the experimental setup are provided in Appendix
A
and
B
.
3.1
Training Token Scaling and Downstream Saturation
Prior work has demonstrated that decoder models continue to improve at extreme token scale
(
Sardana et al., 2024
)
, and that their downstream task performance is also predictable alongside loss
(
Gadre et al., 2025
)
. This raises a natural question: can encoder models also benefit from more training tokens? To answer this question, we train encoders at two scales (150M and 1.3B) on 8.8 trillion tokens, with a masking ratio of 30%. We then evaluate intermediate checkpoints on GLUE, SQuAD 2.0, and BEIR to observe how downstream performance evolves.
The results are shown in Figure
2
. Encoders trained with the MLM objective saturate around 1–2 trillion tokens, while both training and validation losses keep decreasing. In particular, the BEIR scores saturate even earlier, showing no significant improvement beyond the first evaluation point of 275B tokens. These observations indicate that (1) encoders do not continue to improve on downstream tasks with more training tokens, and (2) unlike decoders, their fine-tuned downstream performance is not directly correlated with loss.

Scaling the training token budget.
Pre-training loss and downstream performance of 150M and 1.3B encoders. In (a), solid lines are the training loss (left axis) and dashed lines are the validation loss on WikiText-103
(
Merity et al., 2017
)
(right axis). (b) GLUE average score, (c) SQuAD 2.0 F1, and (d) BEIR average nDCG@10 are obtained.
We then ask whether this saturation reflects a fundamental difference between encoders and decoders. To this end, we evaluate the intermediate checkpoints of our 1.3B encoder and the SmolLM2 1.7B decoder
(
Ben Allal et al., 2025
)
on 6 benchmarks most widely used for decoders.
Figure
3
shows clearly divergent trends. For base models, both the encoder and decoder improve in 5-shot accuracy, and the decoder in particular exhibits a rapid rise known as
emergent abilities
(
Wei et al., 2022
)
. However, after instruction tuning on the multiple-choice question answering (MCQA) format, the encoder saturates as on the previous downstream tasks, while the decoder keeps improving.
These results suggest that encoder models have an upper bound on downstream performance that additional pre-training tokens cannot raise. If we have enough compute budget to exceed the saturation point, how can we raise this upper bound itself? This question strongly motivates us to revisit the encoder pre-training methodology.

3.2
Masking Ratios and Classification Tasks
The MLM objective differs from the causal LM objective in one respect: the masking ratio. Its impact has been investigated in various contexts
(
Dong et al., 2025
;
Yang et al., 2023
;
Wettig et al., 2023
;
Gisserot-Boukhlef et al., 2026a
;
Boizard et al., 2025
)
, yet its effect has been entangled with convergence speed. In this section, we revisit it from the perspective of downstream classification tasks, but after reaching saturation, where this entanglement no longer applies.
In detail, we train encoders at three scales with masking ratios up to 50%. As lower ratios require more tokens to converge
(
Wettig et al., 2023
;
Ankner et al., 2024
)
, we allocate tokens accordingly

Dong et al., 2025
)
, which finds larger masking ratios optimal for larger models. We attribute this discrepancy to eliminating the effect of convergence speed by using trillions of tokens, whereas prior results were confined to 10B–100B budgets. Notably,
Wettig et al. (2023)
argued that higher ratios are preferable since the gain from faster convergence outweighs the cost of corruption, treating the benefit of less corruption only briefly. At saturation, however, we find the limitation from corruption to be far more pronounced.
Impact of contextual corruption
To understand the effect of corruption on encoders, we further compare MLM with RTD.
Conventionally, ELECTRA-style models are considered to be superior on classification tasks
(
Clark et al., 2020
;
He et al., 2023
)
, and they remain so even against recent modernized encoders trained with MLM
(
Antoun et al., 2025
)
, though at a critical cost on retrieval
(
Rep et al., 2024
;
Warner et al., 2025
)
.
The superiority of RTD is often explained by its higher training efficiency
(
Clark et al., 2020
)
. Training to saturation factors out this efficiency, allowing us to isolate what makes RTD superior beyond faster convergence.
One remaining difference lies in
contextual corruption
, i.e., how contextually plausible the corrupted tokens are. In RTD, a generator fills masked tokens with semantically relevant alternatives, which are more informative than the
[MASK]
token. We hypothesize that this difference mainly explains the superiority.

3.3
Masking Ratios and Retrieval Tasks
Lower masking ratios favor classification, but it is unclear whether the same holds for downstream retrieval tasks, the other major application of bidirectional encoders. Beyond the classification tasks, we now examine retrieval, reporting the average nDCG@10 on BEIR as the downstream performance. Figure
6
(a) shows the opposite trend to classification. The masking ratio of 50% consistently achieves the highest score across all model scales, which is also in line with a few prior works
(
Boizard et al., 2025
;
Gisserot-Boukhlef et al., 2026a
)
. Overall, no single masking ratio is optimal for both classification and retrieval.
Figure 6:
Masking ratios and retrieval.
(a) BEIR average nDCG@10 against the masking ratio for 150M, 380M, and 1.3B encoders. (b) Effective rank of the 150M model against the masking ratio. (c) GLUE average score and (d) BEIR average nDCG@10 of the 150M encoder pre-trained at 6% masking with (dashed) and without (solid) the KoLeo regularizer.
To understand why higher masking ratios benefit retrieval, we look into what changes in the representation space.
We measure the effective rank
(
Roy and Vetterli, 2007
)
of the mean-pooled vectors of each trained model. The effective rank reflects how many dimensions in the representation space the model actually uses. As shown in Figure
6
(b), the effective rank tends to increase with the masking ratio, indicating that higher masking ratios lead to higher utilization of the representation space.
We cannot tell whether the higher effective rank causes the improvement in retrieval, or whether the two are independent phenomena.
Since using more dimensions is generally favorable for retrieval
(
Kusupati et al., 2022
;
Rottach et al., 2025
)
, we ask whether the effective rank can be raised by regularization instead.
As we need to use a lower masking ratio for classification, we train the encoder with 6% masking but try adopting the KoLeo regularizer
(
Sablayrolles et al., 2019
)
, which is used for representation learning
(
Oquab et al., 2024
;
Siméoni et al., 2026
)
.
This encourages the vectors to spread isotropically, resulting in a higher effective rank
(
Zhang et al., 2025a
)
.

Figure

Raising the effective rank artificially cannot resolve the trade-off at hand: (1) the regularizer indeed improves BEIR but less than single 50% masking, and (2) it also harms the GLUE score. From this experiment, we confirm the strong contrast between classification and retrieval across masking ratios, and the limitation of using a single masking ratio.
3.4
Mixing Masking Ratios with LM Head Decoupling
From the previous sections, we observed that different masking ratios specialize encoders for different tasks, and that regularization cannot naturally resolve the trade-off. Since the two tasks favor opposite masking ratios, achieving the best of both without compromise can be viewed as multi-task learning (MTL). Under this view, we instead consider mixing multiple masking ratios within a batch, treating each ratio as a task and jointly optimizing their losses.
MTL frequently faces conflicts across tasks due to parameter sharing
(
Sener and Koltun, 2018
;
Yu et al., 2020
)
.
He et al. (2023)
claimed that sharing input embeddings between the generator and discriminator leads to tug-of-war dynamics, where the two disturb each other, and resolved this with gradient-level disentanglement. Similarly, we observed the divergence of the encoders’ representation spaces through their effective rank in Section
3.3
. We thus visualize the input embeddings and LM heads to look for such a conflict.

The discrepancy is also observed in Figure

The LM head for 6% masking shows more concentrated singular values, indicating that the matrix occupies a lower-dimensional subspace. This is consistent with the model’s lower effective rank. The encoder’s gradients are computed from the loss through its LM head, so we conjecture that sharing it may cause gradient-level interference, and test decoupling the two heads.

3.5
Revisiting Pruning and Distillation
We resolve the trade-off between classification and retrieval with LM head decoupling, but each is still bounded by what the model can reach on its own. To go beyond the limit, we revisit pruning and distillation, as in
Sanh et al. (2019)
,
Muralidharan et al. (2024)
, and
Liu et al. (2026)
, under different masking ratios.
For distillation, we restrict ourselves to logit-based distillation, which is still widely used in recent LLMs. Attention-based distillation, including self-attention scores
(
Jiao et al., 2020
;
Wang et al., 2020
)
and Gram matrices
(
Wang et al., 2020
;
Wang et al., 2021
)
, requires
memory and computational costs for length
. These are unaffordable for long contexts and incompatible with FlashAttention.

This degradation may come from the trivial signal of the teacher at 50% masking, similar to the interpretation of RTD in Section

. Regardless of the underlying cause, pruning and distillation yield no gains over training from scratch at 50% masking, motivating us to apply distillation only at the low ratio.
4
UltraBERT: Beyond the Limit of BERT Pre-Training

Overview of UltraBERT.

Building on these findings, we introduce
UltraBERT
, a family of encoders in two sizes (150M and 380M) with 32K context that pushes the limit of existing encoders on both classification and retrieval without trade-off. Following

and

3.4
, we construct each batch with both 5% and 50% masking at a 4:1 ratio, with decoupled LM heads. Following Section
3.5
, we apply KL divergence loss only to the 5% masked sequences, while the 50% ones are trained with the standard MLM loss.

As shown in Table
3
, UltraBERT achieves state-of-the-art performance on almost all benchmarks. For the base size, UltraBERT achieves the best score on all benchmarks except MultiCoNER v2, where it still outperforms both ModernBERT and Ettin Encoder. For the medium size, UltraBERT achieves the best on all benchmarks except MLDR, where it is behind LFM2.5-Encoder-350M.
As the UltraBERT series natively supports 32K context length, both scales achieve the best score on LongEmbed, where even the base model outperforms the medium models of other families. Furthermore, the largest improvements are on code and math retrieval, with gains of 7.3 and 8.3 points at base and 8.4 and 7.7 points at medium over the second best, respectively.
Most importantly, previous encoders each excel in different areas. On GLUE, SQuAD 2.0, and RewardBench, Ettin Encoder was the best among prior models. On the NER tasks, NomicBERT, RoBERTa, and GTE-en-MLM led. On BEIR, ModernBERT was the strongest base model and LFM2.5-Encoder-350M the strongest medium model. None of them, however, reaches the top across the full range of benchmarks. UltraBERT is the first to do so at both scales, indicating that our recipe yields a truly general encoder rather than one specialized for a single task family.
5
Conclusion
We revisit the pre-training recipe of bidirectional encoders at the scale of trillions of tokens. Encoders saturate on downstream tasks at a few trillion tokens, beyond which
the masking ratio determines their specialization: lower ratios favor classification, higher ratios favor retrieval. We reconcile the two by mixing both ratios within a batch with decoupled LM heads, and by applying logit-based distillation only at the low ratio. Built on these findings, UltraBERT achieves state-of-the-art results across classification and retrieval at both 150M and 380M with a 32K context, showing that the specialization of encoders is not inherent but a consequence of the recipe.

Appendix A
Training Setup
A.1
Architecture and Optimization
All encoders share the same architecture design. We use RoPE
(
Su et al., 2024
)
, SwiGLU
(
Shazeer, 2020
)
, and alternating local and global attention similar to
Warner et al. (2025)
, where every third layer applies global attention and the remaining layers apply local attention. The RoPE base is 10K for local attention and 640K for global attention, and the latter is increased to 2.56M during the 32K context extension. The LM head is untied from the input embeddings. We use the same tokenizer of SmolLM2
(
Ben Allal et al., 2025
)
, with a vocabulary of 49,152 tokens.
6
6
6
https://huggingface.co/HuggingFaceTB/cosmo2-tokenizer
We scale the encoders by width while keeping the depth fixed at 24 layers. The hidden size grows from 768 for 150M to 1,024 for 380M and 2,048 for 1.3B, and the intermediate size of SwiGLU is set to 1,024, 3,072, and 5,632, respectively. The 150M model uses 6 attention heads and the two larger models use 8, with a head dimension of 128 for 150M and 380M and 256 for 1.3B.
We use AdamW with
,
,
, a weight decay of 0.1, and gradient clipping at 1.0. The learning rate follows the warmup-stable-decay schedule
(
Hu et al., 2024
)
, with 10B tokens of warmup and a decay phase over the last 10% of the token budget. Note that the learning rate is decayed to zero for every model evaluated in this paper, including the intermediate checkpoints in Section
3.1
, each of which is obtained by branching a decay phase from the stable phase. We use a peak learning rate of 3e-4 for the experiments in Section
3
, and 1e-4 for distillation and the final training of UltraBERT. The batch size is 4M tokens, with 2,048 sequences of 2,048 tokens. For UltraBERT, we train on 2T tokens in total, and extend the context length to 32K for the last 100B tokens
(
Gao et al., 2025
)
.
Each model is trained on a single pod slice of TPU v4-512, v5e-256, or v6e-256, all with 256 chips. We use 16 data-parallel replicas with 16-way fully sharded data parallelism (FSDP), and for the 32K context extension we reduce FSDP to 8-way and add 2-way tensor parallelism.
A.2
Pruning and Distillation
We derive the 380M and 150M encoders by reusing the best 1.3B encoder in Section
3.2
as the teacher, through Cascade Distillation
(
Liu et al., 2026
)
, an iterative process of pruning and distillation: the 1.3B encoder is pruned to 380M and distilled, and the distilled 380M encoder is pruned again to 150M and distilled. We prune only along the width, keeping all 24 layers, following the observation of
Muralidharan et al. (2024)
that width pruning outperforms depth pruning at this scale. The 1.3B encoder is pruned to 380M by reducing the hidden size from 2,048 to 1,024, the head dimension from 256 to 128, and the intermediate size from 5,632 to 3,072, and the 380M encoder is pruned to 150M by reducing the hidden size to 768, the number of attention heads from 8 to 6, and the intermediate size to 1,024.
Following
Muralidharan et al. (2024)
, we estimate the importance of hidden channels, attention heads, and FFN neurons from activations with forward passes only. As a calibration set, we take 2,000 documents from the pre-training mixture and pack them into 1,024 sequences of 1,024 tokens. For hidden channels, we use the output of the pre-attention normalization in each layer; for attention heads, the per-head output entering the output projection; and for FFN neurons, the gated activation entering the down projection. In all three cases, the activation magnitude is averaged over the sequence and then aggregated by the L2 norm over the batch. Hidden channel scores are further aggregated over layers, since the residual stream is shared across the network, whereas head and neuron scores are kept per layer. We keep the top-scoring channels, heads, and neurons.
When the head dimension is halved from 256 to 128, we reduce the query and key projections by keeping every other rotary frequency pair, so that the pairing of RoPE dimensions is preserved, and scale the query by
to keep the attention logits at the same magnitude. For the value and output projections, we apply a shared random orthogonal projection to the output of the value and the input of the output projection, which preserves their product in expectation.
Each pruned encoder is then distilled from the 1.3B teacher. As described in Section
3.5
, we apply the KL divergence between the teacher and student logits on the 5% masked sequences, and the standard MLM loss on the 50% masked sequences. The 150M encoder is distilled from the same 1.3B teacher rather than from the 380M encoder, following
Muralidharan et al. (2024)
and
Liu et al. (2026)
, who found that distilling from the original parent gives better results than distilling from the intermediate child.

We also explored multi-stage training that adjusts the mixture ratios across stages, as in Ettin
(
Weller et al., 2026
)
and EuroBERT
(
Boizard et al., 2025

. This is common practice for decoder LLMs
(
OLMo Team et al., 2024

Ben Allal et al., 2025

We hypothesize that the exposure ratio of each dataset changes the token prediction distribution of the model, and thus its apparent zero-shot and few-shot performance, but has little effect on the intrinsic linguistic knowledge that fine-tuning builds on. We therefore use a single mixture throughout training.
Appendix B
Evaluation Protocols
In this section, we detail the fine-tuning and evaluation protocols used throughout the paper. For the baselines in Table
3
, we take scores from the literature where available, such as GLUE and SQuAD 2.0, and fine-tune and evaluate the rest ourselves under the same protocol.
B.1
Classification Tasks
For every fine-tuning run, we use AdamW with
,
,
, weight decay 0.1, gradient clipping at 1.0, a linear learning rate schedule with 5% warm-up, and bfloat16 mixed precision. Following the common practice of prior encoders
(
Liu et al., 2019
;
Warner et al., 2025
;
Weller et al., 2026
)
, we perform a grid search per task and per model. The learning rate is always swept over
, jointly with the task-specific maximum lengths, batch sizes, and epochs in Table
5
. During each run, the model is evaluated on the development set periodically and the best checkpoint is kept. We then select the best configuration on the development set and report its score; for tasks with a labeled test set (MultiCoNER v2, Few-NERD), the test score of the selected configuration is reported.
GLUE
We evaluate on the 8 GLUE tasks
(
Wang et al., 2018
)
excluding WNLI, as in
Devlin et al. (2019)
, and report development-set scores: Matthews correlation for CoLA, Pearson correlation for STS-B, accuracy on the matched set for MNLI, and accuracy for the remaining tasks. Inputs are truncated to 128 tokens. Following
Liu et al. (2019)
, RTE, MRPC, and STS-B are initialized from the best MNLI checkpoint of the same model instead of the pre-trained weights. The large tasks (MNLI, QQP, QNLI, SST-2) use a batch size of 64 with
epochs. The small tasks (CoLA, STS-B, MRPC, RTE) are known to be unstable
(
Portes et al., 2023
;
Nussbaum et al., 2025
)
, so we sweep the batch size and
epochs with 4 seeds, take the best development score over the grid for each seed, and report the median across the seeds.
SQuAD 2.0
We fine-tune on SQuAD 2.0
(
Rajpurkar et al., 2018
)
with the same protocol as GLUE. Question–context pairs are truncated to 384 tokens with a document stride of 128. We sweep the batch size over
and the number of epochs over
, and report the F1 score on the development set of the best configuration.
Named Entity Recognition
We adopt two fine-grained NER benchmarks, which are known to be more challenging and thus discriminative among models. MultiCoNER v2
(
Fetahu et al., 2023
)
contains short, lower-cased sentences with 33 entity types; we use the English subset (16,778 / 871 / 249,980 sentences for training, development, and test). Since the development set is small, every configuration is run with 3 seeds, and the average development score is used for selection. Few-NERD
(
Ding et al., 2021
)
is used in its supervised setting with 66 entity types and the official split (131,767 / 18,824 / 37,648). To avoid tokenization mismatches with byte-level BPE tokenizers, we prepend a leading space to each word so that the pre-tokenized encoding is identical to that of the raw string. We compute entity-level F1 and report the test scores of the selected configurations: macro-F1 over entity types for MultiCoNER v2 and micro-F1 for Few-NERD.
RewardBench
To examine encoders as reward models, we fine-tune with the Bradley-Terry loss. The training pairs are the union of
HuggingFaceH4/UltraFeedback-binarized
(
Cui et al., 2024
)
and
Skywork/Skywork-Reward-Preference-80K-v0.2
(
Liu et al., 2024
)
, restricted to single-turn conversations. This yields 135,981 pairs, of which 5% (6,800 pairs) are held out as a development set. Prompts and responses are concatenated with a plain template (
USER:
and
ASSISTANT:
) and truncated to 1,024 tokens. Within each run, the epoch checkpoint with the lowest loss on the held-out pairs is kept. We compute the accuracy of the 4 sections (Chat, Chat Hard, Safety, and Reasoning) with the official per-subset weighting, and report their average.

,

B.3

To compare encoders and decoders on LLM-style benchmarks in Section
3.1
, we evaluate 6 multiple-choice question answering (MCQA) benchmarks: ARC
(
Clark et al., 2018
)
, BoolQ
(
Clark et al., 2019
)
, CommonsenseQA
(
Talmor et al., 2019
)
, MMLU
(
Hendrycks et al., 2021a
)
, OpenBookQA
(
Mihaylov et al., 2018
)
, and SIQA
(
Sap et al., 2019
)

We report accuracy, averaged over the 57 subjects for MMLU and over the Easy and Challenge sets for ARC, and the final score is the average over the 6 benchmarks.
Prompting and Scoring
Each question is formatted with the prefix (
Question:
) followed by the options, and the suffix (
Answer:
). For BoolQ and SIQA, which come with contexts, the context is additionally prepended with the prefix
