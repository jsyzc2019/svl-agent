Abstract
When told that robins have sesamoid bones, people are far more prone to extend the property to sparrows than to whales. This behavior, called category-based property induction, is well documented in humans, but whether and how it develops in language models trained only to predict the next token is unclear. To shed light on this, we construct a hierarchical taxonomy of synthetic entities grouped by the properties they share. We then inject facts from this latent taxonomy into the continued pre-training of Pythia and OLMo 2 models from 70M to 7B parameters, and track the development of inductive behaviors. We find that taxonomically aligned induction emerges from next-token prediction alone: a property learned for one entity transfers more strongly within its taxonomic category than across categories. Internally, entities that share a deeper category become more similar in activation space, and clustering recovers the full taxonomic tree from entity representations. Contrary to the usual expectation that scale helps, we find that induction weakens with model size when the data budget is fixed, suggesting that larger models memorize individual facts rather than compress them into shared structure. Induction also strengthens with more pre-training before the taxonomy injection, transfers to novel in-context properties, and can be overridden by a latent taxonomy implied by the prompt. Together, these results show that a model’s inductive generalization reflects the latent organization of its training data, with implications for data curation and for predicting how far a learned fact will spread.

Introduction
Human semantic cognition is organized around categories composed of related entities
(
Rogers and McClelland, 2004
)
. We do not store the properties of every object independently; instead, we infer them from category membership. This lets us predict unobserved features of novel or partially observed entities
(
Rogers and McClelland, 2004
)
. Such an inference is called
category-based property induction
1
1
1
We refer to this as
property induction
or just
induction
in the rest of the paper.
: learning that a novel property holds for one entity, we extend it more strongly to taxonomically close entities than to distant ones. For example, when told that robins have sesamoid bones, we expect sparrows to have them too, but are far less sure about whales
(
Osherson et al., 1990
)
. This graded generalization is not innate, but develops through increasingly systematic induction across human development
(
Keil, 1979
;
Mandler, 1992
)
.
Knowing whether language models (LMs) acquire comparable structure matters for human-AI collaboration and for anticipating LM outputs. In particular, useful communication presupposes shared categories: a chatbot assistant is only reliable if its notion of which things are alike matches its users’. Additionally, inductive generalization of properties is the mechanism by which a fact stated about one entity influences predictions about others, so understanding it is a prerequisite for anticipating what a model will infer, and what it will overgeneralize, from its data. Such understanding is particularly relevant for training data curation and AI safety
(
Anwar et al., 2024
)
.
Prior work on property induction in LMs focuses primarily on the final behavior of pre-trained models
(
Han et al., 2024
;
Rodriguez et al., 2025
)
, but little is known about how such induction develops. Work that studies models during training fine-tunes for yes/no property judgment
(
Misra et al., 2022
)
, which differs from the standard next-token objective under which LMs acquire most of their semantic knowledge during pre-training
(
Zhou et al., 2023
;
Gekhman et al., 2024
)
. Studies that evaluate pre-trained models on real entities cannot control entity-property co-occurrences the models have seen
(
Misra et al., 2022
;
Han et al., 2024
;
Rodriguez et al., 2025
)
, and do not have full control over the ground-truth taxonomy. We close these gaps with a synthetic but realistic setup. Specifically, we construct a controlled taxonomy of novel entities, express it as natural-language text, and inject it into the continued pre-training of real LMs ranging from 70M to 7B parameters alongside their original pre-training corpora. Because every entity and property is a pseudoword, co-occurrence is fully controlled, and leakage is highly unlikely by construction, while the training signal remains ordinary next-token prediction on realistic text.
Using this controlled setup, we make the following findings:
1.
We show that LMs develop taxonomically aligned inductive generalization purely from next-token prediction, supported by a latent taxonomy in the training data (
subsection 3.1
).
2.
This generalization is reflected in the model’s internal representations: entity representations become more similar the deeper their shared category, and the full taxonomy can be recovered from them via clustering, validating
Saxe et al. (2019)
’s theory (
subsection 3.2
).
3.
We find that the strength of acquired induction grows with the number of pre-training steps on taxonomy-unrelated text. Counterintuitively, given the same data budget, increasing model size
reduces
correct induction (
subsection 3.3
).
4.
We show that learning a conflicting taxonomy on top of an old one erases the old grouping, and the new one is learned less well than without the prior commitment (
subsection 3.4
).
5.
We find that distilling only a latent taxonomy into a model is enough to enable in-context inductive generalization over novel data, and when in-context and in-weights taxonomies disagree, the model generalizes according to the context (
subsection 3.5
).

Data mixing.
Each training batch contains 32 sequences of 2,048 (Pythia) or 4,096 (OLMo 2) tokens. For the first 5,000 of the total 10,000 steps of continued pre-training, we inject synthetic facts from our taxonomy into each training batch (
Figure 1
b). In particular, we inject (1) one sequence per depth level in the taxonomic tree, where each sequence contains all entities and properties at that level, and (2) one sequence with the probe and control properties. We randomize the order of sentences with the entity-property facts within these sequences for each batch, so models cannot rely on positional cues. For a balanced taxonomic tree of depth
, this methodology results in training batches with 28 sequences from the background pre-training corpus and 4 sequences with synthetic facts. We ensure that the frequency of all entity-property pairs at a particular level is matched, thus balancing exposure to each part of the latent taxonomic tree. Sequences shorter than the context length are padded with a padding token that we exclude when calculating the loss.
These injections ensure that the synthetic signal is a relatively small share of tokens (
2–4% after padding), as a rare but consistent fact would be in real data. While the resulting frequency is not calibrated to any measured rate in natural corpora, we believe this design is a good initial step toward approximating real-world pre-training while still allowing controlled studies of how data organization shapes generalization. We provide a comparison to training with lower ratios of synthetic data and the original batch size in
subsection D.5
in the Appendix.

3.1
LMs inductively generalize along the latent taxonomy
The next-token objective does not ask a model to build categories explicitly: the taxonomy is only implicit (latent) in which entities share which properties. We therefore begin by asking whether taxonomically aligned induction appears at all under ordinary pre-training, or whether it requires the specialized supervision used in earlier work
(
Misra et al., 2022
)
.
To answer this question, we track the memorization and induction within and across categories over continued pre-training of Pythia 160M and OLMo 2 1B. As
Figure 2
(left) shows, all three quantities rise as the taxonomy is learned, but they separate from each other as training progresses: memorization is highest, induction within category sits well above induction across categories (

e

), and the ordering persists for the rest of training.
The correct generalization along the latent taxonomy is, however, forgotten once the evidence for the taxonomy is removed from the training signal (Appendix,
Figure 11

Chang et al. (2024)
report similar catastrophic forgetting for individual injected facts during pre-training, and our result extends it from the recall of single facts to the taxonomic organization that supports generalizing them.
Ablation: no shared properties.
When we remove the shared properties from training, effectively leaving no signal about which entities belong together in a category, within- and across-category induction collapse onto each other (
Figure 2
, right). This further demonstrates that the correct induction is driven by the model learning the latent taxonomy.

3.2
The taxonomy is recoverable from entity representations
Where does the correct induction come from?
Saxe et al. (2019)
showed that in deep linear networks, hierarchically organized concepts that share deeper categories become more similar in representation. Whether this carries over to Transformer-based LMs is not obvious, since they compose many non-linear operations, use attention rather than fixed weights between fixed units, and are trained on natural text rather than on clean one-hot attribute vectors. We test a stronger case of
Saxe et al. (2019)
’s theory:
Can the entire tree, including its branching structure, be fully reconstructed from the representations?
Using a standard clustering technique, we find that it can.
In particular, we cluster the last-token entity representations from mid-to-late layers of our trained checkpoints (layer 8 out of 12 for Pythia 160M, and layer 8 out of 16 for OLMo 2 1B), following prior work that finds these intermediate layers carry more high-level semantic information than early or final ones
(
Nostalgebraist, 2020
;
Geva et al., 2021
;
Skean et al., 2025
)
. We use
Matching Affinity Clustering
(MAC)
(
Hajiaghayi and Knittel, 2021
)
with cosine distance for this purpose (details in Appendix,
subsection C.6
).
After the data injection phase of our continued pre-training, we find that the recovered tree matches the ground-truth taxonomy perfectly for OLMo 2 1B (
Figure 3
, bottom) and near-perfectly for Pythia 160M (Appendix,
Figure 10
, bottom). The same models before taxonomy injection do not recover it (
Figure 3
and
Figure 10
, top), so the structure is not an artifact of the clustering algorithm, the tokenization, or the geometry of the layer we selected. Additionally,
subsection D.1
in the Appendix shows that the average similarity between entity representations grows with the depth of their shared category, providing further evidence that the representations reflect the taxonomy.
3.3
Development and the effect of scale
The human developmental literature suggests that induction in later learning stages might be more taxonomy-aligned, since children generalize more systematically as their existing category knowledge matures
(
Fisher and Sloutsky, 2005
;
Badger and Shapiro, 2012
)
.
Does the same hold for language models?
Additionally, in terms of model scale, the usual expectation in deep learning is that bigger is better, since loss falls predictably with parameters, data, and compute
(
Kaplan et al., 2020
;
Hoffmann et al., 2022
)
, and downstream capability generally follows
(
Brown et al., 2020
;
Wei et al., 2022
)
. Whether the same scaling trends hold for inductive generalization is an open question.
More mature checkpoints acquire induction more strongly.
We repeat the experiment from
Figure 2
for Pythia 160M checkpoints spanning 0k to 143k pre-training steps, and for OLMo 2 1B checkpoints from 0k to 1.9M steps. Specifically, we vary the base checkpoint from which we continue pre-training, holding the taxonomy, vocabularies, and number of training steps the same. To quantify the strength of inductive generalization with a single number, we average the induction advantage (
Equation 1
) over the last 1,000 continued pre-training steps of the data injection.
As shown in
Figure 4
, the average induction advantage increases with the base checkpoint’s pre-training step. With the exception of a few checkpoints, the overall trend shows that a model that has learned more general language before the taxonomy is introduced learns to generalize over it more strongly. The capacity for correct generalization is, therefore, something that pre-training facilitates.

At a fixed data budget, scale hurts.
Scaling the model has the opposite effect. Across Pythia 70M–2.8B and OLMo 2 1B–7B trained on identical injected data, the induction advantage
decreases

Circles indicate induction advantage for individual evaluation templates, while horizontal lines show the average induction advantage. All reported values are averages from three random seeds.
We hypothesize that this reflects a trade-off between memorization and generalization. Larger models may fit the injected facts fast enough to memorize them directly, which would weaken the pressure to generalize along the taxonomy to minimize loss on the shared properties. A smaller model, in contrast, may be unable to store every entity and property pair separately fast enough, making it cheaper to represent the entities in terms of the categories they share, which in turn would produce correct induction.
If this hypothesis holds, larger models should memorize the injected facts faster, but generalize along the taxonomy more slowly. We test this prediction on the Pythia family, which provides a denser span of model scales. In
Figure 12
in the Appendix, we plot memorization as defined in
subsection 2.3
over training for each model size (left), along with the induction advantage averaged over the first 1,000 continued pre-training steps (right). The ordering of the curves on the left confirms that larger models memorize faster, while the downward trend on the right shows that they inductively generalize along the taxonomy more slowly.
These two findings go in opposite directions and complicate a common assumption: more pre-training improves property induction, but more parameters at a fixed data budget do not. Practitioners injecting new domain knowledge into larger models should therefore expect them to more readily recall rather than inductively generalize, unless the data gives the taxonomy enough support to make generalizing worthwhile.

3.5
In-weights taxonomy shapes in-context induction
All results presented so far concern knowledge the model acquired through training. In real-world deployment, however, information a model is given frequently arrives only in the prompt. Training and in-context learning (ICL) therefore meet in every real interaction, and how they combine determines what a user can actually get a model to do. We pose two research questions: (1)
Does a taxonomy learned in the weights help the model reason about properties it only sees in context?
and (2)
What happens when the prompt and the weights disagree?
Learning a taxonomy enables in-context induction of novel properties.
We train Pythia 1B

Context overrides weights under conflicting taxonomies.
Users may supply categorizations that contradict pre-training: a document seen in pre-training might organize products by supply chain, while a user groups them in the prompt by function. Whether the model follows such a correction or reverts to its learned taxonomy sets a limit on how far it can be steered by context.

One potential implication of this finding is that when provided with a misleading context, a model can be steered in the wrong direction even if the pre-trained categorization was correct. On the other hand, it appears that a user can rewrite a taxonomy at the prompt level when the one from training becomes outdated.

Related work
Property induction in language models.
Misra et al. (2022)
fine-tune LMs to answer yes/no questions about propositions and find that inductive preferences track taxonomic information latent in the data.
Rodriguez et al. (2025)
show that property inferences of instruction-tuned LMs are jointly explained by taxonomic relations and pairwise categorical similarity, and
Han et al. (2024)
find broadly human-like patterns in GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, evaluating purely in-context with premises in the prompt. All three read out induction from a final checkpoint after task-specific supervision or from the prompt alone, and all use real entities. We instead study in-weights induction acquired under the next-token objective alone, track it across pre-training rather than at an endpoint, and use pseudowords for both properties and entities, which removes the taxonomic structure that prior knowledge of the entities would otherwise supply.
Appendix B
reviews the cognitive-science literature that defines this paradigm and motivates some of the experimental choices.
Learning dynamics and representational geometry.
Our developmental analysis is anchored in the connectionist theory of semantic development
(
Rogers and McClelland, 2004
;
Saxe et al., 2019
)
, in which linear networks trained on hierarchical entity-property matrices admit closed-form learning trajectories predicting coarse-to-fine differentiation of representations, mirroring conceptual development in children
(
Keil, 1979
;
Mandler, 1992
)
. We test whether this survives in deep Transformer-based LMs trained on natural text, using open intermediate checkpoints
(
Biderman et al., 2023
)
. This complements work on factual acquisition during pre-training
(
Chang et al., 2024
;
Zucchet et al., 2025
)
, which treats facts atomically rather than as inductively related category members. Relatedly,
Park et al. (2025)
analyze how hierarchical concepts are encoded as linear structure
(
Park et al., 2023
)
, and
Nava and Wyart (2026)
argue this geometry emerges from co-occurrence statistics; both target end-of-pre-training checkpoints on natural concepts, whereas our controlled taxonomy lets us track how such representations develop.

Conclusion
We study whether category-based property induction, a behavior long documented in humans, develops in language models trained only to predict the next token. By injecting a controlled synthetic taxonomy
4
4
4
We discuss the limitations of our setup in
Appendix A
.
of pseudoword entities and properties into the continued pre-training of Pythia and OLMo 2, we show that taxonomically aligned induction emerges from the next-token objective alone, and that it is written into the model’s representations. Specifically, entities grouped under a deeper category move closer together in activation space, and the full taxonomic tree can be recovered from those representations by clustering. We further find that induction strengthens with the amount of pre-training preceding the injection, but weakens with model size at a fixed data budget. Our analysis of learning speed supports one explanation: larger models memorize each fact faster, possibly reducing the pressure to compress entities into the categories they share. Beyond acquisition, we find that a taxonomy in the weights interferes with learning a conflicting one, yet transfers to novel properties supplied in context and can be overridden by a taxonomy implied by the prompt. Together, these results show that a model’s inductive behavior is a property of the latent organization of its training data, that strong recall and strong inductive generalization need not coincide, and that the resulting structure is more easily changed by context than rewritten by further training.

Appendix
Appendix A
Limitations
Our setup diverges from realistic settings in some aspects and leaves a few promising future directions open. We briefly describe each below.
First, the taxonomies we use are small, balanced, and noiseless, whereas real conceptual structures are often large, overlapping, admit exceptions, and depend on which property is being generalized. This is a deliberate trade-off: control over the ground-truth taxonomy and over every entity-property co-occurrence is what makes induction separable from recall, which is close to impossible when evaluating real entity-property pairs with pre-trained LLMs. We expect a larger or noisier taxonomy to weaken the measured effect rather than reverse it, since it depends only on entities sharing properties, and the theory this builds on predicts differentiation by shared depth for any hierarchically structured data
(
Saxe et al., 2019
)
. Verifying this is left to future work.
Second, we inject four synthetic sequences into every training batch for 5,000 steps. While this is a small fraction of the tokens in each batch, appearing in every batch is a far denser exposure than the sparse, irregular recurrence of rare facts in real corpora. We chose this schedule because in preliminary experiments with sparser injection, the models failed to memorize even the entity-property facts they had been trained on, making any measurement of inductive generalization questionable (there is nothing to generalize from). The difficulty of acquiring rarely occurring facts, particularly for smaller models, is well documented
(
Kandpal et al., 2023
;
Chang et al., 2024
;
Huang et al., 2026
)
. At the same time, prior controlled studies of fact learning train from scratch on purely synthetic corpora
(
Allen-Zhu and Li, 2024
;
Zucchet et al., 2025
)
, which removes competition from natural text for model capacity and gradient signal completely. Our setup instead injects into an ongoing pre-training run on the model’s original corpus, so the taxonomy is acquired alongside, and in competition with, real language. In this respect, our setting is a step closer to real pre-training than synthetic-only training.
Third, our scaling and checkpoint results vary one factor at a time. In particular, the finding that induction weakens with model size holds the injected data fixed, whereas in practice model size and data are scaled together
(
Hoffmann et al., 2022
)
. Larger models may require proportionally more exposure before compressing entities into shared categories. Varying the data budget and model size jointly, and locating the exposure at which induction emerges for each size, is a promising avenue for future work.
Appendix B
Category-based induction in cognitive science
The behavior we measure was defined and characterized in cognitive psychology, and that literature informs our definition of what counts as correct inductive generalization.
Rips (1975)
first showed that inductive judgments track similarity rather than logical entailment.
Osherson et al. (1990)
cataloged the robust human phenomena (premise typicality, diversity, similarity, monotonicity, and conclusion specificity) under a similarity-coverage account, and
Sloman (1993)
proposed a feature-based alternative requiring no explicit category representation; see
Heit (2000)
;
Murphy (2002)
for reviews. Later work casts induction as Bayesian inference over structured hypotheses such as taxonomic trees
(
Kemp and Tenenbaum, 2009
;
Kemp and Tenenbaum, 2008
)
. In parallel, the developmental literature established that the taxonomy supporting these inferences is itself learned, differentiating from coarse to fine over years of exposure
(
Keil, 1979
;
Mandler, 1992
;
Pauen, 2002
)
.
Two features of the standard experimental design in human studies are worth stating, because our setup with LMs imitates both. First, the inductively generalized property is a
blank predicate
: a property that participants have no prior knowledge about, chosen so that judgments cannot come from what the reasoner already knows about the property and must instead come from the relation between the entities
(
Osherson et al., 1990
)
. Second, the categories and the underlying taxonomy are
familiar
to the participants.
This second requirement is the constraint under which human studies operate. Because participants arrive with years of prior experience, an experimenter can guarantee no prior exposure to the property, but cannot do the same for the categories, which must be familiar for the task to make sense. The taxonomy participants actually use, therefore, has to be inferred by the experimenter, and their lifetime exposure to it cannot be quantified. Our setting removes this constraint by making both the properties and the entities novel, at the cost of a taxonomy far smaller and more regular than a human conceptual system. A second difference is the read-out: rather than collecting graded verbal judgments, we interpret token probabilities as argument strength.

C.2
Construction of the synthetic taxonomy
Every training sentence associates an entity with one of its properties (
e.g.
,
bisder gruffles deakly
), and the categories themselves are never explicitly stated. The taxonomy is therefore only latent in which entities share which properties. Our setup extends naturally to explicit category labels such as
is an animal
: such a label is formally just another property shared by all entities under a node, so it is covered by the same construction.
The inheritance rule in our taxonomies fixes how much two entities have in common as a function of where they are in the taxonomic tree. With
, every entity inherits six shared properties, two from each of the three levels above it, and any two entities agree on exactly as many of them as the depth of their lowest common ancestor allows: six for siblings (L1, L2, and L3 shared properties), four for entities sharing only an L2 ancestor, and two (the root-level properties) otherwise.
On top of the shared properties, one entity receives the probe property
, and its sibling is the target entity, for which
is held out. Each of the six entities outside the target’s L3 category receives its own control property, giving
. Induction across categories, as measured in
subsection 2.3
, is the probability the model assigns to a control property for the target entity, averaged over all control properties and prompt templates (
Equation 1
).
Each random seed regenerates the entire pseudoword vocabulary, both the entity names and the properties, and reassigns them to positions in the taxonomic tree. Running each experiment across three seeds therefore evaluates property induction with three different vocabularies and three different assignments to the tree, which checks that our results do not depend on any particular set of pseudowords.
C.3
Constraining the completion space
The three quantities evaluated in the main text (memorization, induction within category, and induction across categories) are defined over property completions. However, comparing whole property phrases directly turns out to be unreliable. Each property consists of a verb and an adverb. Rather than sharing nothing between categories, we share the property verb across categories and compare only the probabilities of the token-length-matched adverbs that follow it. For example, instead of comparing the probability of
gruffres branely
in
hodger gruffres branely
against that of
crambles peedly
in
hodger crambles peedly
, we share the root verb
gruffre
between categories and compare the completions
branely
and
peedly
after the common prefix
hodger gruffres
.
This design serves two purposes. First, it constrains the space of plausible continuations, which sharpens the contrast between within-category and across-category induction, because both candidates are grammatically and distributionally supported at the position being scored. Second, it removes residual associations for entity names that resemble real words. For instance,
cokol
, produced by the Wuggy generator, is close to the Czech word for falcon,
sokol
. In our initial experiments, some entity names produced much higher entropy in the next-token distribution, which made results unstable across random seeds. Prefilling the property verb largely resolved this issue.
C.4
Tokenization
Entity names occupy two or three tokens under both tokenizers, and no two entity names share the same last token. Probe and control adverbs are exactly token-length-matched, and no two adverbs in a taxonomy share their first token. The comparison between candidate completions is therefore decided at the first scored position rather than accumulating over a shared prefix, and no candidate is favored by being shorter: the induction advantage in
Equation 1
compares sums of log-probabilities over completions of equal length.
C.5
Training and evaluation templates
Using a single fixed phrasing would let a model solve the task by matching one surface pattern, so we vary the sentence template while holding the underlying statements constant. Each training sentence is drawn uniformly from the pool of 30 templates in Box
C.5
. The pool is split into three groups that differ in how the property is realized. Direct present-tense frames take the property as a finite verb phrase, as in
hodger gruffres branely
. Indirect frames embed the property under an infinitive or raising construction, so the property appears in its bare form, as in
pirer tends to gruffre branely
. Past-tense frames take the property in the past, as in
colel gruffred branely
. We create these inflected forms (
e.g.
,
gruffres
,
gruffre
, and
gruffred
) manually from the Wuggy output.
At evaluation, we score property completions against the 12 templates in Box
C.5
. Of these, 2 also appear in the training pool and the remaining 10 are never seen during training. This held-out majority rules out the most immediate alternative explanation for our results, namely that the model has learned to complete a specific surface pattern rather than to project a property onto an entity. The evaluation templates follow the same three groups as the training pool, so the evaluation covers the same range of constructions the model was trained on, and averaging over them prevents any single phrasing from driving the measurement. Templates ending in an infinitival marker or modal auxiliary, such as
often tends to
or
will
, require the bare property form, and the completion is scored accordingly.
C.6
Matching Affinity Clustering
Matching Affinity Clustering (MAC)
(
Hajiaghayi and Knittel, 2021
)
starts with all entities as singleton clusters. In each round, it partitions the current clusters into disjoint pairs by a minimum-weight perfect matching on the complete graph whose edge weights are the average-linkage cosine distances between clusters, then merges every matched pair.
Because each round matches every cluster exactly once, the number of clusters halves per round and the procedure yields a balanced binary tree in
rounds (for
a power of two), matching the shape of our ground-truth taxonomy. In contrast, standard agglomerative clustering performs
sequential merges, each joining the closest available pair, and generally produces unbalanced trees, which is why we do not use it in this work.

D.3
Forgetting after injection stops
Figure 11:
over the last 6,000 continued pre-training steps of Pythia 160M (left) and OLMo 2 1B (right), after synthetic data injection stops at step 5,000. Shaded regions indicate the standard error of the mean across three random seeds.
Correct induction grows over continued pre-training, so more exposure to the taxonomy yields stronger taxonomic generalization rather than only more memorization (
Figure 2
). This generalization does not, however, persist on its own. When we stop injecting the synthetic data at step 5,000 and continue training on the background corpus alone, both memorization and correct induction decay within a few hundred steps (
Figure 11
). Taxonomic structure is therefore maintained only while the data continues to support it.
Chang et al. (2024)
report comparable forgetting for individual injected facts during pre-training; our result shows that the taxonomic organization supporting their generalization is no more durable than the facts themselves.
D.4
Memorization speed versus induction across model scale

D.5
The effect of batch size
Figure 13:
Comparison for Pythia 160M between the batch size used in all our experiments (32 sequences; 65k tokens) and the batch size of the original Pythia training run
(
Biderman et al., 2023
)
(1,024 sequences; 2M tokens). Shaded regions indicate the standard error of the mean across three random seeds.
All experiments in the paper use a batch size of 32 sequences, or roughly 65k tokens for Pythia and 130k tokens for OLMo 2. This is much smaller than the 1,024 sequences (2M tokens) and 512 sequences (2M tokens) used in the original Pythia and OLMo 2 runs
(
Biderman et al., 2023
;
OLMo et al., 2024
)
. We chose the smaller batch size because of the available compute budget: continued pre-training has to be repeated across model sizes, checkpoints, seeds, and ablations, and the full batch size would have made that grid unaffordable. If our results held only at a batch size chosen for convenience, however, they would say more about our training setup than about LMs. We therefore repeat continued pre-training of Pythia 160M at the original batch size of 1,024 sequences, holding everything else fixed, including the taxonomy, the number of injected sequences per batch, the evaluation templates, and the seeds.
As
Figure 13
shows, the ordering of memorization above within-category induction above across-category induction is preserved at the larger batch size, so the qualitative finding does not depend on our choice. The differences are in the overall magnitude of all three quantities and in the speed at which correct induction is acquired: larger batches lead to slower differentiation between within- and across-category induction and to lower probabilities assigned to the synthetic properties. This follows from the ratio of synthetic entity-property data to the background corpus falling to
0.06–0.13% after padding, which lowers the influence of the synthetic sequences on each training step.
What matters for the rest of the paper is which parts of the result are robust to this choice. The ordering of the three curves is the claim that our paper relies on, and it is the part that needs to be independent of batch size. Absolute values of the induction advantage are more fragile, which is why we compare them only within a fixed batch size and never across the two settings.
