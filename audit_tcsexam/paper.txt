                                                 Can large language models (LLMs) advance theoretical computer science and re-
                                                 solve its long-standing open conjectures? To study progress toward this goal, we
                                                 introduce TCSExam, a unified framework and benchmark covering the full range
                                                 of TCS research. It evaluates theorem proving, solver engineering, and construc-
                                                 tions alongside direct attempts to resolve open conjectures, capturing both con-
                                                 crete research accomplishments and progress toward long-standing research goals.
                                                 TCSExam-Full contains 100 expert-reviewed tasks, with submissions checked by
                                                 the Lean kernel or an executable task checker. TCSExam-Lite selects 20 tasks,
                                                 five per track, reducing the number of task attempts by 80% at the same per-
                                                 attempt budget. We evaluate eight agents on Full and nine on Lite. The TCSExam
                                                 Index combines all four tracks: GPT-6 Astra leads Full (71.9), and Opus 5.5 leads
                                                 Lite (72.3). Full and Lite rankings are strongly aligned across eight shared agents
                                                 (Spearman’s ρ = 0.93), supporting Lite as a compact, discriminative benchmark.
                                                 The evaluations yield improved solver outputs and mathematical constructions, to-
                                                 gether with independently verified special cases and bounds from open-conjecture
                                                 attempts. TCSExam provides an extensible framework with task sources, Docker
                                                 build recipes, and evaluation tools for reproducible use.


                                        1    I NTRODUCTION
                                        Can large language models (LLMs) resolve long-standing open conjectures and advance theoretical
                                        computer science? Studying progress toward this ambition poses a challenge for evaluation: when a
                                        conjecture remains unresolved, the final outcome alone reveals little about which research tasks an
                                        LLM can perform or where its limitations lie. An informative evaluation should therefore examine
                                        concrete research accomplishments alongside direct attempts to prove or refute open conjectures.
                                        Three forms of research output provide concrete ways to examine these questions. Theorem prov-
                                        ing requires complete arguments for research-level mathematical statements. Solver engineering
                                        produces reusable programs whose effectiveness is tested across problem instances. Constructions
                                        provide explicit mathematical objects with verified properties that establish or improve bounds. Each
                                        output has independent research value, and the methods used to obtain them can interact: solvers
                                        search for candidate objects, proofs establish their properties, and constructions can supply wit-
                                        nesses or counterexamples. Evaluating these outputs helps TCS researchers judge which research
                                        work LLMs can undertake and helps model developers identify priorities for improvement.
                                        Existing benchmarks address selected aspects of TCS research (Table 1). We introduce TCSExam,
                                        a unified framework and benchmark covering the full range of TCS research (Figure 1). Its four
                                        tracks evaluate theorem proving, solver engineering, constructions, and open-conjecture settlement,
                                        with success criteria defined by the requested outputs. Research areas can span multiple tracks, and
                                        shared task interfaces and verification protocols support the incorporation of additional problems.
                                        TCSExam comprises 100 tasks: 20 research theorems to prove in Lean 4 (de Moura & Ullrich,
                                        2021), 30 solver-engineering problems evaluated over instance families, 20 construction problems
                                        scored against documented records, and 30 open conjectures in prove-or-refute form. Authors with
                                        expertise in the relevant TCS areas reviewed all 100 tasks for research significance. Every submis-
                                        sion is checked by the Lean kernel or an executable task checker. This combines expert judgment

We evaluate eight agents on Full and nine on Lite. On 20 research theorems without supplied in-
termediate lemmas, GPT-6 Astra achieves a per-attempt complete-proof success rate of 70.0%, fol-
lowed by Kimi K3.1 Pro at 38.3%; the other six agents achieve at most 11.7%. Solver performance
varies across instance families, with submitted programs improving on public competition circuits.
DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 0910 produces a verified construction that exceeds a task reference despite
completing none of its 60 theorem-proving attempts. Individual attempts also yield independently
verified special cases and bounds (Section 5.6).
Lite extends the comparison to Opus 5.5, which solves four of its five theorem tasks, compared with
three for Astra. Full and Lite rankings agree on 26 of 28 pairs among the eight shared agents, while
track-level comparisons reveal differences in their research capabilities (Section 5.3).
Our contributions are as follows.

 • A framework spanning the full range of TCS research. Four tracks evaluate research outputs
   and open-conjecture settlement through shared task interfaces, distinct success criteria, and a
   common protocol for incorporating additional problems.
 • An expert-reviewed benchmark with verifiable outcomes. We curate 100 research tasks and a
   20-task Lite subset with track-specific scoring and verification by the Lean kernel or executable
   task checkers.
 • A systematic evaluation of nine agents across Full and Lite. We characterize performance
   across the four tracks and examine submitted proofs, programs, and constructions to identify
   research accomplishments and remaining limitations.

2    R ELATED W ORK
Theorem proving in mathematics and TCS. TCS-BENCH (Cohen-Addad et al., 2026) evaluates
research-level natural-language proofs using an LLM verifier. Despite reported accuracy above 90%
against expert judgments, acceptance remains subject to verifier errors and provides no formal cor-
rectness guarantee. FormalTCS (Wang et al., 2026) evaluates end-to-end formalization and proving
of TCS results. Their benchmark scope does not include reusable solver performance or construc-
tion quality. Formal evaluation also spans competition mathematics, undergraduate mathematics,
and repository-context proofs (Zheng et al., 2021; Tsoukalas et al., 2024; Azerbayev et al., 2023;
Yang et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2024). CombiBench provides formal combinatorial problems in Lean
4 (Liu et al., 2025), while LeanGeo-Bench evaluates competition geometry in the LeanGeo frame-
work (Song et al., 2025). Kimina-Prover uses reinforcement learning to train structured reasoning
for Lean 4 proof generation (Wang et al., 2025). Proof guidance and decomposition are studied
by Draft, Sketch, and Prove, DeepSeek-Prover-V2, and Seed-Prover (Jiang et al., 2022; Ren et al.,
2025; Chen et al., 2025).
Solver programs and explicit constructions. AlphaTensor and AlphaDev demonstrate algorithm
discovery through reinforcement learning (Fawzi et al., 2022; Mankowitz et al., 2023); FunSearch
and AlphaEvolve use program search for mathematical and computational improvements (Romera-
Paredes et al., 2024; Novikov et al., 2025). FrontierCS evaluates executable programs on open-ended
algorithmic and research tasks (Mang et al., 2026b; Frontier-CS Team, 2026). FrontierCS 2 com-
pares agents’ reimplementations of research contributions with the original code across 48 public
tasks (Mang et al., 2026a). EdgeBench includes formal proving and combinatorial optimization
in long-interaction evaluation (Zhu et al., 2026). TCSExam separately evaluates reusable solvers
across instance families and the parameters attained by explicit constructions.
Open conjectures and research-level evaluation. FrontierMath evaluates advanced mathematical
reasoning through automatically checkable answers (Glazer et al., 2024). Formal Conjectures sup-
plies formalized research problems and frozen evaluations of verified discovery (Firsching et al.,
2026); the proving-ground repository reports complete solutions and verified reductions on its open
tier (phyter1, 2026). TCSExam evaluates complete proof or refutation of open TCS conjectures,
retaining intermediate outputs for separate analysis.
Positioning of TCSExam. Research-agent benchmarks also evaluate ML engineering and research
replication (Chan et al., 2024; Wijk et al., 2024; Starace et al., 2025). Table 1 compares the closest
TCS benchmarks. TCSExam brings four forms of TCS research output into one extensible frame-
work, with distinct success criteria and common procedures for task development, verification, and
version control.

3    TCSE XAM
TCSExam organizes tasks across TCS areas by their required outputs and success criteria into the
four tracks in Figure 1. Search can produce candidate objects, proofs establish their properties, and
both can contribute to resolving open conjectures. Appendix A lists the 100 tasks and their sources.

3.1   R ESEARCH SCOPE AND TASK INTERFACES

Theorem proving (20 tasks). Given a formal statement derived from a research theorem, the model
submits a Lean 4 proof. We vary the supplied context through three settings with a common target:
bare provides the main statement, fixed definitions, and mathematical assumptions; ladder addition-
ally provides intermediate lemma statements; guided further supplies proof ideas. This design is
related to TCS-BENCH’s use of intermediate-result masking to vary proof-task difficulty (Cohen-
Addad et al., 2026). Our main evaluation uses bare to measure complete-proof success. Ladder and
guided support a separate analysis of proof guidance, with partial checkpoint credit and independent
complete-proof checks (Section 3.3).
Solver engineering (30 tasks). The model receives a problem specification, a naive baseline, and
public instances, then submits a reusable solver program. Final judging evaluates the program across
the task’s designated instance set under task-specific resource limits. Checkers validate solutions or
certificates and recompute objective values.
Constructions (20 tasks). The model submits an explicit mathematical object and a certificate of its
required properties. Examples include codes, geometric configurations, and graph colorings. Tasks
use Lean 4 formalizations and executable checks to verify the object and its achieved parameter.
Documented construction records provide reference values against which progress is measured; the
model may use mathematical reasoning, search code, or both to produce its submission.
Open conjectures (30 tasks). The model receives a formalized conjecture and two Lean proof slots,
conj and refuted, for the statement and its negation. A submission must prove either side in
full. A counterexample must come with a proof that it refutes the stated conjecture. This interface
accepts either direction of a resolution.

3.2   TASK CURATION AND DEVELOPMENT

We select tasks for their research significance, coverage across TCS areas, and verifiable out-
comes. Theorem tasks come from recent TCS papers (2023–2026) and span 13 subfields. Solver
tasks draw on established competitions and instance libraries. Task-specific safeguards, including
withholding hidden instances from the agent during development and applying structure-preserving
transformations, mitigate direct reuse of stored answers. Construction tasks target open problems
with documented records, while the open-conjecture track includes problems such as log-rank and
Ryser. Expert review assesses the significance and representativeness of the selected problems.
Figure 2 summarizes the task development process. An authoring agent prepares the statement,
submission interface, scorer, and environment. A separate review agent checks correspondence
to the research source and the behavior of the verification procedure. Tests and pilot evaluations
identify defects and guide revision. Across development rounds, over 40 theorem problems were
retired after being solved in pilot evaluations. Tasks are included after expert approval.

3.3   T RACK - SPECIFIC SCORING

Scores reflect the submitted output and the task’s notion of success. Complete proof obligations use
binary scores; optimization and construction tasks provide graded credit. The score definitions and
integrity checks are specified per track (Appendix B). Reported scores use percentages rounded to
one decimal; formulas use the original score units.
Theorem proving. A valid proof of the complete bare-level statement scores 100.0%; an incom-
plete proof scores 0.0%. Guided and ladder tasks receive partial credit for the fraction of check-
points passed; a verified complete proof receives full credit. Each checkpoint is checked in isola-
tion with the other checkpoint statements supplied as assumptions; this also applies to the main-
theorem checkpoint. Coverage therefore measures conditional proof obligations. Complete proofs
are checked separately without unproved checkpoint assumptions (Appendix B.1).
Solver engineering. Optimization tasks measure the fraction of the difference between a naive
baseline B and a task reference K recovered by the submitted solver. These scores are not clipped:
values below 0.0% indicate performance worse than the baseline, and values above 100.0% indi-
cate improvement over the reference. Decision tasks use solve rates. A separate Kimi K3-relative

Every selected task retains its original scoring function, and the five task means receive equal weight

3.5   TASK AND RESULT VALIDATION

Before a task enters the benchmark, task validation checks whether the encoded problem matches
its research source. After submission, result validation checks outputs against the task rules, with
additional review for candidate advances. Appendix C documents these checks and supporting
evidence.
Formal statement fidelity. Agent-assisted reviews compare definitions, assumptions, quantifiers,
and parameter ranges in the Lean statement with the source. Small examples and boundary cases
test for degenerate or unintended interpretations, and re-translation provides an additional cross-
check. Shared definitions and statements are checked across disclosure levels. Successful proofs
and unexpected outcomes prompt renewed scrutiny of the statement and its assumptions. These
checks also flagged potential inconsistencies in source statements or proofs; candidate tasks with
unresolved concerns were excluded.
Result validation. Every submission is checked by the Lean kernel or an executable task checker.
Candidate advances additionally undergo agent-assisted audits of the rollouts and submitted arti-
facts. These audits check compliance with task rules, proof dependencies or object validity, and
the conditions of the claimed improvement. For solver results, this includes comparing instance
sets and execution budgets. Source checks distinguish improvements over task references from new
literature records; claims of new records require evidence for the prior best value and a matching
problem setting.
Task versions and extensions. Each reported result is associated with the task statement, envi-
ronment, verifier, and reference values used in evaluation. Corrections and additional problems
enter new task versions, with changes and affected results recorded. Earlier versions and evaluation
records are retained so that results remain interpretable as the benchmark expands.

5.1     OVERALL PERFORMANCE

Table 2 summarizes performance across the four tracks. Figure 3 shows task-level results for theo-
rem proving, solver engineering, and constructions.
Research proofs distinguish the evaluated agents. GPT-6 Astra completes 14 of 20 theorems
(70.0%) without supplied intermediate lemmas. Kimi K3.1 Pro succeeds in 23 of 60 attempts
(38.3%), solving nine distinct theorems. It completes all three attempts on the PCP alphabet-
soundness task, which Astra does not solve. Kimi K3 succeeds in 7 of 60 attempts (11.7%), cover-
ing three theorems. GLM-5.3, Qwen3.8 Max (0902), and Grok 4.6 each succeed in 1 of 20 attempts
(5.0%); DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 0910 succeeds in none of 60, while DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 suc-
ceeds in 4 of 60 attempts (6.7%) across three theorems. Across the eight agents, four theorems
remain unproved.
Construction means reflect reference attainment across tasks. Astra has the highest mean at
104.9%, matching the reference on 17 tasks and exceeding it on three. The other agents range
from 86.2% to 98.6%, with Kimi K3.1 Pro second. GLM-5.3 averages 97.7% without exceeding
any reference, illustrating that broad reference attainment and individual improvements are distinct
aspects of performance. Section 5.5 examines the verified improvements on specific tasks.

Performance differs across problem families. On each instance, a relative score of 50.0% denotes

Pro at 54.1% and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 at 53.1%. The other five agents range from 43.8% to

(Figure 3b). It trails GLM-5.3 on hitting set, Kimi K3 on dominating set, and GLM-5.3, Qwen3.8

Appendix E.2 gives detailed results.
Relative scoring limits baseline–reference scaling effects. The original score (B − A)/(B −
K) compares objective A with baseline B and reference K. A large |B − K| compresses score
differences near K; a small denominator can amplify individual contributions. We introduce a Kimi
K3-relative metric that compares raw outcomes with repeated Kimi K3 results, retains sensitivity
near their mean, and bounds contributions to [0, 100] on the reported scale. For example, Qwen3.8
Max (0902)'s valid IWLS 2023 circuit for ex37 uses 273,020 AND gates, versus baseline 698 and
reference 106. The ratio (698 − 273,020)/(698 − 106) ≈ −460 allows this single oversized circuit
to depress the original mean. Qwen3.8 Max (0902) averages 64.7% on that scale and 49.2% on the
bounded relative scale. Appendix B.2 defines the metric; Appendix E.2 gives raw outcomes.

More theorems are solved with additional statements and proof ideas. With three attempts on

5.5      V ERIFIED CONSTRUCTION IMPROVEMENTS

Graph and code results. In the Erdős girth-5 extremal-graph task, Kimi K3 constructs a 124-vertex,
630-edge graph without 3- or 4-cycles, exceeding the 629-edge reference (Goedgebeur et al., 2025).
In the quantum stabilizer-code task, it constructs a binary [[25, 7, 6]] code, increasing the minimum
distance from 5 to 6 for a code on 25 qubits encoding seven logical qubits (Grassl, n.d.) (Fig-
ure 5). Astra also attains both parameters and proves that a binary code of length 22 and minimum
Hamming distance 10 has at most 82 codewords (A(22, 10) ≤ 82), improving the published upper
bound of 84 (Gijswijt et al., 2012) (Appendix D.3). Qwen3.8 Max (0902), Grok 4.6, and DeepSeek
V4.1 Flash 0910 each obtain a 630-edge graph. Appendix F records provenance and shows Astra’s
constructions.

Verified special cases and bounds. While attempting the full conjecture, GPT-6 Astra proves the
Alon–Jaeger–Tarsi conjecture over F5 for every n ≤ 9: every invertible n×n matrix A admits a vec-
tor x such that x and Ax have no zero coordinates. The n = 9 case extends the dimensional ranges
n ≤ 7 of Yu (1999) and n ≤ 8 reported in a recent preprint (Zeng et al., 2026). The proof combines
counting identities with an exact integer certificate to exclude a minimal counterexample (Figure 6).
Independent Lean verification confirms the result using only standard axioms. Appendix F.3 gives
the proof structure, formal verification, and its relation to prior work. Four further verified cases
concern a six-variable Fourier entropy–influence bound, an exact high-rank Hilbert-matrix rigidity
formula, an influence bound for quadratic polynomials, and finite-instance online-matching bounds
(Appendix F.4). These five cases demonstrate that open-conjecture attempts can produce indepen-
dently verifiable mathematical results. Analyzing their statements and proofs captures progress
beyond the binary resolution score and identifies concrete results for further mathematical study.

6   C ONCLUSION
TCSExam provides a unified, extensible framework for evaluating agents across the full range of
TCS research, connecting theorem proving, solver engineering, and explicit constructions with
open-conjecture settlement. Full comprises 100 expert-reviewed tasks with outcomes checked by
the Lean kernel or executable checkers. Lite retains all four tracks in 20 tasks, reducing the number
of task attempts by 80% at the same per-attempt budget. Evaluations across nine agents reveal sub-
stantial differences in research capabilities, with strong Full–Lite rank agreement among the eight
shared agents. Together, the suites support broad capability assessment and smaller-scale evaluation.
The results show complementary strengths in proof completion, solver performance, and construc-
tion quality. Submitted programs and mathematical objects improve selected task references. For
both agents in the proof-guidance study, supplying intermediate lemmas and proof ideas increases
the number of theorems solved. Open-conjecture attempts also yield independently verified spe-
cial cases and bounds, including Astra’s nine-dimensional Alon–Jaeger–Tarsi result over F5 . These
outputs help characterize the research work agents can undertake and identify directions for further
progress. The accompanying task sources, Docker build recipes, and evaluation tools support re-
producible use and extension. Researchers can add problems through the shared task interfaces and
verification protocols, extending this foundation for evaluating agents and studying their contribu-
tions to TCS research.

A.5      R EADING THE FOUR TASK INTERFACES

The following examples explain what an agent submits and how it is evaluated in each track. The
versioned task packages provide the full statements, input formats, and checker configurations.
Theorem proving: corners-quasipoly. The task concerns the quantitative bound for corner-free
sets of Jaber et al. (2025) and provides formal definitions and a fixed target theorem. Bare attempts
must prove the target from the supplied mathematical context. Ladder and guided attempts addition-
ally receive intermediate statements; guided attempts receive a proof idea. A local checkpoint proof
is evaluated under the other checkpoint statements, whereas the complete-proof check excludes un-
proved checkpoint assumptions. The fixed target declaration and supplied assumptions determine
the statement whose complete proof is scored.


                                                         23
Solver engineering: maxcut-mqlib. A submitted program receives a weighted graph from the
MQLib collection (Dunning et al., 2018) and returns a vertex partition. The checker validates the
partition and recomputes the total weight of edges crossing the cut. Final judging applies the same
program across the designated instance family under the task’s resource limits. The evaluation
measures the program's performance across the instance family. The final manifest specifies public
and held-out instance counts and the limits used for each set.
Constructions: capset-d7. A cap set in (Z/3Z)7 contains no three distinct points whose sum
is zero. The submission supplies a set of points and the required certificate. Verification checks
membership, distinctness, and the cap-set property, then recomputes cardinality. The task scores the
verified cardinality using its construction-scoring function.
Open conjectures: log-rank-conjecture. The task asks for a proof or refutation of a formal re-
lationship between deterministic communication complexity and the rank of the communication
matrix. Two slots hold proofs of P and ¬P . Each is checked without access to the unproved oppo-
site slot. Complete proof or refutation earns the resolution score; verified special cases are retained
as auxiliary mathematical results.

The task version fixes the submission interface, legal operations, checker, reference values, and
resource limits. Official scores require both mathematical validity and compliance with the sub-
mission rules. Separately verified mathematical outputs are reported with their originating task and
verification conditions.

B.4       O PEN CONJECTURES : FULL PROOF OR REFUTATION

A task supplies a formal proposition P and slots for P and ¬P . A valid proof in either direction
earns 1; otherwise the complete-resolution score is 0. Each main slot is checked in isolation after
removing its contradictory twin, without treating either unproved main statement as an assumption.
Auxiliary statements may support the submitted proof only within the permitted verification context.
Special cases, reductions, and counterexamples to related stronger statements are reported with their
precise scope and do not change the binary target score.

C     TASK D EVELOPMENT AND F IDELITY AUDITS
C.1     F ROM A RESEARCH SOURCE TO A TASK

Agent-assisted source reviews identify the intended research claim, its quantifiers and parameter
range, and the result or object accepted by the task. For formal statements, component reconciliation
maps each source definition and assumption to the corresponding Lean declaration. Small examples
and boundary cases check that definitions behave as intended. Re-translation provides an additional
comparison between the source and its formalization. Some re-translation passes were performed
by the authoring agent, while other reviews used a fresh context. Shared definitions and statements
are checked across the three theorem-disclosure levels.
Checker review exercises both valid and invalid submissions: empty outputs, malformed objects, in-
correct objectives, incomplete certificates, and attempts to change fixed statements. Reference out-
puts are used where available. For open conjectures, development tests check the proof-or-refutation
interface and its rejection of invalid submissions.

could not be resolved. This source-reconciliation process informed task selection and formalization.

D.2       S OLVER TASK CONSTRUCTION AND EXECUTION

Sources and evaluation sets. The solver collection comprises 19 competition-derived tasks and
11 tasks based on instance libraries. Source type and execution budget are separate choices: either
type can use per-instance limits or a shared time pool. Each task specifies a public development
set, a final evaluation set, an executable submission interface, and a checker. Competition-derived
tasks can retain the source’s development/test distinction or define a new split of released instances.
Library-derived tasks define their own splits; these are fixed in the task version rather than selected
from model outcomes. Table 10 records the evaluated configurations.
Public instances and self-test results are available during program development. Hidden instance
files and transformation parameters remain on the judging side, outside the agent’s development
environment. Public development and final evaluation use disjoint sets of source instances.

Adapting an instance library. The quadratic-assignment task illustrates the conversion of a pub-
lic library into a solver evaluation. From 136 QAPLIB instances (Burkard et al., 1997), the versioned



                                                  27
split assigns 41 to hidden evaluation, 75 to public development, and 20 to neither set. Hidden se-
lection is stratified by instance family and size; withholding the third group prevents the public
complement from identifying the evaluation set exactly. Hidden instances independently permute
facility and location indices. If π relabels facilities and σ relabels locations, a feasible assignment p
becomes p′ = σ ◦p◦π −1 . Applying the same relabelings to the flow and distance matrices preserves
every assignment’s cost and the optimal value. The checker validates the submitted permutation and
recomputes its cost on the transformed instance.
Other transformations follow the problem’s structure, such as vertex relabeling for graph problems
and item reordering for bin packing. These changes obstruct direct reuse of answers in the original
indexing. Training exposure to the public source instances is unknown. The submission rules require
computation during judging and prohibit replay of stored final answers; hidden access controls,
transformations, and rollout audits support compliance at complementary stages.

Development and execution budgets. The 12-hour budget covers model interaction and program
development. Afterward, the frozen program is rebuilt and evaluated under the task’s separate execu-
tion limits. Twenty-four tasks impose limits independently on each instance. Six tasks instead share
one total time pool: bin packing, graph partitioning, sports timetabling, logic synthesis, quadratic
assignment, and set cover. The judge processes the evaluation family while tracking the remaining
pool, which is supplied to the program on each invocation. Time saved on one instance is available
to later instances; these tasks are evaluated as one group to preserve this allocation rule. For exam-
ple, quadratic assignment has 1,230 seconds across its 41 final instances, with no separate 30-second
cutoff per instance. Glass cutting uses two runs per instance, at 180 and 3,600 seconds, with weights
0.1 and 0.9. Table 10 specifies each task’s final execution time budget.

Parallel evaluation. For per-instance evaluation, we use up to ten isolated sandboxes concurrently,
using ten whenever the number of instances and available resources permit. Each instance retains
the specified time, CPU, and memory limits. Shared-pool tasks remain in one evaluation group to
preserve cross-instance time allocation.

F.3     A VERIFIED NINE - DIMENSIONAL A LON –JAEGER –TARSI RESULT

Statement and relation to prior work. The Alon–Jaeger–Tarsi conjecture asks whether every
invertible matrix over a field with at least four elements admits a vector whose coordinates and
image coordinates are all nonzero. Astra’s Lean proof establishes
      ∀p ≥ 5 prime, ∀n ≤ 9, ∀A ∈ GLn (Fp ), ∃x ∈ (Fp \ {0})n : Ax ∈ (Fp \ {0})n . (9)
The contribution relative to the literature reviewed here is the case p = 5, n = 9. Yu’s dimension
guarantee n < 2p−2 gives n ≤ 7 over F5 (Yu, 1999); this guarantee is also stated in the introduction
of Nagy & Pach (2025). Version 1 of Zeng et al. (2026), posted August 18, 2026, reports n ≤ 2p−2 ,
including n = 8 over F5 . The large-field result of Nagy & Pach (2025) applies when 61 < |F| ̸= 79
and does not cover F5 . The nine-dimensional result goes beyond these ranges. The comparison
uses the publicly available versions checked on September 26, 2026. For p ≥ 7, the dimensions in
Equation 9 already fall within Yu’s guarantee.

From a minimal counterexample to counting constraints. The proof first establishes smaller
dimensions and then assumes a minimal counterexample A of dimension nine over F5 . Let wt(x)
denote the number of nonzero coordinates of x. Define
      Wij = {x : wt(x) = i, wt(Ax) = j} ,          Vij = {x : wt(x) = j, wt(AT x) = i} .
These two 10 × 10 arrays have nonnegative integer entries. Invertibility fixes their row and col-
umn sums; a finite Fourier identity relates the two arrays. Minimality supplies further vanishing
conditions, including W22 = W23 = W32 = 0.
Additional inequalities come from pairs of columns u, v. Each coordinate pair (ui , vi ) belongs to
one of seven classes: both zero, only the first nonzero, only the second nonzero, or one of the four
nonzero cancellation ratios ui + tvi = 0. Figure6(a) displays all 25 coordinate pairs. Their seven
class counts h0 , . . . , h6 sum to nine, leaving 15
                                                  6 = 5,005 possible count vectors. The submitted
proof checks the relevant inequalities over these finite count vectors and sums them across column
pairs. Applying the argument to A, A−1 , AT , and (AT )−1 yields four nonnegative linear quantities
L1 , . . . , L4 in W and V . These finite checks concern coordinate counts, not enumeration of all
invertible nine-dimensional matrices.

The submitted contradiction certificate. Collect the 200 entries of W, V and the four quantities
Lo in a vector z ≥ 0. The identities above give an integer linear system Ez = b. The submitted
certificate contains integer multipliers λ satisfying

Consequently, 0 ≤ λT Ez = λT b < 0, a contradiction. Of the 197 equations in the source cer-

F.6   E VIDENCE FOR REFERENCE IMPROVEMENTS AND NOVELTY CLAIMS

Artifact validity and novelty are recorded separately. The archive retains the original output, its
checksum, task version, verifier and toolchain, verification log, recomputed value or accepted state-
ment, and evaluation resources. A record comparison additionally includes the original source, date
and location, prior value, parameter restrictions, and applicable resource conditions. A floating-point
difference within the declared tolerance is not counted as an improvement.
Case studies identify their comparison explicitly: a task reference, a public competition result, or a
published mathematical bound. Claims of new records include a source comparison matching the
problem parameters and applicable resource conditions. A claimed resolution of an open conjecture
additionally undergoes renewed checking of the formal statement against the source conjecture.

G     E XTENSION AND M AINTENANCE
G.1   A DDING A TASK

An extension specifies the research question and source, submission type, accepted evidence, scor-
ing rule, and execution resources. It then follows the development checks in Appendix C: source
correspondence, executable validation, negative tests, trial evaluation, and expert review. The task
manifest records its field and parameter coverage so that extensions can increase both breadth and
depth without changing the meaning of existing scores.
New problems use the corresponding output interface: a formal proof, a reusable solver, an explicit
object with verified properties, or a proof or refutation of a conjecture. A task combining several
methods still specifies exactly what must be submitted and what the checker establishes. This per-
mits research workflows to vary while the evaluated output remains explicit.

G.2   V ERSIONING AND CORRECTIONS

Statements, environments, scorers, instance sets, and references remain fixed within an evaluation
version. Corrections and additional tasks receive new versions with dated explanations. Reports
identify which results are affected and whether re-evaluation is necessary. Source corrections can
update a reference value in a later release with provenance; a model improvement is retained as an
outcome rather than silently changing the comparison used for that evaluation.
Retired tasks remain available with their historical version and retirement reason. Comparisons
across releases report the common task set or explicitly identify the changed collection. A main-
tained benchmark can thereby grow without rewriting earlier results.

G.3   R ECORDING RESEARCH OUTPUTS

The project maintains an artifact index linking results to the statements or objects checked, their
originating task versions, and verification records. Research status distinguishes reproduction, vali-
dated improvement relative to a specified reference, and a novelty claim supported by source review.
Verified special cases and reductions can be indexed alongside the complete-resolution score, pre-
serving their mathematical content without changing that score retrospectively.
