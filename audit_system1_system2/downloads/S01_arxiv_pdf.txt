                                             System-2, through larger models, more data and compute, longer reasoning, and more agents. We ask whether the next
                                             stage may come instead from co-designing it with System-1, a lightweight, semantically aware predictor that evaluates
                                             candidate interactions before execution, so that System-2 acts only on those worth their cost. Our answer is a qualified
                                             yes. We study conditional interaction, a predict–gate–execute–update loop: System-1 estimates each candidate’s

                                         1 The Interaction Problem at Scale
                                         Recent advances toward artificial general intelligence (AGI) (Morris et al., 2024) have largely focused on scaling
                                         model capacity, data volume, and computational resources (Sutton, 2019; Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022),
                                         along with innovations in architectures, pretraining or post-training techniques, retrieval, and inference-time search.
                                         A significant portion of this investment has gone to a resource-intensive intelligent system, referred to as System-2,
                                         which handles reasoning, planning, decision-making, function calling, or even computer use. As frontier large models
                                         (Jaech et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025), extend compute, and larger collectives of agents emerge, the potential of this
                                         executive system grows proportionally with resource availability. However, as systems become more capable, the costs
                                         of generating, evaluating, and acting on each additional interaction may become a constraining factor. This presents an
                                         alternative scaling challenge: not merely how many interactions a system can manage, but which interactions justify
                                         their associated costs.
                                         An interaction encompasses any information exchange between an intelligent system and its data, computations, or
                                         environment. It gathers information at a cost: for a large language model (LLM), a generation episode in which a prompt
                                         produces tokens, such as a Chain-of-Thought (CoT) (Wei et al., 2022); for an embodied or reinforcement-learning
                                         agent, a trajectory of states, actions, and rewards; for a tool-using agent, a tool invocation that calls an external
                                         resource and integrates its result. Here, we treat interaction as the unit of analysis, recognizing that these operations
                                         are not necessarily equivalent in a physical or statistical sense; they share that each incurs resource commitments
                                         before observing the outcome and realizes utility only afterward. Even though reasoning models (Guo et al., 2025)
                                         and embodied agents (Kim et al., 2024) benefit from increased interactions, the relative returns from each interaction

Determining which interactions to execute is a challenge that System-2 cannot resolve autonomously because assessing
an interaction’s value must cost considerably less than executing it. This necessitates the co-design of System-1
and System-2, and this work introduces the conditional interaction as a feasible co-design principle: a lightweight
System-1 predicts the utility of potential interactions, the resource-intensive System-2 executes only those interactions
that exceed a resource-aware threshold, and the resulting outcomes then serve to refine System-1. Because each
system shapes the other, the two must be designed together, and this Perspective asks whether such co-design is a
promising path toward AGI. Our central hypothesis posits that the density of utility-relevant interactions, denoted as
𝜌𝑢 , rather than the sheer count of interactions alone, serves as a pivotal metric of scaling efficiency. The co-design of
System-1 and System-2, as detailed below, indicates a promising approach for scenarios where interactions are costly,
exhibit uneven value, and can be anticipated. This framework is based on the premise that low-cost experiments can
be conducted and foreseen effectively.

The Closing of the Accumulation Frontier. The pursuit of accumulating additional data is increasingly difficult.
Human-generated data are finite, since pretraining consolidates much of humanity’s recorded text into a single model
and the stock of public text may be depleted within this decade (Villalobos et al., 2024), while high-quality data
are scarcer still, as the subset that teaches most is only a fraction of that stock. Synthetic data shift the focus from

quantity to coverage: they can partially replenish both (Xu et al., 2025), but the space of synthesizable interactions is
combinatorially vast, e.g., robotic data alone would require covering scenes, objects, dynamics, lighting, and sensing,
so the pertinent question becomes which synthetic interactions are worth their cost. Moreover, some interactions
are inherently expensive: in scientific discovery, robotics, and specialized clinical and industrial settings, data are
produced one experiment, actuation, or measurement at a time, at the pace of the underlying process, and no amount
of computational power can accelerate a chemical reaction. Shifting the focus toward continual, post-deployment
adaptation, we can find that self-evolution sharpens rather than alleviates the bottleneck: an agent that interacts
indiscriminately exhausts its finite budget on experiences that yield minimal task-relevant learning, so its progress
hinges on which interactions it selects, precisely the decision this Perspective examines.

All of the above discussions raise a fundamental Research Question: How can a System-2 be aware of an interaction’s
value before execution?

Our position: co-design the two systems, and the conditional interaction decides what the other pays for. In
response, we prioritize architecture over algorithm: give the executive a partner whose job is to judge, and design
the two together. This work argues that a major part of the next advance toward AGI and beyond may come from
co-designing System-1 and System-2 rather than from scaling System-2 alone: specifying, training, and scaling the
two as one system, where System-1 decides what System-2 executes, which executive runs it, how much it may spend,
and what it keeps in memory; System-2’s outcomes are System-1’s supervision; and each is sized with the other in
view (Section 3). Integrating a cheap predictor with a costly executor introduces three decisions at scale: which
prompts, tasks, or requests merit rollouts, trials, or extensive thought chains (compute); which samples, buffers, caches,
and context entries should be preserved (storage); and how costly outcomes should adjust the inexpensive judgment
that allocated them (feedback).

𝜌𝑢 : an eﬃciency coordinate to scale limited resource. Classical scaling laws express performance P through
parameters 𝑁, data 𝐷, and compute C. The density of utility-relevant interactions 𝜌𝑢 , the fraction of candidate
interactions whose utility 𝑢 clears a declared floor 𝑢 min in Eq. (14), is not an additional resource but an efficiency
coordinate P = 𝑓 (𝑁, 𝐷, C; 𝜌𝑢 ) describing how well the three convert into capability, generated by the learning
dynamics rather than supplied externally. For the canonical utility, Bayesian information gain, 𝜌𝑢 is the informative
interaction density 𝜌info , a special case of the general 𝜌𝑢 in which every formal result is stated; every statement is
nonetheless relative to a declared utility, since the densities of prompts with non-zero GRPO advantage, of tasks above
an adaptation-risk threshold, and of queries whose answer improves with more compute are different quantities and
are not interchangeable.
Uninformative interactions waste both computation and the data slots they occupy, so the effective resources under
unconditional training are discounted as 𝜌𝑢 𝐷 and 𝜌𝑢 C. The compute discount is a direct accounting identity; 𝜌𝑢 𝐷 is
a proposed measure of utility-relevant data consumed, not a smaller available corpus, which a gate leaves unchanged.
Two interpretations of this fraction must be kept apart. The pool fraction 𝜌𝑢 (𝜃 𝑡 ) is a property of the candidate
distribution at the current state, produced by the learning dynamics, and no gate can change it; the executed fraction
is the share of interactions actually paid for that are informative. Unconditional training executes the pool, so the
two coincide, while a perfect gate drives the executed fraction to one. Conditional interaction raises the executed
fraction, so that at fixed 𝑁, 𝐷 and C more of the budget tends to buy utility-relevant interactions and, hypothetically,
as a dynamic account of the exponent. Note that 𝜌𝑢 depends on the policy state, it is not a free axis.

What we claim, and with what conﬁdence. Throughout this work, the claim hierarchy is as follows. (i) Premise:
future digital and robotic systems may be limited by interaction efficiency more than by parameters or data. (ii)
Position: progress toward general intelligence should co-design a cheap System-1 with a costly System-2, coupled by a
strategy that values interactions before execution and suppresses those predicted to be uninformative. (iii) Hypothesis:
the density of utility-relevant interactions 𝜌𝑢 (𝜌info for information gain) is an important determinant of how efficiently
resources become capability. (iv) Prediction: 𝜌𝑢 decays as the system improves, and this decay may account for part
of the diminishing returns and of the scaling exponent, so that scaling laws are better described in informative compute
than in compute alone.
The rest of this Perspective proceeds as follows: formalizing the predict–gate–execute–update loop to implement
the conditional interaction (Section 2); building and co-scaling the two subsystems (Section 3); deriving the loop’s
screening and regret properties, and why a lightweight System-1 can provide them (Section 4); instantiating the
framework across digital training, physical adaptation, test-time computation and agentic use (Sections 5 and 6); and
formulating informative compute as a scaling hypothesis (Section 7).


2 Conditional Interaction as A Co-Design Strategy: Predict Before You Pay
Co-design requires an interface: a precise statement of what System-1 judges, what System-2 executes as a resource-
intensive operation, and what flows back. Conditional interaction is that interface. Both systems are defined by
execution role rather than model family: System-1 is a lightweight, semantic-aware module that takes a candidate
interaction as input, e.g., a prompt, task configuration, query, or tool call, with its context and returns a score rather
than a solution, and it is consulted on every candidate at a cost 𝑐 pred ≪ 𝑐 exec . System-2 is the resource-demanding
model or agent that performs the interaction, and is invoked only on the candidates System-1 certifies. The loop runs
in both directions, making it co-design rather than a filter bolted onto a finished executive: System-1 shapes the data
and effort System-2 receives, and System-2’s outcomes become System-1’s supervision. AlphaZero illustrates this,
since its play comes not from deeper search alone but from a policy-value network and a tree search that trained
each other (Silver et al., 2018): a more capable System-2 raises the intelligence of accomplishing tasks, while a
well-calibrated System-1 determines how much of it is reached per unit of cost and latency (See Fig. 3).

Static cascades versus adaptive co-design. The pairing can be built in two ways (See Fig. 4). In a static cascade,
a standalone fast model classifies each request and hands the hard minority to a large model; Jev, a commercial
model described by its developers as a “System One” (TypeSafe AI, 2026), is a clear instance, returning calibrated
probabilities over answers defined in advance (Osman, 2026). Such products illustrate commercial interest in fast,
structured judgment models compatible with the System-1 role, although a single product does not establish an
industry-wide convergence. A static cascade is, by construction, trained once, routes only at the inference layer, and
does not receive System-2’s outcomes, which suffices when its predictions are stationary but fails here, because how
informative an interaction is for the current System-2 changes with every update. In co-design, System-1 instead
predicts utility such as difficulty or learning value relative to the current System-2; it acts in training as well as at
inference, scheduling compute and storage such as rollout budgets, replay buffers, batches, key–value (KV) caches,
context windows, rather than a single call path. System-2’s realized rewards, gradients, and outcomes recalibrate
System-1 online (See Fig. 5). A cascade retrained online on its outcomes moves toward co-design, so the distinction is
not the presence of two models but feedback and joint optimization.

Conditional interaction provides a common predict-then-gate architecture for online prompt selection in LLM

2.4 Scope: When Gating Is and Is Not Justiﬁed

The principle applies only where interactions are individually costly, heterogeneous in informativeness, and predictable
before execution by a model cheaper than execution itself. Gating has no guaranteed advantage when candidates are
homogeneous in utility, when predicting their utility costs about as much as executing them, or when execution is
cheap. Dense pretraining, minibatch stochastic gradient descent over independent, identically distributed data, and
static supervised learning often come close to these conditions, but need not: a fixed corpus can hold examples whose
value differs and is predictable from cheap features, which data-selection methods exploit. A gain observed there calls
for an accounting of where it comes from, not a refutation of the principle. Within that regime, gating is justified
when a predictor exists whose cost is well below execution cost and whose error 𝜀 𝜙 is small relative to the utility gaps
it must resolve (Theorem 18).
It should be relaxed, executing uncertain or even predicted-uninformative interactions, when: (1) the predictor is poorly
calibrated or off-distribution, since its blind spots are where it wrongly suppresses; (2) interactions carry option or
exploratory value not captured by one-step utility; (3) prediction costs about as much as execution, voiding Theorem 14;
(4) false negatives are catastrophic (safety-critical control, irreversible actions); or (5) the utility is misspecified, so
that the predictor systematically suppresses the very interactions that would reveal the misspecification.
In such regimes the right object is an exploratory value of information, e.g. an upper-confidence or Thompson
criterion, replacing 𝑢ˆ in Eq. (15) with 𝑢ˆ + 𝑐 𝜎𝜙 , which recovers pure exploitation as 𝑐 → 0 and uncertainty-seeking
exploration, dominated by 𝜎𝜙 , as 𝑐 → ∞. That limit favours the most uncertain candidates rather than sampling
the pool uniformly; undirected exploration needs a separate mixture with a uniform policy, as in Section 7.2; the
uncertainty-aware acquisition functionals of Eqs. (65) and (62) (Section B) are the mechanism by which the principle
avoids over-pruning. Conditional interaction is a statement about how to spend a trustworthy informativeness signal,
not a claim that one always exists. Regimes (1) and (5) are the most insidious, since the predictor’s errors concentrate
precisely where it has no data and the interactions that would reveal them are never executed; the audit sampling
described after Theorem 14 is the minimal safeguard, and Section 7.2 treats this censoring formally.

3.2 Co-Scaling: System-1’s Size as System-2 Grows

Current practice sizes System-2 and treats any selector as an afterthought. Eq. (13) suggests this is incomplete: a
larger System-1 lowers its error rates but raises 𝑐 pred , and where the balance lies depends on how expensive System-2
is. Let System-1 have a size 𝑠 from a totally ordered set S1 (parameters, depth, or context), with error rates fp(𝑠),
fn(𝑠) and scoring cost 𝑐 pred (𝑠), and let scaling System-2 multiply every execution cost by a factor 𝑘 ≥ 1, so that
C̄rel ↦→ 𝑘 C̄rel and C̄dead ↦→ 𝑘 C̄dead .
Proposition 13 (Co-Scaling Under Fixed Error Curves). Assume the setting of Theorem 7(ii); that 𝜌𝑢 , fp(𝑠) and
fn(𝑠) < 1 do not depend on 𝑘; that 𝑐 pred (𝑠)/(1 − fn(𝑠)) is non-decreasing in 𝑠; and that the IIE ratio Eq. (13) attains
its maximum over S1 for every 𝑘, on a set 𝑆★ (𝑘) of optimal System-1 sizes. Then (i) 𝑆★ is non-decreasing in 𝑘 in
the strong set order: if 𝑘 ′ > 𝑘, 𝑠 ∈ 𝑆★ (𝑘) and 𝑠′ ∈ 𝑆★ (𝑘 ′ ), then max(𝑠, 𝑠′ ) ∈ 𝑆★ (𝑘 ′ ) and min(𝑠, 𝑠′ ) ∈ 𝑆★ (𝑘), so the
largest and the smallest optimal sizes, where they exist, are non-decreasing in 𝑘; and (ii) the maximal IIE ratio is
non-decreasing in 𝑘.

The mechanism the proof isolates is that scaling System-2 dilutes the predictor’s own cost, 𝑐 pred /𝑘 relative to execution,
but not the cost of its mistakes, which grows with System-2 itself, so accuracy becomes relatively cheaper to buy.
Under these assumptions, a System-1 that is optimal for one System-2 remains optimal or becomes too small, never
too large, as System-2’s cost scale grows; the proposition does not say how large the resulting loss is. The result is
comparative statics under fixed error curves: it does not show that growing System-1 helps an evolving executive, nor
at what rate System-1 should grow, and both remain experimental hypotheses. Its assumptions do most of the work,
and it does not imply the same monotonicity when scaling System-2 changes the prediction problem itself, as happens
whenever a stronger executive changes the distribution of utilities, the separation between useful and dead interactions,
or the cost structure. The statement is also qualitative: it fixes the direction of co-scaling, not its rate, since fp(𝑠),
fn(𝑠) and 𝑐 pred (𝑠) are empirical curves. Measuring them, and hence a compute-optimal ratio between the two systems
in the spirit of the parameter-to-data ratio of Hoffmann et al. (2022), is the co-design counterpart of a scaling law
(Section 7).

4 What Prediction Buys, and Why a Small System-1 Can Work
Sections 2 and 3 defined the gate and the architecture built around it. Co-design pays only if System-1 is both much
cheaper than System-2 and accurate enough to steer it; this section asks when both hold. Section 4.1 states the core
formal results: what prediction buys, in time and memory, and the regret a cheap predictor risks. The remaining
subsections ask why a cheap System-1 can make a useful prediction about an expensive interaction at all, from three
complementary views. These connections are interpretive rather than separate contributions; each supplies insight
and, under its own assumptions, a guarantee, but none is a derivation of the framework. Information-theoretically
(Section 4.2), the quantity gated on is expected information gain net of cost, with epistemic action selection as its
limiting case. Generatively (Section 4.3), an interaction is a transition of a world or policy model, and System-1
is an amortized projection of that model onto a single scalar, which is why valuation is cheaper than simulation.
Control-theoretically (Section 4.4), executing only what the gate admits is receding-horizon control of a learner whose
own dynamics are still being learned.

limit (Theorem 2), not a consequence of it. For a fixed positive selection margin, regret is exactly zero once the
predictor is accurate relative to the separation of interactions (2𝜀 𝜙 < gapmin ); regret is measured in the units of the
declared utility, and gapmin enters not as a denominator but as the threshold deciding whether any misranking can

5 Conditional Interaction Across Digital and Physical Domains
Reasoning-model fine-tuning and embodied-agent training are instances of one abstraction, distinguished by state
space, cost structure, and verifier. In both, conditional interaction learns a predictor of value from past outcomes and
executes the interactions whose predicted value clears the threshold 𝜅 𝑡 of Theorem 1. The two instantiations share one
predicament — finding out whether a task is worth training on often costs almost as much as training on it — while
differing in why. Table 1 summarizes the correspondence together with the test-time and agentic settings.

5.1 Digital and Physical Instantiations in the Literature

Digital: from ﬁltering after generation to predicting before it. In RLVR only prompts whose sampled responses
disagree carry a group-relative advantage, and that band drifts as the policy improves (Qu et al., 2026b). Early methods
filter after generating: dynamic sampling in DAPO discards prompts whose groups are uniformly correct or wrong
(Yu et al., 2026), and online difficulty filtering keeps prompts of intermediate pass rate (Bae et al., 2026), both paying
in full for the rollouts that reveal a prompt to be uninformative — the execute-then-filter baseline of Theorem 14. Later
methods predict before generating: GRESO skips prompts that their reward history marks as likely zero-variance
(Zheng et al., 2026); MoPPS tracks each prompt’s success rate with an online Bayesian posterior and approaches an
evaluation-intensive dynamic-sampling baseline with far fewer rollouts (Qu et al., 2026a); DPS, BOTS and PCL model


                                                                   22
prompt difficulty with a hidden-Markov process, Thompson sampling and an on-policy value model, respectively
(Mao et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2026; Gao et al., 2026); and GPS trains one small generative predictor on the shared
optimization history to select prompts for a much larger reasoning model, improving training efficiency and test-time
compute allocation (Qu et al., 2026c). The same move extends beyond prompts, to online batch selection in supervised
fine-tuning (Zou et al., 2025), to demonstration selection for in-context learning, where a small trained model makes
a single edit to the retrieved demonstrations instead of iteratively scoring or searching over demonstration subsets
(Wen et al., 2026b), to rollout allocation across tasks and trajectory prefixes in agentic RL (Zou et al., 2026b), and to
critic-free, tree-aware credit assignment for long-horizon agents (Zou et al., 2026a). These results illustrate rather
than validate the framework.

Physical: from randomizing to predicting adaptation risk. For embodied agents, judging a task configuration
means acting on it, on hardware or in a high-fidelity simulator, at the pace of physics. Domain randomization trains
across wide ranges of simulated appearance and dynamics (Tobin et al., 2017), with an explicit assessment of transfer
(Muratore et al., 2018); because uniform randomization spends most trajectories on mastered configurations, later
work prioritizes, through active and automatic domain randomization (Mehta et al., 2020; Akkaya et al., 2019) and
through environment design and level replay driven by regret or learning potential (Dennis et al., 2020; Jiang et al.,
2021), though still by executing configurations to score them. When robustness matters, the target is the adverse tail
of post-adaptation risk, measured by the conditional value-at-risk (Rockafellar et al., 2000) and pursued in robust
meta-learning (Finn et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2023; Lv et al., 2024), risk-averse planning (Carpin et al., 2016) and
safety-critical scenario generation (Rempe et al., 2022). Locating that tail requires evaluating many tasks; MPTS
removes the evaluation by learning a risk predictive model from the optimization history (Wang et al., 2026), and its
successors add posterior sampling and diversity (Qu et al., 2025) and in-context risk prediction (Wen et al., 2026a),
yielding curriculum-like sampling under a calibrated predictor.

What the two literatures share. Evaluation requires generating in the digital regime and acting in the physical one,
yet both literatures converge on the same loop: System-1 scores a candidate from cheap features and history, System-2
executes only what clears the score, and System-2’s outcomes refit System-1. They differ in utility (mixed outcomes
versus tail adaptation risk; Theorem 5) and in the conserved resource (compute versus actuation); their acquisition
functions are collected in Section B. Unlike post-hoc data valuation by influence functions or data Shapley (Koh and
Liang, 2017; Ghorbani and Zou, 2019), and unlike active learning, experimental design and bandit index policies over
a static pool (Krishnakumar, 2007; Chaloner and Verdinelli, 1995; Gittins, 1979), these methods value candidates
before execution and track a moving learner. They also extend earlier fast–slow pairings, such as expert iteration and
AlphaZero (Anthony et al., 2017; Silver et al., 2018) and speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023), from one game
or one decoding step to training and deployment.


6 Conditional Interaction at Test Time
The framework so far concerns training. The same decision problem appears at inference, but with a different state,
utility and cost ledger. Nothing in Theorem 1 and Eq. (9) requires the training setting: the rule gates an interaction on
predicted utility net of cost, whether the resource is a training rollout or an inference-time forward pass. We argue
that test-time scaling and adaptive decision-making, usually studied separately, can be cast as test-time instances of
conditional interaction. Prospective metacognitive control of test-time compute is an active line of work in its own
right (Nazi and Dipta, 2026; Cao et al., 2026); the contribution here is not to discover it but to place it in the same
ledger as training. Co-design therefore does not end at deployment: the System-1 that shaped training goes on to
ration System-2’s inference effort.

7 A Testable Scaling Hypothesis, and Open Problems
Co-design makes two kinds of prediction: what the pair should achieve as it scales, and where it can fail. We state the

a changed scaling exponent.

7.2 Selective Supervision: The Gate Censors Its Own Training Data

The most consequential technical limitation is structural: once System-1 decides which interactions are executed, it also
decides which interactions it is trained on. With 𝑝 cand (𝑖) the candidate distribution and 𝑒(𝑖) = Pr[𝑖 executed | 𝑖, H0:𝑡 ]
the gate’s execution probability, the predictor is trained on

                                          𝑝 train (𝑖) ∝ 𝑝 cand (𝑖) 𝑒(𝑖) ≠ 𝑝 cand (𝑖),                                  (57)

so labels are missing not at random: whether an outcome is observed depends on the predictor’s own estimate of it,
and in the worst case a candidate scored as low-value is suppressed, its outcome never observed, and the score never
corrected. This is the selective-labels problem of algorithmic decision-making (Lakkaraju et al., 2017), the positivity
problem of causal inference, and, under shifting distributions, the label-querying problem of active self-training (Zhou
et al., 2022b,a). Three features make it tractable rather than hopeless. Positivity can be designed in, but not by
temperature: the indicator in Eq. (3) gives every candidate with 𝑢ˆ < 𝜅 𝑡 probability zero at every finite 𝛽, so a soft
gate only randomizes within the admissible set. Positivity needs an explicit component that reaches the suppressed
region: an exploration mixture 𝜋 𝜖 = (1 − 𝜖) 𝜋CI + 𝜖 𝜋0 with 𝜋0 (𝑖) > 0 on the whole pool, which gives 𝑒(𝑖) ≥ 𝜖 𝜋0 (𝑖)
per draw, or independent audit sampling of suppressed candidates at a known rate 𝑎, which gives 𝑒(𝑖) ≥ 𝑎. Either way
𝑒(𝑖) ≥ 𝑒 min > 0. The propensities are known, because the gate assigns them, so weighting the predictor’s loss by
1/𝑒(𝑖) (Horvitz and Thompson, 1952), or combining that weighting with the predictor’s own regression in a doubly
robust estimator (Dudík et al., 2011), gives unbiased estimates of its error on the full candidate distribution. And
the audit sample of suppressed candidates (Section 4.1) is exactly such a positivity-preserving exploration stream,
provided its rate is fixed independently of the predictor’s scores. What remains open is quantitative: how large 𝑒 min
must be for the predictor’s error to shrink while the policy and the candidate distribution drift, how the variance of
inverse-propensity weights trades against the screening gain, and whether any rate like Eq. (20) survives adaptive,
censored collection. Of all the problems the framework raises, we regard this as the one most in need of theory.

7.3 Open Problems and Outlook

The decay profile of 𝜌𝑢 (𝜃 𝑡 ) has not yet been measured, and doing so is the cleanest single test of the framework; it
would also test a mechanistic account of emergence, since a sub-skill whose 𝜌𝑢 crosses a usable threshold abruptly
should appear abruptly. We regard this as the most actionable contribution of this Perspective.
Several questions stay open. Because aggressive gating discards informative interactions while permissive gating
readmits dead ones, efficiency is maximized at an interior admission rate 𝜌 ∗ (𝜀 𝜙 ), whose characterization sits inside a


                                                             28
non-stationary bandit problem (Auer et al., 2002), as does its inference-time counterpart, whether a halting predictor
can reach the accuracy Theorem 22 requires without its own forward pass eroding the savings. Theorem 13 fixes the
direction in which System-1 should grow with System-2, and sweeping System-1’s size at several System-2 sizes while
measuring fp, fn and 𝑐 pred would give the compute-optimal split between them. And the framework is single-learner:
in agent societies, self-play and debate, informativeness is a property of joint beliefs, suggesting a relational utility
and an informative fraction defined on an interaction graph, since an interaction uninformative to its initiator may
inform an observer. We regard this last as the most consequential boundary of the framework.
Conditional interaction finally resonates, without being equivalent, with the enactive view of cognition urged for
AI by Rafiee and Sutton (2026), building on Varela et al. (2017) and the “era of experience” (Silver and Sutton,
2025): perception as selective activity, in which an agent acts in order to reveal information. Our utility nonetheless
answers to an externally given task and cost ledger rather than the agent’s own self-maintenance, so we formalize the
selective-engagement facet alone. Within that scope, routing tasks to System-2 executives (Section 2.3) extends the
gate from data to model selection and Section 6 adds the inference boundary, so a fully conditional system, whose data,
computation, inference effort and external actions are all gated by a co-designed System-1, is a promising architecture
for resource-efficient intelligence at scale, and the argument strengthens with capability if both Theorem 25 and
Theorem 13 hold. Beyond human-level performance (Mulgan, 2016), human data and curricula would no longer say
what is worth learning, and a system would have to choose its own experiments; its System-1 would then decide what
a superhuman System-2 learns and does, making the gate a major source of efficiency.


8 Conclusion
This work poses the perspective that the next stage of progress toward AGI may come from the co-design of
System-1 and System-2: a cheap System-1 that values each candidate interaction before it is paid for, and a costly
System-2 that executes only what clears the gate, each trained on the other’s outputs. Conditional interaction, the
predict–gate–execute–update loop that couples them, applies alike to training rollouts, test-time reasoning and agentic
actions, and under explicit assumptions yields screening, memory, regret and co-scaling results. These results motivate
the scaling hypothesis of Section 7. Our answer to the research question is therefore a qualified yes: the co-design
is promising wherever interactions are costly, unequal in value and predictable in advance. The decisive next step
is measurement: quantify 𝜌𝑢 , compare scaling curves at matched informative compute, and test whether co-design
changes more than a constant factor.
