 Abstract 
 Computing molecular free-energy landscapes over collective variables (CVs) is central to understanding molecular mechanisms across physics, chemistry, and biology, but poses a difficult learning problem without training data available a priori . The microscopic potential is known, yet samples of the marginal CV distribution must be generated by molecular dynamics (MD), whose trajectories can remain trapped in metastable states and rarely cross the free-energy barriers governing conformational changes or chemical reactions. We introduce VESPER, which couples the generation of these samples to neural-network-driven free-energy learning: MD supplies configurations for learning, while the evolving model biases the MD to accelerate exploration and generate further training data. VESPER minimizes a single variational objective that is convex in the free energy and can combine several tempering levels, each using a distinct temperature-boosting factor to flatten the effective free-energy landscape and promote barrier crossing. Every level has the same physical free energy as its minimizer, allowing data from all levels to train one shared model without reweighting. Learning is driven by the difference in mean parameter gradients between biased MD configurations and auxiliary walkers that explore the flattened model directly in CV space. Replica exchange between levels can further accelerate exploration. We prove conditional convergence of the coupled overdamped population dynamics without replica exchange under log-Sobolev and Polyak-Łojasiewicz assumptions. VESPER matches or improves on established enhanced-sampling methods on alanine dipeptide and yields self-consistent free-energy reconstructions from three to six CVs on alanine tetrapeptide. For methane dehydrogenation on Ni(511) with a machine-learned interatomic potential, it recovers two-CV Boltzmann marginals and a reactant-to-saddle free-energy difference along a three-CV path, both qualitatively consistent with the lower-dimensional reference. It also samples RMSD-defined folded and unfolded regions of chignolin while biasing seventeen CVs. 
 1 Introduction 
 Molecular dynamics (MD) is a central tool for studying the structure and dynamics of molecular systems in physics, chemistry, and biology. A recurring goal is to compute the free-energy landscape over a set of informative collective variables (CVs), defined as the negative logarithm of their marginal density under the equilibrium distribution, multiplied by the thermal energy k_{B}T . This landscape quantifies the relative stability of metastable states and the barriers separating them. Given samples from this marginal, learning it would be a standard estimation problem. In MD, however, the microscopic potential is known, but the relevant configurations must be generated by simulation. Brute-force MD is often computationally prohibitive: barrier-crossing transitions between metastable states can occur on microsecond-to-millisecond timescales, whereas integration requires femtosecond timesteps [ Hénin et al., 2022 ] . Free-energy learning is therefore coupled to a sampling problem, and the model must accelerate the simulation while it is itself being learned. 
 We introduce VESPER (Variational Extended Space Parallel Ensemble Regression), which learns a neural free energy G_{\theta} while using it to bias the simulations that produce its own training data. VESPER minimizes a single variational objective: a positively weighted sum of components, one per tempering level, each using a distinct temperature-boosting factor to flatten the effective free-energy landscape. Each component is convex in the free energy and is minimized by the physical free energy at every such level. The sum therefore has the same minimizer up to an additive constant, and every level contributes its gradient to one model without reweighting to the physical ensemble. VESPER couples biased microscopic dynamics, persistent auxiliary CV walkers, and free-energy learning through one shared model. Through the CV map s(x) , the same G_{\theta} sets both the MD bias and the tempered auxiliary potential, with no separate free-energy recursion. We also establish conditional convergence of the coupled sampling and learning dynamics. These contributions build on established variational connections: individual components extend the pseudo-spherical family to negative exponents and admit Rényi variational forms [ Yu et al., 2021 , Fujisawa & Eguchi, 2008 , Birrell et al., 2021 ] , and their gradients recover the instantaneous update of well-tempered variationally enhanced sampling (VES), up to scale, when its target is held fixed during differentiation [ Valsson & Parrinello, 2014 , Valsson & Parrinello, 2015 , Bonati et al., 2019 ] . 
 The gradient of each component is the difference between two averages of \nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta} : one over MD configurations evolving under the microscopic potential plus a bias built from G_{\theta} , the other over persistent auxiliary walkers evolving directly in CV space under a tempered G_{\theta} . At the optimum both populations sample the same flattened distribution, and the walkers interact with the molecular system only through the shared parameters, so no adiabatic limit is needed to identify the free energy, unlike in temperature-accelerated MD and driven adiabatic free-energy dynamics [ Maragliano & Vanden-Eijnden, 2006 , Abrams & Tuckerman, 2008 ] . Replica exchange can additionally carry configurations discovered under strongly flattened biases down the ladder, facilitating barrier crossing along the CVs. 
 Our main contributions are: 
 • 
 A single objective with a common minimizer. 
We establish that the losses associated with all tempering levels are convex in the free-energy function and share the physical free energy as their minimizer, up to an additive constant. Their positively weighted sum therefore defines one objective for one model, with every level contributing its native gradient without reweighting to the physical ensemble ( Proposition 1 ). 
 • 
 Online learning from coupled sampling populations. 
Biased MD and persistent auxiliary CV walkers generate the configurations needed for learning as the model evolves, without a pre-existing dataset or an adiabatic limit between microscopic and auxiliary coordinates. We prove conditional convergence of the coupled overdamped population dynamics without replica exchange under log-Sobolev and Polyak-Łojasiewicz assumptions, controlling both objective error and sampling lag ( Section A.6 ). 
 • 
 Replica exchange on the learned free energy. 
The Metropolis acceptance probability for exchange between adjacent levels depends only on learned free-energy differences in lower-dimensional CV space, avoiding additional evaluations of the microscopic potential. At a fixed model, the exchange kernel preserves the product of the sampling laws across levels and therefore leaves the population gradient unchanged. 
 • 
 Controlled tests of tempering and replica exchange. 
On the Müller-Brown potential, the tempering ladder balances basin accuracy, measured by KL divergence, with landscape reconstruction, measured by RMSE. On four-well landscapes spanning two to one hundred dimensions, a fixed ladder remains competitive without dimension-specific selection of \alpha , with replica exchange improving accuracy and convergence in the most challenging high-dimensional case ( Section 3.1 ). 
 • 
 Molecular free-energy reconstruction and high-dimensional exploration. 
On alanine dipeptide, VESPER achieves competitive reconstruction accuracy and convergence relative to the tested enhanced-sampling baselines. On alanine tetrapeptide, agreement between Boltzmann marginals learned with three and six CVs demonstrates dimensional self-consistency. For methane dehydrogenation on Ni(511) with a machine-learned interatomic potential, VESPER learns a three-CV landscape whose two-CV Boltzmann marginals and reactant-to-saddle free-energy difference along a three-CV path are consistent with the lower-dimensional reference. On chignolin, biasing seventeen dihedral CVs produces repeated RMSD-defined unfolding and refolding transitions, demonstrating effective exploration of a high-dimensional molecular CV space ( Section 3 ). 
 2 Methodology 
 2.1 Collective variables and free energy 
 Let x\in\mathcal{X} denote a molecular configuration, U(x) its microscopic potential energy, and \varrho(x)=Z^{-1}e^{-\beta U(x)} its equilibrium density at inverse temperature \beta=(k_{B}T)^{-1} . A collective-variable map s:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{S} reduces the d -dimensional configuration space to a D -dimensional CV domain, typically with D\ll d . The equilibrium CV density and its associated free energy are 
 p(s)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\delta(s-s(x))\varrho(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad G_{\star}(s)=-\beta^{-1}\log p(s). 
 (1) 
 The microscopic potential supplies the forces for simulation, whereas the marginal p(s) must be inferred from configurations generated by the simulation. VESPER represents G_{\star} by a differentiable model G_{\theta} and uses it to bias the molecular dynamics that generate its training configurations. 
 Figure 1: VESPER couples sampling and learning. Each tempering level k carries two walker populations, and all levels share one model G_{\theta} . Left: physical walkers evolve in configuration space under U(x)-(1-\alpha_{k})G_{\theta}(s(x)) and are mapped to CV space by s(x) . Replica exchange swaps configurations between adjacent levels. Right: auxiliary walkers evolve directly in CV space under \alpha_{k}G_{\theta}(s) . The two populations supply the averages in equation 10 , whose c_{k} -weighted difference updates \theta . The updated model then sets the forces on both populations. At convergence, both populations at level k sample the same flattened landscape \alpha_{k}G_{\star} . During learning, the instantaneous target for the physical CVs is Q_{G_{\theta},k}\propto p\,e^{(\beta-\hat{\beta}_{k})G_{\theta}} . 
 2.2 The variational objective 
 Fix an auxiliary inverse temperature 0 
 \mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}}[G]=\frac{1}{\hat{\beta}}\log\int_{\mathcal{S}}e^{-\hat{\beta}G(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s+\frac{1}{\beta-\hat{\beta}}\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{(\beta-\hat{\beta})G(s(x))}\varrho(x)\,\mathrm{d}x. 
 (2) 
 The two terms are the log-partition functions of a tempered version of the model on \mathcal{S} and of the equilibrium density tilted by the model. They define the auxiliary CV law 
 P_{G,\hat{\beta}}(s)=\frac{e^{-\hat{\beta}G(s)}}{A_{G}},\qquad A_{G}=\int_{\mathcal{S}}e^{-\hat{\beta}G(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s 
 (3) 
 and the biased microscopic law together with its CV marginal, 
 \nu_{G,\hat{\beta}}(x)=\frac{e^{(\beta-\hat{\beta})G(s(x))}\varrho(x)}{B_{G}},\qquad Q_{G,\hat{\beta}}(s)=\frac{p(s)e^{(\beta-\hat{\beta})G(s)}}{B_{G}},\qquad B_{G}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{(\beta-\hat{\beta})G(s(x))}\varrho(x)\,\mathrm{d}x. 
 (4) 
 The first variation of equation 2 in a direction h is \mathbb{E}_{Q_{G,\hat{\beta}}}h-\mathbb{E}_{P_{G,\hat{\beta}}}h , so G is stationary exactly when the two CV laws coincide: 
 \frac{e^{-\hat{\beta}G}}{A_{G}}=\frac{p\,e^{(\beta-\hat{\beta})G}}{B_{G}}\iff e^{-\beta G}\propto p\iff G=G_{\star}+C. 
 (5) 
 The tempering exponent cancels: for every \alpha , the stationary point is the physical free energy. The second variation, \hat{\beta}\,\mathrm{Var}_{P_{G,\hat{\beta}}}(h)+(\beta-\hat{\beta})\,\mathrm{Var}_{Q_{G,\hat{\beta}}}(h) , is nonnegative, so \mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}} is convex and its minimizer set is \{G_{\star}+C:C\in\mathbb{R}\} ( proposition S1 ). At the minimizer, 
 P_{G_{\star},\hat{\beta}}=Q_{G_{\star},\hat{\beta}}=\pi_{\alpha},\qquad\pi_{\alpha}\propto e^{-\alpha\beta G_{\star}}\propto p^{\alpha}, 
 (6) 
 so both populations explore the same flattened landscape, with barriers reduced by the factor \alpha . Near the minimizer,
 \mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}}[G]-\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}}[G_{\star}]\approx\tfrac{\beta}{2}\mathrm{Var}_{\pi_{\alpha}}(G-G_{\star}) :
 \alpha does not change the target, only where errors are weighted, with smaller \alpha giving more weight to high free-energy regions. 
 For a parametric model G_{\theta} , the chain rule gives 
 \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}}[G_{\theta}]=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{G_{\theta},\hat{\beta}}}\!\left[\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta}(s(x))\right]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{G_{\theta},\hat{\beta}}}\!\left[\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta}(s)\right]. 
 (7) 
 Neither expectation requires knowing p . The first is over configurations sampled by MD biased with G_{\theta} , and the second over walkers sampling the tempered model in CV space ( Section 2.4 ). 
 2.3 The combined objective 
 Choose 0\leq\hat{\beta}_{1} 2 for 0 Section A.2 : 
 \mathcal{L}_{\beta}[G]=\frac{1}{\beta}\log\int_{\mathcal{S}}e^{-\beta G(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s+\mathbb{E}_{p}[G],\qquad\mathcal{L}_{0}[G]=-\mathbb{E}_{u}[G]+\frac{1}{\beta}\log\mathbb{E}_{p}\bigl[e^{\beta G}\bigr]. 
 (8) 
 Here u is the uniform law on \mathcal{S} , and \mathcal{L}_{0} is taken after subtracting the constant \hat{\beta}^{-1}\log|\mathcal{S}| from equation 2 . The first is the maximum-likelihood objective, \beta^{-1}\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q_{G}) up to a constant with q_{G}\propto e^{-\beta G} : its physical walkers are unbiased. The second is the fully flattened level, whose auxiliary target is P_{G,0}=u . These definitions agree with the corresponding interior limits, renormalized at zero, under the additional moment conditions in the proof of proposition S2 . 
 Proposition 1 (VESPER objective and minimizer) . 
 Let c_{k}>0 with \sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}=1 , and define 
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VESPER}}[G]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}\,\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}_{k}}[G], 
 (9) 
 with components given by equation 2 , or by equation 8 if \hat{\beta}_{1}=0 or \hat{\beta}_{K}=\beta . Under the integrability assumptions of Section A.4 , \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VESPER}} is convex in G , invariant under addition of a constant, and has minimizer set \{G_{\star}+C:C\in\mathbb{R}\} . In a realizable model family, every minimizer therefore represents G_{\star} up to a constant. For a differentiable model G_{\theta} , 
 \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VESPER}}[G_{\theta}]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{G_{\theta},k}}\!\left[\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta}(s(x))\right]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{G_{\theta},k}}\!\left[\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta}(s)\right]\right\}. 
 (10) 
 Every level, including strongly flattened ones, thus trains the same model directly, without reweighting between levels. The weights c_{k} and exponents \alpha_{k} leave the target unchanged but set how errors are weighted: near the minimizer, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VESPER}}[G]-\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VESPER}}[G_{\star}]\approx\tfrac{\beta}{2}\sum_{k}c_{k}\,\mathrm{Var}_{\pi_{\alpha_{k}}}(G-G_{\star}) . 
 2.4 Coupled sampling and optimization 
 We estimate the expectations in equation 10 with two populations at each level, all sharing G_{\theta} ( Fig. 1 ). At level k , n_{k} physical walkers evolve under the biased potential U(x)-(1-\alpha_{k})G_{\theta}(s(x)) , and N_{k} auxiliary walkers evolve directly in CV space under the tempered potential \alpha_{k}G_{\theta}(s) : 
 \displaystyle m_{s}\ddot{s}_{t}^{i,k}+\gamma_{s}\dot{s}_{t}^{i,k} 
 \displaystyle=-\alpha_{k}\nabla_{s}G_{\theta_{t}}(s_{t}^{i,k})+\sqrt{2\gamma_{s}\beta^{-1}}\,\eta_{t}^{i,k}, 
 (11) 
 \displaystyle m_{x}\ddot{x}_{t}^{j,k}+\gamma_{x}\dot{x}_{t}^{j,k} 
 \displaystyle=-\nabla_{x}U(x_{t}^{j,k})+(1-\alpha_{k})\nabla_{x}G_{\theta_{t}}(s(x_{t}^{j,k}))+\sqrt{2\gamma_{x}\beta^{-1}}\,\tilde{\eta}_{t}^{j,k}, 
 where m_{s},m_{x} are masses, \gamma_{s},\gamma_{x} friction coefficients, and \eta_{t}^{i,k},\tilde{\eta}_{t}^{j,k} independent white noises. The bias force on the molecular system is \nabla_{x}G_{\theta}(s(x))=J_{s}(x)^{\top}\nabla_{s}G_{\theta}(s(x)) , with J_{s}=\partial s/\partial x\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times d} . For fixed \theta , these dynamics have configurational invariant laws P_{G_{\theta},k} and \nu_{G_{\theta},k} ( Section A.3 ), so their walker averages estimate the two expectations in equation 10 . The parameters follow the corresponding gradient flow with \gamma_{\theta}^{-1} the learning rate, 
 \gamma_{\theta}\dot{\theta}_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}\left[\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta_{t}}(s_{t}^{i,k})-\frac{1}{n_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}}\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta_{t}}(s(x_{t}^{j,k}))\right]. 
 (12) 
 Three features of this construction matter in practice. 
 • 
 Timescales. Since \theta moves, the walkers track moving targets, and the averages in equation 12 approximate the gradient when \theta evolves more slowly than the walkers relax. Section A.6 makes this quantitative for the overdamped dynamics: objective error and sampling lag decay together when the learning rate is below a threshold set by the log-Sobolev constants of the sampling laws and a bound on the parameter gradients, so no adiabatic limit is needed. 
 • 
 Cost. Auxiliary walkers live in the D -dimensional CV space and need one network evaluation per step, so N_{k} can be large ( 10^{4} - 10^{5} in the molecular runs) with cost dominated by the biased MD. 
 • 
 Persistence. Auxiliary walkers persist between parameter updates, as in persistent contrastive divergence [ Tieleman, 2008 ] , staying close to equilibrium in the slowly changing tempered model. 
 Section A.6 derives a conditional convergence bound for the coupled overdamped population dynamics without replica exchange. For 0 35 . Under assumptions (C1)-(C4), for 0 
 V_{t}\leq e^{-\kappa t}V_{0},\qquad\kappa=2\min\{\eta\mu,a-\eta B^{2}\}. 
 (13) 
 Thus objective error and sampling lag decay together. Under (C1)-(C3), the same learning-rate restriction gives the time-averaged stationarity bound 
 \frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla F(\theta_{t})\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{V_{0}}{\eta T}. 
 (14) 
 In the implementation, equation 12 is discretized: sampling epochs alternate with optimizer steps, and batches may be reused across several steps with within-batch importance weights ( Algorithm 1 , Section B.7 ). When equilibrium configurations from \varrho are available, the physical average can instead be computed from them with self-normalized weights ( Section A.9 ). 
 2.5 Replica exchange across tempering levels 
 The common-minimizer property does not require the levels to communicate. Sampling, however, benefits from it: strongly flattened levels cross barriers readily, while weakly flattened ones, including the physical level, may remain trapped. We therefore couple adjacent levels by replica exchange [ Sugita & Okamoto, 1999 , Affentranger et al., 2006 , Earl & Deem, 2005 ] , performed between parameter updates with G_{\theta} held fixed. For a symmetric swap proposal between levels i and j , the Metropolis acceptance probability for physical walkers is 
 A_{x}(x^{i},x^{j})=\min\!\left\{1,\exp\!\left[(\hat{\beta}_{i}-\hat{\beta}_{j})\bigl(G_{\theta}(s(x^{i}))-G_{\theta}(s(x^{j}))\bigr)\right]\right\}, 
 (15) 
 and A_{s}(s^{i},s^{j}) is identical with s(x^{i}) , s(x^{j}) replaced by s^{i} , s^{j} .
Each swap preserves the fixed-model target product law \prod_{k}\nu_{G_{\theta},k} or \prod_{k}P_{G_{\theta},k} , and hence the population-gradient identity ( Section A.5 ).
All physical replicas share the same potential U and thermostat temperature, so the microscopic energy and Maxwell factors cancel from the exchange ratio. Both acceptance ratios therefore depend only on the learned free-energy differences in CV space. Given the CV values, their evaluation requires only two model values and no additional microscopic-energy evaluation. The tempering-level spacing is controlled by fluctuations of G_{\theta} , which can depend on the system and choice of CVs. Physical and auxiliary swaps are proposed independently. 
 2.6 Related work 
 Adaptive-bias enhanced sampling. 
 Umbrella sampling, adaptive biasing force, metadynamics and its well-tempered variant, and on-the-fly probability enhanced sampling accelerate MD with restraining or adaptively built CV-dependent biases during the simulation [ Torrie & Valleau, 1977 , Darve & Pohorille, 2001 , Darve et al., 2008 , Laio & Parrinello, 2002 , Barducci et al., 2008 , Invernizzi & Parrinello, 2020 ] . Neural representations of the free energy or bias have been trained from mean forces, histograms, or both [ Schneider et al., 2017 , Sidky & Whitmer, 2018 , Guo et al., 2018 , Sevgen et al., 2020 , Zubieta Rico et al., 2025 ] . Reinforced dynamics couples such training to an uncertainty-gated bias [ Zhang et al., 2018 ] . VESPER couples biased MD and persistent auxiliary CV walkers through one physical free-energy model, with all tempering levels contributing to an objective with a common minimizer ( Proposition 1 ). Its closest variational connection is to variationally enhanced sampling (VES) [ Valsson & Parrinello, 2014 ] , in its well-tempered [ Valsson & Parrinello, 2015 ] and neural [ Bonati et al., 2019 ] forms. With bias V_{\theta}=-(1-\alpha)G_{\theta} and target t_{\theta}\propto e^{-\alpha\beta G_{\theta}} held fixed during differentiation, the VES gradient equals (1-\alpha)\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}}[G_{\theta}] ( Section D.1 ). VESPER gives this instantaneous update an explicit objective in the physical free energy, with the current model directly determining both the molecular bias and the auxiliary target, without a separate recursive free-energy estimate. Neural VES also evaluates target averages by numerical integration or Monte Carlo. Here, persistent auxiliary dynamics form part of the coupled learning system ( Section A.6 ). 
 Replica ladders with CV biases. 
 Replica exchange improves exploration by swapping configurations between replicas with different temperatures or Hamiltonians [ Sugita & Okamoto, 1999 , Affentranger et al., 2006 , Earl & Deem, 2005 ] . Bias-exchange metadynamics assigns different CVs to different replicas [ Piana & Laio, 2007 ] . Replica exchange with collective-variable tempering uses a ladder of well-tempered biases of increasing strength, which is the closest analogue of VESPER’s physical population [ Gil-Ley & Bussi, 2015 ] . VESPER uses the ladder for joint free-energy learning as well as configuration exchange: every level supplies its native gradient to one shared model, and the common-minimizer property allows these contributions to be combined without reweighting between levels ( Proposition 1 ). 
 Extended-variable methods. 
 Temperature-accelerated MD, driven adiabatic free-energy dynamics, and related schemes couple the CVs to hot auxiliary variables through a stiff spring. They recover the free energy in the limit of adiabatic separation between auxiliary and molecular motion [ Maragliano & Vanden-Eijnden, 2006 , Abrams & Tuckerman, 2008 , Rosso et al., 2002 , Chen et al., 2012 , Awasthi & Nair, 2017 ] , possibly followed by mean-force reconstruction [ Maragliano & Vanden-Eijnden, 2008 ] . VESPER identifies the physical free energy through its variational minimizer, without requiring an adiabatic limit between auxiliary and molecular coordinates. Its auxiliary walkers explore the tempered model and communicate with the molecular system through shared parameters, without a spring coupling between the two populations. 
 Energy-based models and tempered objectives. 
 VESPER connects tempered energy-based learning to molecular simulation through the prescribed CV map s(x) . Biased microscopic dynamics generate the tilted CV law, while persistent auxiliary walkers sample the tempered model directly in CV space [ Hinton, 2002 , Tieleman, 2008 ] . Both populations train the same physical free-energy model without a pre-existing equilibrium dataset ( Section A.3 ). With g=\beta G and \gamma=\alpha-1 , the single-level objective takes the form of the pseudo-spherical contrastive divergence (PS-CD) objective [ Yu et al., 2021 ] , built on the \gamma -cross-entropy [ Fujisawa & Eguchi, 2008 ] , continued to negative \gamma , and admits a Rényi variational representation [ Birrell et al., 2021 ] . At \alpha=1 it reduces to maximum likelihood. For 0 Appendix D ). PS-CD estimates its positive term from a fixed dataset and its tempered model term by importance weighting model samples [ Yu et al., 2021 ] . VESPER realizes both tilted laws through coupled dynamics, extending this variational principle to simultaneous molecular sampling and free-energy reconstruction ( Appendix D ). Parallel tempering has also been used to train restricted Boltzmann machines [ Desjardins et al., 2010 , Cho et al., 2010 ] , with only the untempered chain entering the gradient. In VESPER every level contributes. 
 Learned samplers. 
 Boltzmann generators and non-equilibrium transport samplers learn maps or flows that sample a known energy [ Noé et al., 2019 , Dibak et al., 2022 , Invernizzi et al., 2022 , Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden, 2025 ] , and annealed importance sampling and Jarzynski-type estimators weight paths between distributions [ Neal, 2001 , Jarzynski, 1997 , Carbone et al., 2023 ] . These methods target the full configurational distribution. VESPER instead targets a CV free energy through directly simulated tilted populations. 
 3 Numerical results 
 We evaluate VESPER on analytic landscapes and molecular systems to test free-energy reconstruction accuracy, convergence as the number of CVs increases, and exploration of conformational and chemical changes in complex MD systems. The analytic benchmarks provide exact references and controlled comparisons of tempering and replica exchange, while the molecular applications test the method in realistic MD. Complete simulation settings and numerical comparisons are given in the appendices. Molecular simulation times are per replica. Aggregate MD and population budgets are specified in Tables 2 and C.2 . 
 3.1 Analytic landscapes: tempering and increasing dimension 
 The Müller-Brown benchmark [ Müller & Brown, 1979 ] tests how tempering balances accuracy in low-energy basins with reconstruction over a broader region of the landscape, including high-free-energy regions of low probability mass ( Fig. 2 A-C). Moderate tempering gives the lowest basin-sensitive KL divergence, while stronger tempering gives the lowest full-domain RMSE. The fixed exchange ladder balances these objectives, reaching a KL divergence of 0.017\pm 0.013 while keeping its mean RMSE within 1\% of the lowest tested value. Here the RMSE includes extrapolation beyond the simulation box into a high-energy rim, so it measures broad-domain reconstruction rather than barrier accuracy alone ( Section C.1 ). 
 We then increase the dimension of a rotated four-well landscape from two to one hundred, keeping the hidden width, walker budget and update schedule fixed ( Fig. 2 D-F). The same tempering ladder remains competitive across the tested dimensions without selecting a different tempering strength for each problem. At one hundred dimensions, the exchange ladder gives the lowest mean normalized RMSE among the primary comparisons: 0.32\pm 0.21 , compared with 0.42\pm 0.05 for the best tested single level. These results support using a shared ladder across dimensions, with its strongest advantages appearing in the more demanding exploration problem. The landscape, averaging conventions and larger-population controls are specified in Section C.2 .

 3.2 Alanine dipeptide: comparison with established methods 
 Alanine dipeptide (ADP) in explicit water tests whether VESPER can reconstruct a molecular free-energy surface with accuracy and convergence comparable to established enhanced-sampling methods. With the two backbone dihedrals (\phi,\psi) as CVs, tempered VESPER explores the periodic landscape and recovers the reference surface [ Zubieta Rico et al., 2025 ] , while the unbiased control remains concentrated in its initial basin ( Fig. 3 ). At 10 ns per replica, the exchange ladder reaches an RMSE of 2.02\pm 0.24 kJ mol -1 , comparable to 2.04\pm 0.51 kJ mol -1 for SIREN-based combined force-frequency sampling (S-CFF), the strongest tested baseline at that horizon. A single tempered level also performs well. The ladders make only a small change to the endpoint error on this relatively simple low-dimensional landscape. 
 The VESPER mean error curves first fall below 1 kcal mol -1 and remain there through 10 ns at approximately 2.0 ns for a single level and 1.8 - 1.9 ns for the ladders. Metadynamics and S-CFF reach this threshold earlier, at 1.3 - 1.4 ns. VESPER therefore combines comparable late-time accuracy with rapid practical convergence, within about 2 ns, on this benchmark. Full baseline comparisons and scoring conventions are given in Sections B.1 , B.9 and 3 .

 3.3 Alanine tetrapeptide: consistency as the number of CVs increases 
 Alanine tetrapeptide (ATP) tests whether VESPER retains a consistent molecular description when the number of biased backbone dihedrals increases from three to six. The two models differ only in their input layer, and their two-dimensional Boltzmann marginals retain similar broad structure ( Fig. 4 ). Both six-CV ladder variants also produce more consistent learned shapes between 5 and 10 ns than the selected single-level model: the correlations with their own 10 ns estimates are 0.913\pm 0.012 with exchange and 0.941\pm 0.028 without, compared with 0.804\pm 0.179 for the single level. This suggests that multiple learning levels can stabilize reconstruction over the observed interval in the higher-dimensional six-CV model. Without an independent converged six-CV reference, these comparisons establish dimensional and temporal consistency.

 3.4 Catalytic methane dehydrogenation on Ni(511) 
 We next test VESPER on a reactive free-energy landscape described by a machine-learned interatomic potential. For methane on Ni(511) at 700 K, a single tempered level learns a joint surface over the C-Ni and C-H coordination numbers and the carbon atom’s lateral position. Its two-CV Boltzmann marginals recover the physisorbed and dissociated basins and the transition region of the lower-dimensional reference [ Jin et al., 2025 ] ( Fig. 5 ). 
 The differentiable neural representation also supports post hoc analysis of the joint three-CV landscape using its gradients. A string-method path calculation [ E et al., 2007 ] , followed by constrained gradient-flow refinement, connects reactant and product through a fitted saddle. The bounded reactant-to-saddle free-energy difference is 1.12\pm 0.25 eV, consistent with the reference. Including the lateral position directly in the model locates the fitted saddle near the step-edge atoms. Whereas Jin et al. [2025] obtained an additional spatial free-energy description through post hoc Spectral-ABF analysis of existing trajectories, VESPER learns the joint three-CV surface during sampling. The route, averaging and projection conventions are specified in Section B.5 .

 3.5 Chignolin: exploration with seventeen CVs 
 Finally, we test whether one shared neural free energy can support exploration in the high-dimensional space of chignolin’s seventeen backbone dihedrals. For CLN025 in explicit water, a twelve-level ladder continuously trains the joint model during replica exchange, with every level contributing to learning ( Fig. 6 ). The representation is not restricted to a sum of pairwise terms, and the target is not prescribed as a folded/unfolded mixture, extending earlier work on this system [ Shaffer et al., 2016 ] . 
 Starting from folded configurations, VESPER explores RMSD-defined folded and unfolded regions. Exploration broadens along the ladder while the unbiased level remains predominantly folded. Auxiliary-sample projections place the free-energy minimum in the folded region and represent a broader range of conformations also visited by MD. VESPER thus learns a joint free energy while exploring conformational changes with seventeen CVs.

 4 Discussion and conclusion 
 We introduced VESPER, which casts free-energy learning and enhanced sampling as a single optimization problem. The free energy is parameterized by a neural network G_{\theta} that biases the MD, drives tempered auxiliary walkers in CV space, and is trained on the difference between its mean parameter gradients over the auxiliary walkers and the biased MD configurations. This difference estimates the gradient of one variational objective, so the simulation generates its own training data as the model improves. Working in an extended space of molecular configurations and auxiliary walkers, VESPER minimizes an explicit objective whose minimizer is the physical free energy itself. The free energy is therefore learned directly, rather than recovered from a bias optimized toward a fixed or recursively updated target, and no adiabatic separation between auxiliary and molecular coordinates is required. Because the objective is convex and every tempering level shares the same global minimizer, all levels train one model without reweighting between them. Strongly flattened levels that cross barriers readily therefore work alongside weakly tempered levels, and when the ladder includes the physical level, \alpha=1 , the ladder objective bounds the KL divergence of the model from the true CV distribution. Replica exchange moves configurations along the ladder using only learned free-energy differences in CV space, with no additional evaluations of the microscopic potential. The coupled sampling and learning dynamics also carry a conditional convergence guarantee. 
 VESPER is effective across a wide range of problems. The analytic landscapes provide exact references for controlled tests. On the Müller-Brown potential, the exchange ladder balances accuracy in the low-energy basins with reconstruction of the broader landscape. On a four-well landscape whose dimension grows from two to one hundred, one fixed ladder stays competitive without retuning and shows its clearest advantage at one hundred dimensions. Alanine dipeptide is a standard molecular benchmark with a reference surface for direct comparison. Here VESPER matches or improves on the accuracy of established enhanced-sampling methods, reaching the end-point error of the best tested baseline, and converges within 2 ns. Alanine tetrapeptide tests whether the learned description stays consistent as more CVs are biased, and the Boltzmann marginals learned with three and six CVs agree. Methane dehydrogenation on Ni(511) extends the tests from conformational to chemical change, using a machine-learned interatomic potential. VESPER learns a reactive three-CV landscape whose two-CV marginals recover the basins and transition region of the reference. Because the model is a differentiable neural network, a string-method calculation with gradient-flow refinement then maps the reaction path directly on the joint three-CV surface learned during sampling. Chignolin tests exploration in a realistic high-dimensional CV space. While biasing seventeen backbone dihedrals, one shared model drives repeated unfolding and refolding. These results connect online free-energy learning to enhanced sampling across conformational and chemical changes. Molecular comparisons retain their per-replica cost: a K -level ladder uses K times the aggregate MD of a single replica at the same horizon. 
 VESPER directly accelerates barrier crossing along the chosen CVs. Orthogonal slow motions can still limit exploration by the physical walkers. Our convergence analysis assumes overdamped dynamics and a Polyak-Łojasiewicz condition and excludes exchange, batch reuse and practical optimizers. The high-dimensional molecular results establish self-consistency and exploration without converged free-energy references. The stability controls for batch reuse ( Section B.7 ) add per-system hyperparameters. Natural extensions are joint CV learning, adaptive level weights, and combining online sampling with existing trajectories ( Section A.9 ).

 We retain the notation of the main text, with G in energy units and \alpha=\hat{\beta}/\beta . When considering a single tempering level, we write P_{G} , \nu_{G} , and Q_{G} for P_{G,\hat{\beta}} , \nu_{G,\hat{\beta}} , and Q_{G,\hat{\beta}} , respectively. 
 A.1 Convexity and the physical free-energy minimizer 
 Let p be the physical CV density in equation 1 . We work on its support \mathcal{S} , where p>0 almost everywhere, and for the chosen 0 
 C_{\alpha}=\int_{\mathcal{S}}p(s)^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}s 
 (16) 
 We consider finite-valued measurable free energies G for which the two normalizers 
 A_{G}=\int_{\mathcal{S}}e^{-\hat{\beta}G(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s,\qquad B_{G}=\int_{\mathcal{S}}p(s)e^{(\beta-\hat{\beta})G(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s 
 (17) 
 are finite and positive. 
 The auxiliary density, biased microscopic density and its CV marginal are 
 P_{G}(s)=\frac{e^{-\hat{\beta}G(s)}}{A_{G}},\qquad\nu_{G}(x)=\frac{e^{(\beta-\hat{\beta})G(s(x))}\varrho(x)}{B_{G}}, 
 (18) 
 Q_{G}(s)=\frac{p(s)e^{(\beta-\hat{\beta})G(s)}}{B_{G}}. 
 (19) 
 Proposition S1 (Convexity and the free-energy minimizer) . 
 The functional in equation 2 is convex in G , invariant under addition of a constant, and has minimizer set \{G_{\star}+C\} . Its minimum is 
 \mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}}[G_{\star}]=\frac{\log C_{\alpha}}{\beta\alpha(1-\alpha)}. 
 (20) 
 At this minimum, P_{G_{\star}}=Q_{G_{\star}}=p^{\alpha}/C_{\alpha} . 
 Proof. 
 Each term of equation 2 is convex by Hölder’s inequality. Adding a constant c to G changes the two terms by -c and c , so their sum is unchanged. To identify the minimizer, differentiate in a bounded direction h : 
 \displaystyle D\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}}[G](h) 
 \displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Q_{G}}h-\mathbb{E}_{P_{G}}h, 
 (21) 
 \displaystyle D^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}}[G](h,h) 
 \displaystyle=\hat{\beta}\operatorname{Var}_{P_{G}}(h)+(\beta-\hat{\beta})\operatorname{Var}_{Q_{G}}(h)\geq 0. 
 (22) 
 The first variation vanishes for every such h exactly when P_{G}=Q_{G} . Using equation 18 and equation 19 , this condition becomes 
 \frac{e^{-\hat{\beta}G(s)}}{A_{G}}=\frac{p(s)e^{(\beta-\hat{\beta})G(s)}}{B_{G}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad G(s)=-\beta^{-1}\log p(s)+C. 
 To see that this candidate attains the minimum, apply Hölder’s inequality once more: 
 C_{\alpha}=\int_{\mathcal{S}}(e^{-\hat{\beta}G})^{1-\alpha}(pe^{(\beta-\hat{\beta})G})^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq A_{G}^{1-\alpha}B_{G}^{\alpha}. 
 Taking logarithms gives the lower bound in equation 20 . Equality holds precisely when the two normalized factors coincide, hence precisely at G=G_{\star}+C . This candidate is admissible by equation 16 , and substitution gives P_{G_{\star}}=Q_{G_{\star}}=p^{\alpha}/C_{\alpha} . Finally, applying the chain rule to equation 21 gives the parameter gradient in equation 7 , provided differentiation under the integrals is justified.
∎

 A.3 Dual formulation and coupled dynamics 
 For a probability density \rho on \mathcal{S} , define its entropy relative to the CV reference measure by
 \operatorname{Ent}_{\mathrm{d}s}(\rho)=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\rho\log\rho\,\mathrm{d}s .
Let \nu be a probability law on configuration space. Define 
 \mathcal{J}(G,\rho,\nu)=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[G\circ s]-\mathbb{E}_{\rho}G-\frac{\operatorname{Ent}_{\mathrm{d}s}(\rho)}{\hat{\beta}}-\frac{\operatorname{KL}(\nu\|\varrho)}{\beta-\hat{\beta}}. 
 (27) 
 Proposition S3 (Dual formulation and stationary sampling laws) . 
 For 0 
 \mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}}[G]=\sup_{\rho,\nu}\mathcal{J}(G,\rho,\nu),\qquad\min_{G}\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}}[G]=\min_{G}\sup_{\rho,\nu}\mathcal{J}(G,\rho,\nu). 
 (28) 
 The maximizing laws are P_{G} and \nu_{G} , and the pushforward of \nu_{G} is Q_{G} . Suppose the potentials and CV map are smooth, the Langevin equations are well posed, and the boundaries are periodic, reflecting, or confining with sufficient decay. The fixed-model dynamics in equation 11 then preserve the configurational laws P_{G} and \nu_{G} . 
 Proof. 
 The dual identity follows by expanding the relative entropies: 
 \mathcal{J}(G,\rho,\nu)=\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}}[G]-\frac{\operatorname{KL}(\rho\|P_{G})}{\hat{\beta}}-\frac{\operatorname{KL}(\nu\|\nu_{G})}{\beta-\hat{\beta}}. 
 (29) 
 The two KL terms are nonnegative and vanish at \rho=P_{G} and \nu=\nu_{G} , proving equation 28 . The expansion is immediate when all terms are finite, and the Gibbs variational formula gives the same supremum otherwise. 
 For fixed \rho and \nu , differentiating \mathcal{J} with respect to \theta , under the integrals, gives
 \mathbb{E}_{\nu}\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta}\circ s-\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta} .
At the maximizing laws this is \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}}[G_{\theta}] . Taking the negative derivative at each level, weighting by c_{k} , and replacing the expectations by walker averages gives the parameter update in equation 12 . Meanwhile, Wasserstein ascent in \rho and \nu , with mobilities \alpha/\gamma_{s} and (1-\alpha)/\gamma_{x} , gives 
 \displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho 
 \displaystyle=\gamma_{s}^{-1}\nabla_{s}\cdot(\alpha\rho\nabla_{s}G+\beta^{-1}\nabla_{s}\rho), 
 (30) 
 \displaystyle\partial_{t}\nu 
 \displaystyle=\gamma_{x}^{-1}\nabla_{x}\cdot\bigl(\nu\nabla_{x}[U-(1-\alpha)G\circ s]+\beta^{-1}\nabla_{x}\nu\bigr). 
 (31) 
 Their particle realizations are the overdamped sampling equations. Adding momenta and Langevin thermostats yields the inertial realization in equation 11 with the same configurational targets. 
 The CV marginal of \nu_{G} follows from the definition of p : for any bounded test function h , 
 \int_{\mathcal{X}}h(s(x))\nu_{G}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{B_{G}}\int_{\mathcal{S}}h(s)p(s)e^{(\beta-\hat{\beta})G(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s. 
 Thus s_{\#}\nu_{G}=Q_{G} . To check invariance, note that the potentials \alpha G and U-(1-\alpha)G\circ s at inverse temperature \beta have Gibbs factors e^{-\hat{\beta}G} and e^{-\beta U+(\beta-\hat{\beta})G\circ s} . These make the probability currents in equation 30 and equation 31 vanish. For inertial dynamics, the corresponding phase-space laws include Maxwell momentum factors: Hamiltonian transport preserves energy and phase volume, while friction and noise preserve the Maxwell factors. Under ergodicity, time averages therefore recover the required expectations. 
 The sampling laws also hold at \hat{\beta}=\beta , although the dual functional in equation 27 is singular there. The auxiliary potential is then G , with invariant density P_{G,\beta}\propto e^{-\beta G} , and the unbiased physical dynamics preserve \varrho .
∎ 
 A.4 The common-minimizer ladder 
 Proposition S4 (Weighted excess loss and level gradients) . 
 Assume proposition S1 at every interior level and the moment and normalizer conditions of proposition S2 at an endpoint level. For c_{k}>0 with \sum_{k}c_{k}=1 , the ladder functional is convex and its minimizer set is \{G_{\star}+C\} . Its parameter gradient is given by equation 10 . These statements imply Proposition 1 . 
 Proof. 
 For each level, propositions S1 and S2 give 
 \Delta_{k}[G]:=\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}_{k}}[G]-\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}_{k}}[G_{\star}]\geq 0,\qquad\Delta_{k}[G]=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ G=G_{\star}+C. 
 This includes both endpoints by equation 25 and equation 26 . The excess of the combined objective is \sum_{k}c_{k}\Delta_{k}[G] . Since every c_{k} is positive, this sum vanishes exactly when every component vanishes, proving the common-minimizer claim. Each component is convex and invariant under constant shifts, so the weighted sum has both properties as well. At either endpoint, these properties follow directly from equation 8 : the log-partition term is convex, the expectation is linear, and their shifts cancel. If the model family contains G_{\star}+C , it attains the same minimum. 
 Finally, differentiating the finite sum gives 
 \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VESPER}}[G_{\theta}]=\sum_{k}c_{k}\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}_{k}}[G_{\theta}]. 
 Substitution of equation 7 proves equation 10 . At the physical endpoint, differentiating equation 8 gives \mathbb{E}_{p}\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta}-\mathbb{E}_{P_{G_{\theta},\beta}}\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta} , assuming differentiation under the integral is justified. This has the same form, since \nu_{G,K}=\varrho and s_{\#}\varrho=p . At the zero endpoint the gradient is \mathbb{E}_{Q_{G_{\theta},0}}\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta}-\mathbb{E}_{u}\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta} , again assuming justified differentiation, with P_{G_{\theta},0}=u and s_{\#}\nu_{G_{\theta},0}=Q_{G_{\theta},0} . Thus each level contributes expectations under its own sampling laws, with coefficient c_{k} .
∎ 
 A.5 Replica-exchange acceptance 
 Proposition S5 (Exchange preserves the level product laws) . 
 Fix G for which all level distributions are normalizable, and consider replicas of the same physical system at inverse temperature \beta , with auxiliary inverse temperatures \hat{\beta}_{k} . A symmetric proposal to swap two physical configurations, accepted with A_{x} in equation 15 , is reversible with respect to \prod_{k}\nu_{G,k} . The analogous auxiliary swap with acceptance A_{s} is reversible with respect to \prod_{k}P_{G,k} . 
 Proof. 
 For an auxiliary swap between levels i and j , the ratio of the swapped and original product densities is 
 \frac{P_{G,i}(s^{j})P_{G,j}(s^{i})}{P_{G,i}(s^{i})P_{G,j}(s^{j})}=\exp\!\left[(\hat{\beta}_{i}-\hat{\beta}_{j})(G(s^{i})-G(s^{j}))\right]. 
 For a physical swap, write G_{i}=G(s(x^{i})) and G_{j}=G(s(x^{j})) . The common physical factors \varrho(x^{i})\varrho(x^{j}) and the normalizers cancel, leaving 
 \displaystyle\log\frac{\nu_{G,i}(x^{j})\nu_{G,j}(x^{i})}{\nu_{G,i}(x^{i})\nu_{G,j}(x^{j})} 
 \displaystyle=(\beta-\hat{\beta}_{i})G_{j}+(\beta-\hat{\beta}_{j})G_{i} 
 \displaystyle\quad-(\beta-\hat{\beta}_{i})G_{i}-(\beta-\hat{\beta}_{j})G_{j} 
 \displaystyle=(\hat{\beta}_{i}-\hat{\beta}_{j})(G_{i}-G_{j}). 
 Thus the two log ratios are 
 \ell_{s}=(\hat{\beta}_{i}-\hat{\beta}_{j})[G(s^{i})-G(s^{j})],\qquad\ell_{x}=(\hat{\beta}_{i}-\hat{\beta}_{j})[G_{i}-G_{j}]. 
 (32) 
 In either case, let T denote the swap and \Pi the product density. Since T^{2}z=z , the Metropolis acceptance A(z)=\min\{1,\Pi(Tz)/\Pi(z)\} satisfies
 \Pi(z)A(z)=\min\{\Pi(z),\Pi(Tz)\}=\Pi(Tz)A(Tz) .
Together with the symmetric proposal, this proves detailed balance. With equal masses and a common thermostat, the Maxwell factors also cancel if momenta are exchanged. Leaving momenta in place preserves the same independent Maxwell law. Composing pair-exchange kernels therefore preserves \Pi . A joint symmetric swap of both populations instead uses the sum \ell_{x}+\ell_{s} as its log acceptance ratio.
∎ 
 A.6 Convergence of the coupled population dynamics 
 We now consider the overdamped population dynamics with continuous parameter descent and no replica exchange. The aim is to control the objective error together with the lag of the sampling distributions behind their changing targets. 
 First suppose that all levels satisfy 0 0 and \sum_{k}c_{k}=1 . We treat the physical endpoint below. It is convenient to use dimensionless quantities, 
 g_{\theta}=\beta G_{\theta},\qquad F(\theta)=\beta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VESPER}}[G_{\theta}],\qquad f_{\theta}(s)=\nabla_{\theta}g_{\theta}(s), 
 so the instantaneous targets are 
 P_{k}^{\theta}(s)=\frac{e^{-\alpha_{k}g_{\theta}(s)}}{A_{k}(\theta)},\qquad\nu_{k}^{\theta}(x)=\frac{\varrho(x)e^{(1-\alpha_{k})g_{\theta}(s(x))}}{B_{k}(\theta)}. 
 (33) 
 Let \rho_{k,t} and \nu_{k,t} denote the evolving auxiliary and physical laws. Suppressing the time subscript inside expectations, their equations are 
 \displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{k} 
 \displaystyle=d_{s,k}\nabla_{s}\cdot\left(\rho_{k}\nabla_{s}\log\frac{\rho_{k}}{P_{k}^{\theta_{t}}}\right), 
 \displaystyle d_{s,k} 
 \displaystyle=(\beta\gamma_{s,k})^{-1}, 
 (34) 
 \displaystyle\partial_{t}\nu_{k} 
 \displaystyle=d_{x,k}\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(\nu_{k}\nabla_{x}\log\frac{\nu_{k}}{\nu_{k}^{\theta_{t}}}\right), 
 \displaystyle d_{x,k} 
 \displaystyle=(\beta\gamma_{x,k})^{-1}, 
 \displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{t} 
 \displaystyle=-\eta\sum_{k}c_{k}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{k}}[f_{\theta_{t}}\circ s]-\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{k}}f_{\theta_{t}}\right). 
 Here \eta=(\beta\gamma_{\theta})^{-1} matches the normalization in equation 12 . The physical drift is available directly from
 \nabla_{x}\log\nu_{k}^{\theta}=-\beta\nabla_{x}U+(1-\alpha_{k})\nabla_{x}(g_{\theta}\circ s) . 
 Measure the sampling lag by 
 H_{t}=\sum_{k}c_{k}\left\{\frac{\operatorname{KL}(\rho_{k,t}\|P_{k}^{\theta_{t}})}{\alpha_{k}}+\frac{\operatorname{KL}(\nu_{k,t}\|\nu_{k}^{\theta_{t}})}{1-\alpha_{k}}\right\}. 
 (35) 
 These weights will cancel the tempering factors when we differentiate the relative entropies. 
 Let F_{*}=\min_{\theta}F(\theta) be finite and attained. We make the following assumptions on an open neighborhood \mathcal{O} of the initial sublevel \{\theta:F(\theta)\leq F(\theta_{0})+H_{0}\} : 
 (C1) 
 The normalizers are finite, g_{\theta} is differentiable in \theta and smooth enough in space for equation 34 , and
 \sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{O},s}\|f_{\theta}(s)\|\leq B 
 (C2) 
 The evolution is well posed from initial laws with finite relative entropy. Entropy differentiation and integration by parts are valid, with periodic or zero-flux boundaries, or sufficient decay on unbounded domains. 
 (C3) 
 The instantaneous laws satisfy uniform log-Sobolev inequalities on their respective spaces: 
 I(\zeta\|P_{k}^{\theta})\geq 2\lambda_{s,k}\operatorname{KL}(\zeta\|P_{k}^{\theta}),\qquad I(\zeta\|\nu_{k}^{\theta})\geq 2\lambda_{x,k}\operatorname{KL}(\zeta\|\nu_{k}^{\theta}), 
 where I(\zeta\|\pi)=\int\|\nabla\log(\zeta/\pi)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta .
With constants positive and uniform on \mathcal{O} , set
 a=\min_{k}\{d_{s,k}\lambda_{s,k},d_{x,k}\lambda_{x,k}\}>0 . 
 (C4) 
 The objective satisfies the Polyak-Łojasiewicz inequality
 \|\nabla F(\theta)\|^{2}\geq 2\mu(F(\theta)-F_{*}) 
on \mathcal{O} , for some \mu>0 . 
 In (C3), the physical inequality is required for the full microscopic law. Uniform constants follow, for example, from the Holley-Stroock perturbation lemma when the model gives a uniformly bounded tilt of a reference law satisfying a log-Sobolev inequality [ Holley & Stroock, 1987 ] . 
 Theorem S6 (Convergence of the two-population ladder) . 
 Under (C1)-(C4), for 0 34 obeys 
 F(\theta_{t})-F_{*}+H_{t}\leq e^{-\kappa t}\bigl[F(\theta_{0})-F_{*}+H_{0}\bigr],\qquad\kappa=2\min\{\eta\mu,a-\eta B^{2}\}. 
 (36) 
 Thus the objective error and the weighted sampling lag decay together. 
 Proof. 
 Let e denote the error in the population gradient, and write 
 \displaystyle H_{s,k} 
 \displaystyle=\operatorname{KL}(\rho_{k}\|P_{k}^{\theta_{t}}),\qquad H_{x,k}=\operatorname{KL}(\nu_{k}\|\nu_{k}^{\theta_{t}}), 
 \displaystyle e_{k} 
 \displaystyle=(\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{k}}-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{k}^{\theta_{t}}})[f_{\theta_{t}}\circ s]-(\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{k}}-\mathbb{E}_{P_{k}^{\theta_{t}}})f_{\theta_{t}},\qquad e=\sum_{k}c_{k}e_{k}. 
 By equation 10 , the parameter equation is
 \dot{\theta}=-\eta(\nabla F+e) . 
 To differentiate H , first note that normalization of the target densities gives 
 \nabla_{\theta}\log P_{k}^{\theta}=-\alpha_{k}(f_{\theta}-\mathbb{E}_{P_{k}^{\theta}}f_{\theta}),\qquad\nabla_{\theta}\log\nu_{k}^{\theta}=(1-\alpha_{k})(f_{\theta}\circ s-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{k}^{\theta}}[f_{\theta}\circ s]). 
 Integration by parts in equation 34 gives 
 \displaystyle\dot{H}_{s,k} 
 \displaystyle=-d_{s,k}I(\rho_{k}\|P_{k}^{\theta_{t}})+\alpha_{k}\dot{\theta}^{\top}(\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{k}}-\mathbb{E}_{P_{k}^{\theta_{t}}})f_{\theta_{t}}, 
 (37) 
 \displaystyle\dot{H}_{x,k} 
 \displaystyle=-d_{x,k}I(\nu_{k}\|\nu_{k}^{\theta_{t}})-(1-\alpha_{k})\dot{\theta}^{\top}(\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{k}}-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{k}^{\theta_{t}}})[f_{\theta_{t}}\circ s]. 
 (38) 
 Multiplying by c_{k}/\alpha_{k} and c_{k}/(1-\alpha_{k}) , respectively, and summing over levels combines the moving-target terms into -\dot{\theta}^{\top}e . The log-Sobolev inequalities in (C3) therefore give 
 \dot{H}\leq-2aH-\dot{\theta}^{\top}e. 
 (39) 
 The gradient error is controlled by the same entropies. For a vector-valued function f with \|f\|\leq B , applying Pinsker’s inequality to unit scalar projections gives
 \|(\mathbb{E}_{\zeta}-\mathbb{E}_{\pi})f\|\leq\sqrt{2}B\sqrt{\operatorname{KL}(\zeta\|\pi)} .
Applying this to both populations and then using weighted Cauchy-Schwarz yields 
 \displaystyle\|e\|^{2} 
 \displaystyle\leq 2B^{2}\left[\sum_{k}c_{k}(\sqrt{H_{s,k}}+\sqrt{H_{x,k}})\right]^{2} 
 \displaystyle\leq 2B^{2}\left[\sum_{k}c_{k}\{\alpha_{k}+(1-\alpha_{k})\}\right]\left[\sum_{k}c_{k}\left\{\frac{H_{s,k}}{\alpha_{k}}+\frac{H_{x,k}}{1-\alpha_{k}}\right\}\right]=2B^{2}H. 
 (40) 
 Now let V=F-F_{*}+H . Combining equation 39 with the parameter equation cancels the mixed gradient terms: 
 \displaystyle\dot{V} 
 \displaystyle\leq(\nabla F-e)^{\top}\dot{\theta}-2aH 
 \displaystyle=-\eta(\nabla F-e)^{\top}(\nabla F+e)-2aH 
 \displaystyle=-\eta\|\nabla F\|^{2}+\eta\|e\|^{2}-2aH 
 \displaystyle\leq-\eta\|\nabla F\|^{2}-2(a-\eta B^{2})H\leq-\kappa V. 
 The last inequality uses (C4), and Gronwall’s inequality gives equation 36 . This estimate also gives F(\theta_{t})\leq F_{*}+V_{t}\leq F(\theta_{0})+H_{0} . Applying the argument up to a possible first exit from \mathcal{O} therefore rules out such an exit and proves the estimate for all time.
∎ 
 The physical endpoint. 
 The same argument covers a ladder with \alpha_{K}=1 , where the last physical replica has the fixed target \nu_{K}^{\theta}=\varrho . The gradient formula in equation 10 still holds under the endpoint moment conditions of proposition S2 , as shown in proposition S4 . To adapt the entropy H , keep the terms for k 
 c_{K}\{\operatorname{KL}(\rho_{K}\|P_{K}^{\theta})+w\operatorname{KL}(\nu_{K}\|\varrho)\},\qquad w>0. 
 Write H^{\prime} for this modified entropy and use (C1)-(C4) on an open neighborhood of \{\theta:F(\theta)\leq F(\theta_{0})+H^{\prime}_{0}\} , with the physical log-Sobolev inequality at level K imposed on \varrho . 
 Since \varrho does not depend on \theta , its relative entropy has no moving-target term. Thus, with
 e_{x,K}=(\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{K}}-\mathbb{E}_{\varrho})[f_{\theta}\circ s] , the entropy estimate becomes 
 \dot{H}^{\prime}\leq-2aH^{\prime}-\dot{\theta}^{\top}(e-c_{K}e_{x,K}). 
 Pinsker’s inequality and the same weighted Cauchy-Schwarz estimate give 
 \|e\|^{2}\leq 2B^{2}(1+c_{K}/w)H^{\prime},\qquad c_{K}^{2}\|e_{x,K}\|^{2}\leq 2B^{2}(c_{K}/w)H^{\prime}. 
 For V^{\prime}=F-F_{*}+H^{\prime} , the mixed terms therefore cancel as before, except for the contribution from the last physical replica: 
 \displaystyle\dot{V}^{\prime} 
 \displaystyle\leq-\eta\|\nabla F\|^{2}+\eta\|e\|^{2}-\eta c_{K}(\nabla F+e)^{\top}e_{x,K}-2aH^{\prime} 
 \displaystyle\leq-\tfrac{\eta}{2}\|\nabla F\|^{2}-\bigl[2a-\eta B^{2}(3+5c_{K}/w)\bigr]H^{\prime}. 
 Here we used Young’s inequality in the form
 |c_{K}(\nabla F+e)^{\top}e_{x,K}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla F\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|e\|^{2}+c_{K}^{2}\|e_{x,K}\|^{2} .
For 0 
 V^{\prime}_{t}\leq e^{-\kappa^{\prime}t}V^{\prime}_{0},\qquad\kappa^{\prime}=\min\{\eta\mu,\,2a-\eta B^{2}(3+5c_{K}/w)\}. 
 As in the proof above, F(\theta_{t})\leq F(\theta_{0})+H^{\prime}_{0} keeps the trajectory in the region where the assumptions hold. Without (C4), integrating the bound on \dot{V}^{\prime} gives the time-averaged estimate in equation 41 , with 2V^{\prime}_{0}/(\eta T) on the right. 
 Corollary S7 (Time-averaged stationarity) . 
 Under (C1)-(C3) and 0 0 , 
 \frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla F(\theta_{t})\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{F(\theta_{0})-F_{*}+H_{0}}{\eta T}. 
 (41) 
 Proof. 
 The proof above gives \dot{V}\leq-\eta\|\nabla F\|^{2}-2(a-\eta B^{2})H before invoking (C4). Integrating over [0,T] and using V_{T}\geq 0 proves the bound.
∎ 
 A.7 General reference measures 
 The same variational argument applies to a general reference measure. Let r be sigma-finite and let the probability measure p have density f=\mathrm{d}p/\mathrm{d}r>0 r -almost everywhere. For r=\mathrm{d}s , this f is the physical CV density p(s) . Writing g=\beta G , define 
 \ell_{\alpha}[g]=\frac{1}{\alpha}\log\int e^{-\alpha g}\,\mathrm{d}r+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\int e^{(1-\alpha)g}\,\mathrm{d}p. 
 (42) 
 For the physical Lebesgue reference, \ell_{\alpha}[\beta G]=\beta\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}}[G] . When Z_{g}=\int e^{-g}\,\mathrm{d}r 
 q_{g}(\mathrm{d}y)=Z_{g}^{-1}e^{-g(y)}r(\mathrm{d}y). 
 (43) 
 Proposition S8 (Reference-measure minimizer) . 
 For 0 42 , restricted to g for which both integrals are finite, is minimized exactly at g_{\star}=-\log f+C . At the minimum, the two tilted laws coincide with f^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}r/M(\alpha) . 
 Proof. 
 As in the proof of proposition S1 , Hölder’s inequality gives 
 M(\alpha)=\int(e^{-\alpha g})^{1-\alpha}(fe^{(1-\alpha)g})^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}r\leq\left(\int e^{-\alpha g}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)^{1-\alpha}\left(\int e^{(1-\alpha)g}\,\mathrm{d}p\right)^{\alpha}. 
 Equality requires the two factors to be proportional, which is equivalent to g=-\log f+C . At this choice, both normalized densities relative to r equal f^{\alpha}/M(\alpha) .
∎ 
 A.8 Positive-weight effective sample size 
 Proposition S9 (Population normalized ESS) . 
 At the population optimum, the fixed-data weights are proportional to f^{\alpha-1} . If M(2\alpha-1) 
 \frac{(\mathbb{E}_{p}w)^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{p}w^{2}}=\frac{M(\alpha)^{2}}{M(2\alpha-1)}. 
 (44) 
 For independent y_{j}\sim p with w_{j}=w(y_{j}) , the empirical quantity (\sum_{j}w_{j})^{2}/[n\sum_{j}w_{j}^{2}] converges to this value almost surely. At \alpha=1/2 , the denominator is M(0)=r(\operatorname{supp}p) . 
 Proof. 
 The scale of the weights cancels in the ratio, so take w=f^{\alpha-1} . Since \mathrm{d}p=f\mathrm{d}r , we have \mathbb{E}_{p}w=\int f^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}r=M(\alpha) and \mathbb{E}_{p}w^{2}=\int f^{2\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}r=M(2\alpha-1) , proving equation 44 . The assumed second moment allows the strong law of large numbers to be applied to both empirical moments, giving the almost-sure limit.
∎ 
 A.9 Learning from existing data 
 When equilibrium configurations x_{j}^{*}\sim\varrho are already available, their CV values y_{j}=s(x_{j}^{*}) can replace the physical walkers. The physical expectation in equation 7 is then estimated using normalized importance weights 
 \bar{w}_{j}(\theta)=\frac{e^{(\beta-\hat{\beta})G_{\theta}(y_{j})}}{\sum_{\ell}e^{(\beta-\hat{\beta})G_{\theta}(y_{\ell})}}. 
 (45) 
 This leads to the following weighted-data consistency result. 
 Proposition S10 (Weighted-data gradient) . 
 Fix \theta and let x_{j}^{*} be independent samples from \varrho , with y_{j}=s(x_{j}^{*}) . Suppose B_{G_{\theta}} 45 satisfy 
 \sum_{j=1}^{n}\bar{w}_{j}(\theta)\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta}(y_{j})\longrightarrow\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{G_{\theta}}}\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta}\circ s\quad\text{almost surely as }n\to\infty. 
 (46) 
 Moreover, the corresponding weighted population objective has minimizer G_{\star}+C . 
 Proof. 
 Using the CV marginal in equation 19 , the required expectation can be written as 
 \mathbb{E}_{\nu_{G_{\theta}}}\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta}\circ s=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{p}[e^{(\beta-\hat{\beta})G_{\theta}}\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta}]}{\mathbb{E}_{p}[e^{(\beta-\hat{\beta})G_{\theta}}]}. 
 The assumed moments allow the strong law of large numbers to be applied to both sample averages. Their ratio converges almost surely because the limiting denominator is positive, proving equation 46 . 
 Together with the auxiliary expectation, this yields the gradient in equation 7 of the original population objective, whose minimizer is G_{\star}+C by proposition S1 . The exponential weighting is essential: using unweighted samples from p would instead give the functional stationarity condition P_{G}=p , equivalently G=G_{\star}/\alpha+C .
∎ 
 The auxiliary walkers still sample P_{G_{\theta}} , so the resulting update is a weighted-data version of the same learning rule. With g=\beta G and \gamma=\alpha-1 , the objective coincides with the PS-CD/ \gamma -cross-entropy objective continued to negative \gamma [ Yu et al., 2021 , Fujisawa & Eguchi, 2008 ] , with the Rényi variational representation described in Appendix D [ Birrell et al., 2021 ] . 
 The same ratio argument also yields within-population sample-reuse weights. If a batch was generated at G_{0} , then the current-to-generating density ratios are proportional to 
 w^{(s)}=e^{-\hat{\beta}(G-G_{0})},\qquad w^{(x)}=e^{(\beta-\hat{\beta})(G-G_{0})\circ s}. 
 (47) 
 Normalization within each pool removes the unknown partition functions: 
 \mathbb{E}_{P_{G_{0}}}[w^{(s)}f]=\frac{A_{G}}{A_{G_{0}}}\,\mathbb{E}_{P_{G}}f,\qquad\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{G_{0}}}[w^{(x)}f\circ s]=\frac{B_{G}}{B_{G_{0}}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{G}}f\circ s. 
 Hence, if G and G_{0} are admissible, f is integrable under the current law, and the batch satisfies a law of large numbers under its generating law, the corresponding self-normalized estimators are consistent. Their weight concentration is monitored by the ESS criterion described in Appendix E . 
 The single-level update also admits a scalar-objective interpretation in self-consistent well-tempered VES. For V_{\theta}=-(1-\alpha)G_{\theta} and the instantaneous target t_{\theta}=P_{G_{\theta}} , differentiating the VES functional while holding t fixed gives 
 \left.\nabla_{\theta}\Omega[V_{\theta};t]\right|_{t=t_{\theta}}=(1-\alpha)\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}}[G_{\theta}]. 
 (48) 
 The target convention and the recursive neural VES update are detailed in Section D.1 [ Valsson & Parrinello, 2014 , Valsson & Parrinello, 2015 , Bonati et al., 2019 ] .

 B.7 Reweighted inner steps and the step-size controller 
 Within an epoch the walker samples generated under G_{\theta_{0}} are frozen while several parameter steps are taken and reweighted by self-normalized importance sampling at every step ( Section 2.4 ). A trust region bounds the log-weights: a soft threshold \delta=5 on their 95 th percentile, a hard threshold \delta_{\mathrm{hard}}=8 on their maximum, and a floor of 0.2 on the effective sample size of the pool. Exceeding the hard threshold or falling below the floor ends the epoch and forces fresh trajectory data. Exceeding the soft threshold multiplies the parameter step by 1/q^{2} with q=\lVert\log w\rVert_{95}/\delta . It is used on ADP, ATP and catalysis. Chignolin trains on fresh samples only. 
 On ADP, the inner-iteration budget and learning rate are governed by a sample-based controller rather than a predefined schedule. The controller latches a decay phase when cumulative relative parameter change across checkpoints exceeds a budget of 160\% , then multiplies the learning rate by 0.95 and the inner-iteration count by 0.7 per checkpoint, flooring the count at 5 . Once the count reaches its floor and the learning rate is below 5\% of its initial value, a final phase continues the decay to a floor of 0.3\% and deploys an exponential moving average (EMA) of parameters with decay 0.99 per epoch. ATP uses constant-step training. 
 B.8 Readout, optimizer settings and RMSE gauge 
 Models use system-specific parameter readouts. ATP predictions use a uniform average of the last ten checkpoints, and ADP uses the last eight available checkpoints. At the reported horizon these ADP parameters have already undergone EMA smoothing. These are the Polyak-Ruppert or stochastic-weight-averaging (SWA) readouts [ Polyak & Juditsky, 1992 , Ruppert, 1988 , Izmailov et al., 2018 ] . Catalysis uses the deployed EMA readout with decay 0.9 at checkpoint 300, and chignolin reports its final checkpoint ( 26.2 ns per replica). AdamW [ Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019 ] uses decoupled weight decay w=0.01 in every run, and the peptide runs apply the cautious mask of Liang et al. [2026] , which zeroes coordinates whose Adam step is not descent-aligned with the current gradient and rescales the rest. 
 RMSE is reported after removing the additive constant. Mean alignment , used for ADP, removes the mean difference between the learned and reference surfaces on the scored region. Flat-surface baselines, where reported, use the same region and gauge. ATP dimensional-consistency comparisons use complete retained planes, squared-Pearson correlations and mean-aligned RMSEs after physical Boltzmann marginalization.

 C.2 Four-well dimensional scaling 
 Landscape. 
 The reference landscape has four symmetric Gaussian components whose centers lie in a fixed two-dimensional plane involving all d coordinates. Up to an additive constant, its thermal-unit form is 
 \frac{G_{\star}(s)}{k_{B}T}=-\log\sum_{a,b\in\{-1,1\}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{\|s-\delta(av_{1}+bv_{2})\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{w}^{2}}\right],\qquad\delta=1.6,\quad\sigma_{w}=0.3. 
 Here v_{1}=(1,\ldots,1)/\sqrt{d} , and v_{2} is obtained by orthogonalizing the alternating-sign vector against v_{1} and normalizing it. The remaining directions are harmonic, with dimensionless curvature 1/\sigma_{w}^{2} . The adjacent-well barriers are approximately 13.5\,k_{B}T . The CV map is the identity, and the dynamics use reflecting boundaries on [-3,3]^{d} . The two-dimensional core is identical at every d . The reference contains 40{,}000 i.i.d. draws from the analytic teacher Boltzmann distribution, and errors use all d coordinates. 
 Protocol. 
 d\in\{2,5,10,25,50,100\} . The same three-hidden-layer network of width 1024 is used at every d . A separate perfect-information regression of the exact free energy into this network (i.i.d. Boltzmann samples, exact energies, 160{,}000 Adam updates of batch 1{,}000 ) establishes that it can represent the four-well plane at d=100 (widths 128, 256 and 512 do not learn the d=100 plane, while width 1024 reaches a plane error of 0.03 - 0.13\,k_{B}T against 1.29 for a flat surface at every d ). The primary arms use 1{,}000 physical and 1{,}000 auxiliary walkers in total, both started in the deepest well, 160{,}000 overdamped steps at \Delta t=0.005 (reduced time 800 ), Adam at 3\times 10^{-5} , a bias-corrected parameter EMA with time constant 2{,}000 steps for the convergence readout and a trailing-half parameter average for the endpoint. Arms: single levels at \alpha=1 , 0.5 , 0.25 , 0.1 , 0.05 , and four-level ladders ( \alpha=0.5 , 0.25 , 0.1 , 0.05 ; 250 walkers per level and population) with and without symmetric exchange of physical and auxiliary walkers. As a control on population rather than temperature, the same ladders were also run with 1{,}000 walkers per level and population. Three independent runs were specified per arm. The unbiased d=2 arm uses its two completed runs, and all other arms use three.

 Appendix D Connections to VES, PS-CD, and Rényi objectives 
 The following identities connect the VESPER objective to well-tempered VES, pseudo-spherical contrastive divergence, and a Rényi variational principle. 
 D.1 Well-tempered VES and an explicit shared-energy objective 
 Let p be the physical CV density and t the VES target. The fixed-target functional of Valsson & Parrinello [2014] is 
 \Omega[V;t]=\beta^{-1}\log\mathbb{E}_{p}e^{-\beta V}+\int t(s)V(s)\,\mathrm{d}s. 
 Differentiating with t held fixed gives 
 \nabla_{\theta}\Omega[V_{\theta};t]=-\mathbb{E}_{\rm biased}\nabla_{\theta}V_{\theta}+\mathbb{E}_{t}\nabla_{\theta}V_{\theta}. 
 Set V_{\theta}=-(1-\alpha)G_{\theta} and t_{\theta}=P_{G_{\theta}} , with 0 
 \left.\nabla_{\theta}\Omega[V_{\theta};t]\right|_{t=t_{\theta}}=(1-\alpha)\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{G_{\theta}}}\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta}\circ s-\mathbb{E}_{P_{G_{\theta}}}\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta}\right\}=(1-\alpha)\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}}[G_{\theta}]. 
 This proves equation 48 for every admissible model for which differentiation under the integrals is valid. The factor 1-\alpha can be absorbed into the step size for ordinary gradient descent with the same parameterization and sample estimate. 
 VESPER thus gives the instantaneous self-consistent update an explicit scalar objective, convex in the free energy. This point requires holding the VES target fixed during differentiation. Taking the total derivative of \Omega[V_{\theta};t_{\theta}] also differentiates the target and adds 
 \int V_{\theta}(s)\nabla_{\theta}t_{\theta}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=\alpha\beta(1-\alpha)\operatorname{Cov}_{t_{\theta}}(G_{\theta},\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta}), 
 where we used \nabla_{\theta}t_{\theta}=-\alpha\beta t_{\theta}(\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta}-\mathbb{E}_{t_{\theta}}\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta}) . In contrast, differentiating \mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}} directly gives the desired two-population update, with its minimum and KL control established in propositions S1 and S2 . 
 The distinction also appears in how the target evolves. Well-tempered VES sets t\propto p^{\alpha} [ Valsson & Parrinello, 2015 ] . In neural VES, the free-energy relation \widehat{F}=-V-\beta^{-1}\log t and the previous target t_{n-1}\propto e^{-\alpha\beta\widehat{F}_{n-1}} give the recursive update [ Bonati et al., 2019 ] 
 \widehat{F}_{n}=-V_{n}+\alpha\widehat{F}_{n-1}+C_{n},\qquad t_{n}\propto e^{-\alpha\beta\widehat{F}_{n}}. 
 (49) 
 At a fixed point, (1-\alpha)\widehat{F}=-V+C , so V=-(1-\alpha)G gives \widehat{F}=G+\mathrm{const} . During learning, VESPER instead defines the target directly from the current G_{\theta} and represents it with persistent auxiliary walkers. One model therefore determines both the molecular bias and the auxiliary potential, without a separate recursion for the free-energy estimate. The walker laws track these changing targets through the coupled dynamics analyzed in Section A.6 . 
 This formulation extends directly to the combined objective: every \mathcal{L}_{\hat{\beta}_{k}} has the same physical minimizer, so all levels train one G_{\theta} through their native gradients ( Proposition 1 ). In VES notation, the interior-level update is reproduced by weighting the corresponding fixed-target gradients by c_{k}/(1-\alpha_{k}) . The rescaled gradient has the finite physical-endpoint limit 
 \lim_{\alpha\uparrow 1}\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left.\nabla_{\theta}\Omega[-(1-\alpha)G_{\theta};t]\right|_{t=P_{G_{\theta}}}=\mathbb{E}_{p}\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta}-\mathbb{E}_{P_{G_{\theta},\beta}}\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta}, 
 whenever the two expectations converge. VESPER includes this endpoint through equation 8 . 
 D.2 PS-CD and direct sampling of the two tilted populations 
 With g=\beta G , the dimensionless objective \ell_{\alpha} is given by equation 42 . Let q_{g}=e^{-g}/Z_{g} be the model density relative to a reference measure r , with Z_{g} 
 \ell_{\alpha}[g]=\frac{1}{1+\gamma}\log\int q_{g}^{1+\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}r-\frac{1}{\gamma}\log\int q_{g}^{\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}p=\mathcal{L}^{\rm PS}_{\gamma}[g]. 
 (50) 
 The first term contributes -\log Z_{g} and the second +\log Z_{g} , so the normalizer cancels. This is the PS-CD objective [ Yu et al., 2021 ] , including its negative- \gamma extension, whenever both integrals are finite. For VESPER, 0 proposition S1 . 
 For the Lebesgue reference, write q_{\theta}=q_{G_{\theta}}=e^{-\beta G_{\theta}}/Z_{G_{\theta}} . The two populations in the gradient are exactly the laws used in the main text: 
 \displaystyle P_{G_{\theta}}(s) 
 \displaystyle=\frac{q_{\theta}(s)^{\alpha}}{\int q_{\theta}(u)^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}u}, 
 (51) 
 \displaystyle Q_{G_{\theta}}(s) 
 \displaystyle=\frac{p(s)q_{\theta}(s)^{\alpha-1}}{\int p(u)q_{\theta}(u)^{\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}u}. 
 Differentiating the two log integrals gives \nabla_{\theta}\ell_{\alpha}[g_{\theta}]=\mathbb{E}_{Q_{G_{\theta}}}\nabla_{\theta}g_{\theta}-\mathbb{E}_{P_{G_{\theta}}}\nabla_{\theta}g_{\theta} , recovering equation 7 after division by \beta . At the physical endpoint, P_{G_{\theta}}=q_{\theta} and Q_{G_{\theta}}=p , recovering the maximum-likelihood gradient. 
 The computational difference is how these expectations are obtained. PS-CD uses a fixed dataset for the positive term and reweights samples from q_{\theta} to estimate the tempered model expectation [ Yu et al., 2021 ] . VESPER directly evolves auxiliary walkers in \alpha G_{\theta} , removing that temperature-change importance weighting from the fresh-sample gradient. In molecular applications, it also generates Q_{G_{\theta}} online through the biased microscopic law \nu_{G_{\theta}} . This makes the same variational principle usable when equilibrium configurations are not available in advance: sampling supplies new configurations while their gradients update the free-energy model ( Section A.3 ). 
 D.3 Rényi variational identity and its sampling realization 
 For a probability reference r and 0 42 . Then 
 -\ell_{\alpha}[g]=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\int e^{(\alpha-1)h}\,\mathrm{d}p-\frac{1}{\alpha}\log\int e^{\alpha h}\,\mathrm{d}r, 
 which is the variational expression of Birrell et al. [2021, Theorem 3.1] . With f=\mathrm{d}p/\mathrm{d}r>0 r -almost everywhere and M(\alpha)=\int f^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}r , proposition S8 gives g_{\star}=-\log f+C . Substituting this minimizer into the two integrals yields 
 \inf_{g}\ell_{\alpha}[g]=\frac{\log M(\alpha)}{\alpha(1-\alpha)}=-\frac{1}{\alpha}D_{\alpha}(p\|r),\qquad D_{\alpha}(p\|r)=\frac{\log M(\alpha)}{\alpha-1}. 
 The factor 1/\alpha accounts for the divergence convention in that reference. If f vanishes on a set of positive r -measure, the infimum need not be attained by a finite free energy. 
 The value of this identity for VESPER is that its optimizer is also the quantity needed to drive sampling. For r=\mathrm{d}s , g_{\star}=\beta G_{\star} up to a constant. VESPER uses G_{\theta} to bias microscopic dynamics and to evolve the auxiliary walkers, obtaining both gradient expectations without evaluating the unknown CV density p or first collecting equilibrium data ( Section A.3 ). The KL decomposition in proposition S2 connects the objective excess to the discrepancy of P_{G} and Q_{G} from their common tempered target, and Proposition 1 lets several orders train the same physical free energy. 
 For a reference of finite mass R , normalization to r/R subtracts (\log R)/\alpha from \ell_{\alpha} and leaves its gradients unchanged. On infinite-volume CV domains we instead use the exponential integrals directly, with the integrability conditions of Sections A.1 and A.7 . This preserves the same free-energy minimizer without introducing an artificial uniform probability reference.
