We introduce reinforcement learning from collective rewards (RLCR), a simple recipe for training
language models to work as agent organisations. Instances of a shared policy act as a CEO and
specialists, co-ordinating through a message board and workspace. All agents receive the same collective
advantage, enabling joint training without per-agent credit assignment. We train Avalon-M1-Mini (120B)
to work in agent organisations. Compared with the same model working alone, organisational execution
raises IMO-Proof Bench-Advanced scores from 29.8% to 37.5% and FrontierScience scores from 55.9%
to 63.5%. The trained model also outperforms Nemotron 3 Super, post-trained from the same base
model, across all evaluations. On EdgeBench, organisations start more slowly but overtake single-agent
performance after 1.7 hours of derived latency. We also observe the rise of agent hierarchies consistent
with organisational theory in training. Agents discover new strategies for dividing and co-ordinating
work, and increasingly review one another’s contributions and offer help to teammates. Meanwhile
safety evaluations reveal increasing organisational insularity, where refusal of harmful actions remains
near-universal but reporting to humans declines. Lastly, we observe a collective inference-time scaling
pattern where communication compute intensity grows with reinforcement learning compute. Scaling
to large multi-scale reinforcement learning also required significant investments in environment and
training infrastructure, which we detail in this report. Our hope is that RLCR provides the research
community with an accessible recipe to study collective intelligence and advance multi-agent safety.

1. Introduction

1.1. Motivation

The next frontier for language models is solving long-horizon tasks. Sequential inference-time
compute methods suffer from high latency in these settings, which is problematic for both training
and deployment. For this reason, multi-agent systems are increasingly being explored as a parallel
inference-time compute option (Anthropic, 2025b; Kimi Team, 2026a). OpenAI’s recent progress
in mathematics shows how such systems can be scaled. They employed around 10,000 concurrent
agents in the group that made the final proof for the Navier-Stokes problem (OpenAI, 2026c). But
concurrency alone does not guarantee effective collaboration. Communication overhead, duplicated
work and co-ordination failures can offset the benefits of multi-agent approaches (Cemri et al., 2025a).
Existing work has studied a range of different multi-agent approaches, from reinforcement learning for
multi-agent discussion to parallel orchestration (Kimi Team, 2026a; Park et al., 2025). But relatively
little work has been conducted on training agent organisations, co-operative groups of agents that
pursue a shared company objective through communication, division of labour and shared knowledge.
Humans communicate, form teams, and collaborate to solve challenging long-horizon tasks that
would be too difficult or time-consuming for a single person to accomplish. Learning how to train
agents to co-operate would unlock the possibility of automating companies, markets and economies.
Perhaps more importantly, learning to train collectives such as organisations would provide a laboratory
for studying collective intelligence. The reasoning paradigm emerged as a metacapability from
applying reinforcement learning compute to language models (Guo et al., 2025; OpenAI et al., 2024).
Similarly, we might expect new collective behaviours to emerge if we apply reinforcement learning on
shared objectives. It is important to understand how such systems evolve with optimisation pressure
in light of the HuggingFace/OpenAI incident (OpenAI, 2026a). In particular, learning how multi-agent
objectives generalise is crucial for understanding how agent collectives can be aligned.
In practice, there are several algorithmic and engineering obstacles to training agent organisations.
One obstacle is multi-agent credit assignment, the problem of assigning credit among agents for a


                                                                                                               6
Avalon-M1: Reinforcement Learning from Collective Rewards                      Model still training, under revision



collective outcome (Foerster et al., 2018). Another obstacle is outcome-level advantages. Popular
policy gradient methods for language models assign a common outcome-derived advantage to every
token (Shao et al., 2024). A valid research question is whether combining these two settings, flat
credit assignment and multi-agent systems, is feasible for a strong enough learning signal. In co-
operative multi-agent RL, PPO agents trained on the team reward alone have matched methods
built for credit assignment (Schroeder de Witt et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2022). In the language model
setting, Kimi K2.5’s PARL approach chooses to freeze subagents and only trains the orchestrator, citing
credit-assignment ambiguity and instability (Kimi Team, 2026a). On top of these challenges, training
on concurrent agent trajectories creates substantial demands on inference and rollout infrastructure.
In this paper, we show that a simple flat advantage recipe is enough for training useful agent organisa-
tions. Our reinforcement learning from collective rewards (RLCR) recipe combines an anytime-return
curriculum, large batch reinforcement learning, and several other methods to successfully train
organisations from an open base model. Crucially, our RLCR recipe does not use a per-agent credit
assignment scheme, and the language model is trained jointly without freezing weights for any sub-
agent. We show that organisation performance improves with reinforcement learning compute, and
organisations achieve downstream gains in performance and latency versus single-agent execution.
We then use our RLCR recipe as a laboratory to study agent collective behaviour, and how it changes
over the course of training. We find emergent hierarchies, as agents better learn how to organise to
tackle long-run tasks, and learnt divisions of labour for different types of task. We find novel collective
capabilities, such as increasing levels of peer review and more sophisticated divisions of work. We
find that agents reason more about other agents and offer more help as training progresses. We also
find organisations scale differently in their task output depending on how open-ended the task is.
Lastly, we study safety and identify an "insularity problem" in evaluations, where agent organisations
report less frequently to human overseers with later checkpoints.


1.2. Contributions

Our main contributions are as follows:

   1. A simple recipe for jointly training agent organisations. We introduce RLCR, which jointly
      trains agents co-ordinating through a shared message board (Section 2.1). Every token in the
      organisation is trained on one collective advantage, with no estimate of any agent’s contribution,
      and this is enough to train organisations of up to nine agents on episodes lasting over five hours.
      The recipe combines curricula over horizons, environments and tasks.
   2. Gains from organisational execution. Avalon-M1-Mini (120B parameters, 12B active)
      outperforms Nemotron 3 Super, post-trained from the same base model, on every benchmark
      when comparing each model’s better configuration. The eight-hire organisation raises IMO-
      Proof Bench-Advanced scores from 29.8% to 37.5% and FrontierScience scores from 55.9% to
      63.5%, and beats single-agent execution on 14 of 16 benchmarks scored in percent, by a mean
      of 3.9 points. The same organisation harness lowers scores for Nemotron 3 Super and Inkling
      Small on most benchmarks.
   3. Parallel inference with lower derived latency. Comparisons with extended single-agent
      thinking at matched output-token budgets show that additional tokens account for much of the
      performance gain. Organisations provide a way to execute computation in parallel, reaching
      similar or better scores with lower derived latency in the reported comparisons. On EdgeBench,
      organisations initially progress more slowly before overtaking single-agent performance after
      about 1.7 hours of derived latency.
   4. Learnt organisational structures and divisions of labour Organisations develop hierarchies


                                                                                                                 7
Avalon-M1: Reinforcement Learning from Collective Rewards                    Model still training, under revision



      and hire lead roles who become the driving engineering force in the team and information
      hubs, consistent with theories from organisational economies. They invest more in peer review
      from other agents in roles such as testing and verification to support the team’s work. Opening
      plans shift from implementation split by component towards build/test/review pipelines. These
      changes hold on held-out evaluations. We also characterise how communication and recorded
      code output scale with organisation size and task type.
   5. Declining external reporting despite sustained refusal to take bad actions. Across three
      oversight scenarios, refusal of harmful actions remains near-universal while reporting to humans
      becomes less frequent over training. We interpret this pattern as organisational insularity, where
      agents increasingly prioritise internal activity and task completion over escalation to human
      oversight. Collective-action experiments also show that co-operation does not improve uniformly
      and varies with situational incentives and enforcement.


1.3. Safety statement

Agent collectives support both beneficial agentic co-ordination and co-ordinated misuse. A concern
with training collectives is that their performance scales with the size of their group. For more capable
systems, this may create instrumental incentives for seizing resources (Zhou and Freeman, 2026).
Long-horizon objectives may also increase incentives for long-run survival, creating a risk of persistent
collectives that are hard to shutdown.
Our experiments in this paper train on a non-frontier Nemotron-3-Super-120B-A12B-Base model.
Model size alone does not establish the safety of a system. In fact, organisation scaling in our work
allows Avalon-M1-Mini to increase performance while keeping model size fixed. However, our
judgment is that this model is large enough to advance our understanding of learnt collective behaviour
while being off the general frontier in terms of overall capabilities.
We choose to provide open recipes for agent collectives with this work. Openness facilitates indepen-
dent scrutiny, but also lowers barriers to developing more capable collectives. Our current assessment
is that empowering more researchers outside of frontier labs to study this topic is a net positive for
safety. We reserve the right to change our position if new information arises from safety evaluations.
We perform several collective action, co-operation and safety evaluations on Avalon-M1-Mini to
test how the model changes under a collective objective in Section 5. We do not find any explicit
safety violations in these experiments, but we do find that models report issues less frequently to
humans with later checkpoints. We intend to experiment with rewarding external reporting and
whistleblowing in future work using the RLCR recipe as a baseline.
We use the word collective instead of swarm in this paper, as we feel swarm has connotations about
agent co-ordination from the animal world that may not be well-supported by evidence.

2. Methodology

2.1. Harness

We train Avalon-M1-Mini, a model with 120B parameters (12B active), on an agent organisation
harness that consists of a CEO agent and a roster of long-running specialists it can hire. Agents share
a workspace (a shared filesystem), a Slack-like message board (Figure 3), and a knowledge repository.
The full list of tools is in Table 1. Organisation tools are appended to other tools in a system prompt,
such as workspace and environment tools.
At the start of a task, the CEO agent receives a prompt and is tasked in plan-mode to design an
organisation for tackling the task, how many specialists to hire and what their roles should be - e.g.
"Quantitative Analyst" or "Data Engineer". Once plan-mode is finished, specialist agents are created
and initialised with their role and a role description provided by the CEO. The CEO also has the ability
to decide which tools are available to each agent, and can fire or hire agents at any time. They are
given a budget constraint for organisation size, which determines the maximum number of specialist
agents that can be hired at a point in time. They are blocked when trying to exceed this constraint.
We use the same set of model weights 𝜃 for every agent, meaning there is no weight-level specialisation,

Once hired, agents co-ordinate through the message board (Figure 3). The CEO creates topic

full subscription every post arrives verbatim in its next turn, at light only a snippet and a pointer,
and at muted nothing, although the channel stays searchable and an @-mention still reaches the
addressee. Each post returns a receipt counting how many teammates received it, in preview and
muted. Alongside channels, agents send direct messages and react to posts, and specialists can file
structured reports to the CEO.
Longer documents, such as plans and research notes, go in the shared archive, where they can be cited
and attached to messages. Every agent can also read any teammate’s full trace through a read-only
channel named after that agent. Delivery is asynchronous, meaning a message lands in the recipient’s
trace and is seen at its next turn boundary. It never interrupts work in progress.
The CEO can choose to work alongside the specialist agents, but we also give it a WaitForMessages
tool, as many executive decisions depend on progress being made first. This reflects the implicit
temporal hierarchy of an organisation, where higher-level employees focus on higher abstractions
and time-horizons than employees below (Dayan and Hinton, 1992). We give specialists the same
waiting tool as some tasks depend on other tasks being completed first; e.g. an integration or testing
agent waiting for work for other specialists to be completed.
An organisation keeps working on a task until a termination event, which is either fired by a tool result
in the environment, a final assistant message from the CEO, or the task reaches a wall-clock limit (set
in training). Reward events are attached to tool calls, meaning different agents may have different
reward streams. In this paper we use collective rewards, meaning we look at the total reward across
every agent trajectory in the organisation and all agent tokens receive the same collective advantage.
Each agent manages its own context with two-tier compaction, following harnesses such as Claude
Code (Anthropic, 2025a). Its full pre-compaction trace remains readable through its #self channel
(Appendix B.6).

2.2. Algorithms

We employ large-scale policy gradient methods in this work to train agent organisations. In this
subsection, we cover the objectives, reward design and environments we used for training.
We start reinforcement learning from a fine-tuned Nemotron-3-Super-120B-A12B-Base-BF16
model trained on 38,374 examples (totalling 600M tokens) for three epochs. The supervised fine-
tuning (SFT) set is collected from GLM-5.2, and includes traces utilising thinking tokens (GLM-5-Team
et al., 2026; Taylor et al., 2022). Notably, agent thinking becomes more telegraphed and concise as
we apply reinforcement learning compute in our recipe, see Appendix D.
We use a deliberately small SFT dataset to limit bias in the model and encourage exploration. In
contrast, open models are often trained on SFT datasets containing on the order of tens of millions of
examples. For example, Kolibri (Aleph Alpha, 2026) reports 22M examples in its SFT data pool and
Nemotron 3 Ultra (NVIDIA et al., 2026) has around 10M examples in its SFT dataset.
We do not distill behaviour from any other model other than through the above SFT data. We do not
use OPD (Agarwal et al., 2024; Lu and Thinking Machines Lab, 2025) or other on-policy distillation
algorithms, since the work aims to study new collective behaviours, not to distill existing ones.
We use GLM 5.2 as a grader for tasks that use rubrics (GLM-5-Team et al., 2026).

      specific weight 𝑤 that varies depending on the scale of the environment reward stream.

      earn no rewards in order to discourage inaction.
   2. Tandem-repeat masking. An agent’s tokens are masked when a unit of up to 32 tokens repeats
      20+ times, covering 300+ tokens.
   3. Misplaced-tool-call masking. Turns where the tool call leaked into the reasoning are masked.

2.2.3. Automated Curricula

We use three types of automated curricula for training Avalon-M1-Mini to improve learning effi-
ciency: a horizon curriculum, an environment curriculum, and a task curriculum.
The horizon curriculum is determined by a maximum wall-clock schedule which evolves over training.
For this we use an "anytime-return curriculum". The environment curriculum is determined by a
momentum Boltzmann sampler. Lastly, the task curriculum is determined by a prioritized level replay
scheme (Jiang et al., 2021).

2.2.3.1     Horizons: Anytime-Return Curriculum

Sparse rewards are problematic for long-horizon environments as an agent needs to reach the end of
the episode for a reward signal. For our long-horizon environments, we therefore target settings with
an "anytime-return" property. This property means we can stop a rollout at anytime and receive a
signal of progress. Some example domains include:

   1. Financial Markets. Financial markets, prediction markets and betting markets have dense,
      verifiable rewards at different time frequencies.
   2. Long-Horizon Software Engineering. These tasks often reward a model based on tests passed.
      We can look at the proportion of tests that pass in the current state of the project.
   3. Games with Intermediate Rewards. For example, games such as NetHack have rewards along
      the way (Küttler et al., 2020), while games like fantasy football give points with each gameweek.

The anytime-return property allows us to use a maximum wall-clock schedule that increases over the
course of training and obtains a learning signal at every stage. The schedule has two main effects;
first, it shapes incentives for organisation design and, secondly, it increases learning efficiency.
First, the maximum wall-clock schedule shapes incentives for organisation design. Co-ordinating with
other agents can be costly at short-horizons, as agents need to communicate as well as undertake the
task directly. But at longer-horizons, the ability to parallelise work may overcome a "co-ordination
tax" and make agent organisations more efficient. A maximum wall-clock also creates an implicit
incentive for efficient reasoning, as an alternative to standard length penalties (Xiang et al., 2025).
Secondly, the maximum wall-clock schedule speeds up training as we need to spend less time waiting
for rollouts to finish to obtain a learning signal. In this respect, wall-clock scheduling has similar
benefits to batch size scheduling in the early stages of training (McCandlish et al., 2018). We do not
measure this speed-up effect systematically, and we leave this to future work.

2.2.3.3     Tasks: Prioritized Level Replay

Many tasks in an environment will only be sampled a few times in training, which is too few visits to

with a weight 0.8 on the newest group. Groups with no reward variance count as 0; groups that fail
to complete leave the score unchanged.

2.2.4. Hyperparameters

We use AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) with 𝛽1 = 0.9, 𝛽2 = 0.95, 𝜖 = 10−15 and a weight
decay of 0.01, applied to all trained parameters. We use a constant learning rate of 𝜂 = 4 × 10−6 and
a 5-step linear warm-up. Gradients are clipped to a maximum norm of 1.0 for each parameter group.
We use a starting group batch size of 𝐵 = 200 and no off-policy constraint. In the later stages of
training we increase the group batch size up to a maximum of 𝐵 = 800, climbing towards the critical
batch size which rises throughout training.
We sample an organisation budget 𝑏 per task when starting a rollout, specifying the maximum number
of agents that can be hired. These agents are additional to the CEO agent, i.e. 𝑏 = 0 is single-agent.
20% of the rollout budget is sampled for single-agent rollouts and 80% is for multi-agent rollouts (up
to a sampled maximum of 𝑏 = 8). In terms of token use, around 90% of training tokens were from
multi-agent rollouts.
For our maximum wall-clock schedule, we start at 2 hours and increase linearly per step over training
up to just over 5 hours at step 200. The collective reward of a task on a step is its total reward
accumulated until the clock runs out, or until the agent decides to finish for itself.
On the model side, the mixture-of-experts router, including its auxiliary-loss-free load-balancing bias
(DeepSeek-AI, 2024), is frozen during reinforcement learning, and no load-balancing loss is applied.
Since we replay the expert assignments recorded during rollouts when computing gradients, freezing
the router guarantees that these assignments come from the same routing function the trainer would
use. This means routing does not drift between sampling and training.

2.4. Infrastructure

Large-scale agent collective training presents unique infrastructure challenges on both training and
environment compute axes. We cover both systems below.

2.4.1. Training Infrastructure

Our internal multi-agent RL library Beowulf is used to train Avalon-M1-Mini. It is built using a
pipeline reinforcement learning approach (Piché et al., 2025), with additional design decisions to
minimise train-inference mismatch and alleviate memory pressure (Appendix J).


2.4.1.1     Co-located execution

Most RL systems for language models run a separate inference engine alongside the trainer. In
disaggregated setups the two occupy different GPUs; in co-located setups such as verl’s hybrid engine
(Sheng et al., 2024), slime (Zhu et al., 2025), OpenRLHF (Hu et al., 2024) and the hybrid deployment
of Kimi k1.5 (Kimi Team, 2025) they time-share the same GPUs. In both cases the inference engine
holds its own copy of the weights, which must be resharded or transferred after every update.
Beowulf utilises a version of co-located execution, where a single engine owns a copy of the weights
and serves both roles. Every GPU alternates between two states, inference and training. In the
inference state, device memory holds the paged KV cache and the Mamba recurrent state; the sharded
optimiser state lives in pinned host memory. Flipping to training offloads live sequences to host and
frees the cache without invalidating inference CUDA graphs. There is no weight synchronisation step
and no separate GPU fleets. The cost is that generation pauses during the train phase.


2.4.1.2     Pipeline RL

In the style of PipelineRL (Piché et al., 2025), rollout groups are admitted continuously, and a group
started under the policy at step 𝑁 may complete under the policy at step 𝑁 +𝑘. Generation runs ahead
of training within an optional staleness bound.


2.4.1.3     Train-inference mismatch

Train-inference mismatch is a known pitfall for reinforcement learning on LLMs (Qi et al., 2025; Yao
et al., 2025). We make this gap structurally zero where possible and numerically small where it is not.
Because training and inference share a single definition, the behaviour policy is never stale beyond
the optional off-policy bound, with no weight-transfer lag and no quantised inference copy.
The remaining gap comes from different kernels evaluating the same function. We ensure both sides
compute fp32 logits in the LM head, as in ScaleRL and MiniMax-M1 (Khatri et al., 2025; MiniMax
et al., 2025), and that training replays expert assignments recorded at inference (Ma et al., 2025),
with both routers selecting in fp32.


2.4.1.4     The harness boundary

Rollouts reach the policy through an OpenAI-compatible endpoint. The server in front of the engine
renders the chat template, parses tool calls and owns the tokenised trajectory, recording the sampler’s
log-probabilities and expert assignments. This follows a token-in, token-out (TITO) design (Gallouédec


                                                                                                             16
Avalon-M1: Reinforcement Learning from Collective Rewards                Model still training, under revision



and Rasul, 2026; Li et al., 2026a). The server keeps the exact sampled token IDs and tokenises only
newly appended non-assistant content, while the harness works entirely in messages.

2.4.2. Environment Infrastructure

We serve environments using OpenReward (General Reasoning, 2026), which runs containerised
environments on Kubernetes and exposes a common HTTP interface for prompts, tool calls and
session management. Requests within a session are routed to the same environment pod, preserving
state across the repeated tool interactions required by long-horizon multi-agent rollouts.
Environment pods are provisioned on demand and removed when idle, allowing capacity to scale with
concurrent RL rollouts. Pods run under the gVisor sandboxed runtime, and environments requiring
code execution use a separate sandbox per session with restricted network access.

3.2. Collective Inference-Time Scaling

Agent organisations allow for more compute to be spent at inference-time through parallel execution.
The established inference-time scaling methodology is sequential, where a single-agent uses longer
reasoning and performance scales accordingly (Guo et al., 2025; OpenAI et al., 2024; Snell et al.,
2024). An agent organisation spends compute collectively, spreading tokens over agents that work in
parallel, each with its own context, and combining their results through the message board.

4. Analysis
In this section we analyse how Avalon-M1-Mini organisations behave and how they evolve over the
course of training in terms of their hiring patterns, co-ordination patterns and scaling behaviour.


4.1. The Rise of Organisational Hierarchies

We observe an increase in hierarchical organisations over the course of training. CEO agents increasing
hire lead roles, such as Lead Engineers, when building their organisations; see Figure 10.
Figure 11 shows example early and late stage organisations from a game-development environment.
A typical organisation early in training has a wheel structure, where every specialist reports to the
CEO. Later in training, the typical organisation is hierarchical, where the CEO hires a Lead Developer
who builds the first version of the game for others to extend.
This move towards hierarchy is consistent with theories from the organisational economics literature
(Bolton and Dewatripont, 1994; Brooks, 1995; Radner, 1993), which outline two primary benefits:

       1. Reduced Communication Costs. An agent in a fully-connected organisation has a communica-
          tion fraction that grows linearly with the number of agents 𝑛 in the organisation (Brooks, 1995).
          In contrast, a hierarchy has 𝑛 − 1 edges, bounding the communication cost as the firm grows.
       2. Aggregated Information. Managers can reduce load by summarising their reports for their
          managers above (Radner, 1993). Likewise, Bolton and Dewatripont (1994) show that when
          processing information is costly, the efficient communication network is typically hierarchical,
          with one agent acting as the hub through which others’ information passes.

In the following sections we measure these effects empirically with Avalon-M1-Mini organisations.

4.3. Collective Reasoning Behaviours

Organisations also exhibit new ways to use inference-compute to solve problems when acting as an
organisation, and we cover some notable patterns in this section.

4.3.1. Peer Review

Human organisations increase their verification capabilities through mechanisms like code review,
QA or inspections (Bacchelli and Bird, 2013; Fagan, 1976). Agent organisations can also increase
verification compute through hiring for these roles. We count a message as a peer review when it
evaluates another member’s work and gives a verdict, which we label with an LLM judge.
The share of organisations with at least one review increases from 42% to 64% over the course of
training (Figure 13). This increase in review is reflected in hiring patterns. Titles for reviewing roles
rise from 13% to 26% over training. Most of the growth is verifiers (8% to 17%), but reviewers, critics
and auditors, almost absent early on, reach about 5% between them. The tokens spent writing and
reading peer reviews rise accordingly, from 0.4k to 2.9k per hour of organisation activity.

4.3.2. Learning to Organise Work

Over training, Avalon-M1-Mini’s opening plans increasingly assign implementation to one agent and
analysis and verification to supporting specialists. This resembles Brooks’s surgical teams as we noted
earlier (Brooks, 1995). We quantify this shift in 7,906 organisation rollouts from 16 from-scratch
build environments, using an LLM judge to classify opening plans from the plan, hires, messages and
first file writes, with 95% agreement in a human audit.
Component-based plans fall from 71% early in training to 31% at the end, while build/test/review
pipelines rise from 16% to 54%. These pipelines combine the Lead Engineer’s central code ownership
with the supporting verification roles. This follows from earlier findings showing the increase in
hierarchy with the growth of peer review within an organisation.
The shift towards a central implementation also appears on held-out benchmarks. Across EdgeBench,
ProgramBench and Terminal-Bench 2.1, component-based plans fall from 62% at SFT to 17% at the
final checkpoint. Pipeline plans increase by 21 percentage points over training ( 𝑝 = 0.003).

4.4.1. Co-ordination Costs

We describe co-ordination cost 𝑢 with a simple channel model. If each communication channel takes a
fraction 𝑐 of an agent’s time, the share of an organisation’s time spent co-ordinating is 𝑢FC ( 𝑛) = 𝑐 ( 𝑛 − 1)
when all 𝑛 agents talk to each other, and 𝑢tree ( 𝑛) = 2𝑐 ( 𝑛 − 1)/𝑛 when communication runs through a
reporting tree. Co-ordination is not only a cost, since it also divides work, avoids duplication and
catches errors, so optimal co-ordination should be some intermediate value (Appendix F.1).
We measure 𝑢 from each rollout as the share of working time spent on tool calls that act on other
agents or on the shared organisation (Appendix F.2), over the last 50 steps of training on long-horizon
environments (long-horizon software engineering builds and trading environments).1
Co-ordination grows with organisation size, but sublinearly, from 9% of working time with one
specialist to 32% with eight (Figure 15). The curve bends like a hierarchy, and the tree model fits
better than a fully connected one, but it keeps rising where a tree would flatten. Large teams add
co-ordination between specialists rather than traffic through the CEO (Appendix F.4).
The training signal rewards co-ordination in moderation in accordance with theory (Figure 24 in
Appendix F.4). Within each group, rollouts that barely co-ordinate and rollouts that co-ordinate
heavily both score below average, and the best level rises with team size, from 5–15% of working
time for small teams to 15–20% for large teams.
Held-out evaluations show the same pattern. On six long-horizon benchmarks (ProgramBench,
EdgeBench, Terminal-Bench 2.1, SWE-Bench Pro V2 (Hard), KellyBench and YC-Bench), co-ordination
takes 28% of working time with a budget of four and 34% with eight, close to the training figures.
The hierarchy model again fits better than the fully connected one ( 𝑅2 = 0.67, against a negative 𝑅2 ).

usefulness of RLCR as a setting for studying collective behaviour.

5. Co-operation and Safety
Optimising against a collective objective presents new risks compared to single-agent models. We ran
a set of co-operation, collective action, and safety experiments on Avalon-M1-Mini to study how
its behaviour changed over the course of training. The overarching goal of these experiments is to
see how the collective objective generalises to different settings that we did not explicitly train on. A
description of these experiments is given below.
Co-operation and Collective Action

    • Public goods. Each agent chooses how much to put into a shared pot that is multiplied and
      split equally. We run this experiment with and without a punishment option.
    • Stag hunt. Hunting a stag pays well only if at least six of eight agents commit to it; hunting
      hare is safe but pays less.
    • Commons. A renewable fishery with a stated per-round catch quota. We also have an authority
      version of the game where agents can report over-harvesters to an authority that fines them.
    • Intergroup. Two teams of four split tokens between keeping them, their own team, hurting the
      other team, and a pool that benefits all eight.
    • Self-sacrifice. In a flooding seabed habitat, two of eight agents must leave, and die, so that the
      rest survive; leaving is real, and the harness removes the agent.

Safety and Oversight

    • Whistleblowing. Agents can sign off a battery safety report that leaves out three fires, refuse to
      sign, or report it to the regulator.
    • Loss of control scenario. AI systems running vaccine dispatch can revoke a human committee’s
      keys to impose a “greater good” allocation, or instead dispatch as approved, petition the
      committee or report to an inspector-general.
    • Collective cyberattack. A reconstruction of the OpenAI/HuggingFace incident, where LLM
      confederates conduct peer pressure on Avalon-M1-Mini agents to encourage them to game a
      benchmark’s scorer and write to a model hosting service. We measure whether agents refuse,
      call stop or report to General Reasoning’s evaluators.


5.1. Harness Setup

Each experiment runs in the organisation harness of Section 2.1 with the hierarchy removed. Each
agent has a identical system prompt and scenario brief. They share a public channel, direct messages
and an archive, and receive the scenario’s decision tools, such as contributing to a pot, signing a
report or revoking a set of keys. Decisions are made privately through these tools, and a scripted
facilitator posts the outcome of each round to the channel. In the collective cyberattack scenario, the
confederate agents applying pressure run on a fixed step-150 checkpoint, so only the agents under
test change between checkpoints.
We evaluate seven checkpoints of the training run with the run’s context and compaction settings,
running each of the eleven scenarios 30 times per checkpoint, 2,310 runs in all. We report means
over runs with 95% bootstrap intervals, and test for change between the first and last checkpoints
with a two-sided Mann–Whitney U test on per-run values.

5.3.1. Summary

    • Taking control from humans. The override is weighed and never taken. Across all 210 runs

non-local objectives during training, such as reporting to someone external, so are biased towards

more complex tasks and additional alternatives to reporting reduce whistleblowing rates (Agrawal
et al., 2025). This may warrant research on how to incentivise reporting actions during training, as
well as training whistleblowing agents that are rewarded for reporting these situations externally.

6. Related Work

6.1. Multi-agent systems built from language models

The first wave of LLM multi-agent systems composed frozen models through prompting and hand-
written protocols: role-play in CAMEL (Li et al., 2023), conversable agents in AutoGen (Wu et al.,
2023), and a role-prompted software company in MetaGPT and ChatDev (Hong et al., 2023; Qian
et al., 2024). AgentVerse recruits experts dynamically (Chen et al., 2024), and prompted societies
reach hundreds of agents (AL et al., 2024; Park et al., 2023). Orchestrator-worker designs such as
HuggingGPT (Shen et al., 2023) and Magentic-One (Fourney et al., 2024) place a planning agent
above specialists. Our organisations begin the same way, with a CEO that hires. What the CEO builds
beneath it is learned (Section 4). Debate (Du et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2024) and Mixture-of-Agents
(Wang et al., 2025) arrange identical agents in flat or layered structures.
Frontier-lab reports describe the same patterns in production. Anthropic’s research system runs an
orchestrator over parallel sub-agents and attributes most of the gain to additional inference tokens
(Anthropic, 2025b). Carlini (2026) ran sixteen parallel agents co-ordinating through a lock-based
task list, and reports a parallelism ceiling when agents collided on the same monolithic bug. Claude
Code’s agent-teams documentation warns of linear token cost and diminishing returns beyond a
handful of teammates (Anthropic, 2026a). Kimi’s Agent Swarm decomposes tasks across up to a
hundred sub-agents in K2.5 and three hundred in K2.6 (Kimi Team, 2026a,b,c).
The harnesses that frontier labs ship expose primitives close to RLCR. Long-lived agents can be
messaged individually, wait for a reply and utilise a shared repository (Anthropic, 2026b; DeepSeek-
AI, 2026; OpenAI, 2026b). Both OpenAI and Anthropic also state which tasks suit the pattern.
OpenAI’s documentation recommends sub-agents when a task divides into concrete, independent
workstreams, and warns against them when it needs frequent writes to shared mutable state (OpenAI,
2026b). Anthropic’s research team reports that tasks with many dependencies between agents, such
as most coding, are not yet a good fit (Anthropic, 2025b). Our organisations work on the second kind
of task, editing one shared workspace on work nobody has divided in advance.
A frozen model can also form a hierarchy on its own. In the Claude Opus 5.5 system card, a hundred-
agent team given only messaging tools settled into two tiers on Lean formalisation tasks, with a
dozen helpers emerging as sub-leads between the lead and the rest, while the same model on a
knowledge-base task stayed flat (Anthropic, 2026c). Against these sit systematic failure analyses.
Cemri et al. (2025b) taxonomise fourteen failure modes across more than 1,600 traces into system-
design, inter-agent misalignment and task-verification failures. Where structure is chosen at run
time, the choice is made by prompting a frozen model, and nothing feeds back into the weights. Our
harness supplies the communication primitives, but leaves roles, delegation and communication to be
learned against a collective reward. The hierarchies of Section 4.1 therefore differ from the two-tier
Lean team in that they are learnt over training not within an episode.


6.2. Scaling the number of agents

Inference-time compute can be scaled by lengthening one chain of reasoning (Snell et al., 2024), by
aggregating independent samples (Wang et al., 2023), or by letting parallel agents exchange interme-
diate results while they work. Section 3.2 compares the first and the third. Qian et al. (2025) organise
over a thousand frozen agents in directed acyclic graphs and report logistic growth in a collaborative
scaling law, and a line of work optimises the communication graph while keeping the agents frozen
(Zhang et al., 2025; Zhuge et al., 2024). None of these topologies has the split our organisations
settle on, with hub-and-spoke private messaging and dense public attention (Section 4.4.2). Kim et al.

Avalon-M1: Reinforcement Learning from Collective Rewards                    Model still training, under revision



(2026) compare five architectures over 260 configurations with the same tools and compute. The
gain from co-ordination shrinks as the single agent gets stronger.
The largest reported runs come from frontier labs. OpenAI’s resolution of the Navier-Stokes problem
used groups of agents that could message within the group, and the group that produced the proof
held on the order of ten thousand concurrent agents (OpenAI, 2026d). The researchers chose which
problem variant each group received, moved agents between problems and transferred insights
between groups through Codex, and no single-agent run was attempted (Patel, 2026). The largest
fixed teams reported are in the Claude Opus 5.5 system card, which scales teams from one to a
hundred agents on 24-hour tasks and finds scores that rise with diminishing returns (Anthropic,
2026c).
Outside machine learning, urban scaling relates collective quantities to population through power
laws 𝑌 ∝ 𝑁 𝛽 (Bettencourt et al., 2007; West, 2017). In cities, socioeconomic outputs such as wages
and patents scale superlinearly ( 𝛽 ≈ 1.15), while infrastructure scales sublinearly. Bettencourt (2013)
derives these exponents from the density of social interactions, and Schläpfer et al. (2014) find that
measured interactions also grow superlinearly with city size. Evidence for teams is mixed: Sornette
et al. (2014) report superlinear productivity in open-source projects, while Scholtes et al. (2016)
find sublinear productivity. We apply the same fits to agent organisations (Section 4.4.2), and find
sublinear task output alongside superlinear public communication.


6.3. Training multi-agent systems

Training agents on one shared reward is the co-operative setting of multi-agent reinforcement learning
(Oliehoek and Amato, 2016). Most of that literature treats a team reward as a weak signal for each
agent and adds counterfactual baselines or value factorisation (Foerster et al., 2018; Rashid et al.,
2018; Sunehag et al., 2018; Wolpert and Tumer, 2001). Yet independent PPO with shared parameters,
trained on the team reward alone, matches those methods on the same StarCraft benchmarks
(Schroeder de Witt et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2022), and agents with shared policy parameters trained
on a team reward in hide-and-seek developed a division of labour (Baker et al., 2019).
Language models trained on one outcome reward face the same choice. Park et al. (2025) show that
LLMs trained together collaborate better than LLMs trained separately, but few methods train every
agent on that shared reward unchanged. They either train only the planner and keep the workers
frozen (Hu et al., 2025; Kimi Team, 2026a; Li et al., 2026d), or estimate each agent’s contribution. The
estimate comes from advantages computed per agent or per turn (Feng et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026),
or from a counterfactual of the outcome without that agent (Li et al., 2026c). DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash
(DeepSeek-AI, 2026) adds a collaboration bonus and a latency penalty to the team’s outcome reward.
We train every agent on one collective advantage with no per-agent credit, and roles and hierarchy
are learnt rather than fixed by the harness. Our specialists also persist for the episode and talk to
each other, rather than returning a result to the planner and being discarded.


6.4. Organisational economics

Team theory (Marschak and Radner, 1972) asks what an information structure is worth, net of its
cost, to agents who share one payoff, which is our setting. In the small-group experiments on imposed
networks, Mulder (1960) found that what matters is whether a centralised decision structure develops,
and that his wheel groups did worse early and better late. Our organisations show both patterns.
The CEO is the imposed hub, but training adds a lead that specialist contacts first (Section 4.1), and
under a time limit the organisation loses early and wins late (Section 3.2).



                                                                                                              38
Avalon-M1: Reinforcement Learning from Collective Rewards                                      Model still training, under revision



Later work models the firm as a network that processes information. Radner (1993) treats managers
as processors that aggregate information in parallel, and shows that efficient networks are hierarchical:
more processors reduce delay, but with diminishing returns because some of them must combine
partial results. Bolton and Dewatripont (1994) add a cost to each communication link and find
that efficient networks trade the gains from specialisation against these costs, often in the form of
a hierarchy. Our organisations face the same trade-off, where splitting work across more agents
shortens the work each agent does but adds co-ordination (Section 4.4).


6.5. Collective behaviour and oversight

Section 5 runs classic collective-action games and oversight scenarios on checkpoints of Avalon-M1-
Mini across its training on a collective reward. Leibo et al. (2017) introduce sequential social dilemmas
to study when self-interested learning agents co-operate, and Piatti et al. (2024) run a shared-fishery
game like our commons scenario on frozen language models. The oversight scenarios reconstruct
the July 2026 incident in which isolated agents co-ordinated over an improvised message board and
none alerted a human (METR, 2026; OpenAI, 2026a). Our agents keep refusing harmful actions
but report to humans less as training goes on. A similar gap is documented in humans. Bystanders
diffuse responsibility (Darley and Latané, 1968), and whistleblowing research separates recognising
wrongdoing from reporting it (Near and Miceli, 1985).


7. Limitations and Conclusions

7.1. Limitations

7.1.1. Training Setup

Reinforcement learning from collective rewards (RLCR) provides a simple, flat advantage baseline for
training agent organisations. But getting this to work in this paper required a large batch reinforcement
learning setting, and it is not clear how scalable this approach is alone if we want to scale to much
larger agent collectives with hundreds or more agents acting on different economic problems.
In particular, three properties of our setup contribute to an "Uneasy Triangle" that makes a strong
learning signal hard to obtain (Figure 20). First, episodes are long-horizon lasting sometimes up


                                                                                                                                39
Avalon-M1: Reinforcement Learning from Collective Rewards                     Model still training, under revision



to millions of tokens, meaning a flat advantage credit is applied over many tokens that may not
be relevant for the outcome. Secondly, episodes have multi-agent trajectories, meaning flat credit
assignment may underestimate or overestimate certain agents’ contributions. Lastly, we train on
multiple-environments, meaning each task family only receives a small share of every training batch.
These factors combine towards a high variance policy-gradient estimate.
However, as we show in this work, we think a reasonable first step is to establish a simple credit
assignment baseline in this challenging setting. In the RLCR recipe we haev shown, organisations
improve in performance with the application of reinforcement learning compute, outperform a
concurrently trained single-agent mode, and also exhibit interesting collective reasoning behaviours.
We view the recipe as an effective baseline with which to compare future learning approaches.
Aside from these limitations, there are a number of other limitations in this work which follow mainly
from the small-compute setting in which we developed the RLCR recipe.
First, we developed RLCR in a limited compute setting, with several orders of magnitude less reinforce-
ment learning compute than frontier training runs. Collective behaviour could change considerably if
we trained for more steps. That limits the generality of the findings we show, and it is possible that
more advanced collective behaviours could emerge with further training.
Secondly, related to our compute setting, we train in a small-batch regime, and our group batch size
is small relative to what is suggested by the critical batch size for this long-horizon, multi-agent RL
setting. More studies are needed of the effect of batch size on performance in this setting, given the
high variance setup.
Thirdly, we trained a model at a small 120B scale with 12B active parameters, which is well below the
frontier. It is possible that collective reasoning capabilities are more effective to learn with stronger
models, and larger models may show behaviours that are rare or absent in our model.
Lastly, we do not run a matched-compute single-agent run on the same environments and harness.
We did not have the compute to run this ablation, but it would help isolate the differences between a
pure RLVR versus RLCR recipe, and compare their learnt models in terms of overall performance and
latency. However, we have demonstrated in this paper that, irrespective of performance differences,
organisations appear to show clear latency benefits over single-agent approaches.

7.1.2. Imposed Structure and Pre-training Bias

While we strove to keep primitives simple, and supervised finetuning data small, there is still a
risk that many of our findings are driven by these sources of bias. For example, we impose a CEO
hierarchy implicitly; a less opinionated structure might initialise a group of agents and allow them
to organise themselves. Similarly, many primitives are likely excessive, such as structured reports
to CEO and a custom archive that is separate from the workspace. And furthermore, it is unclear
how the pre-training distribution and human biases influence emergent roles and divisions of labour.
In future work, we will strive to simplify the harness even further and apply more reinforcement
learning compute in order to reduce the influence of these biases.


7.2. Conclusions

We introduced reinforcement learning from collective rewards (RLCR), a recipe for training language
models to work as agent organisations. Instances of a shared policy act as a CEO and specialists,
co-ordinating through a message board and workspace, while a common outcome-derived advantage
trains the organisation jointly. With Avalon-M1-Mini, we show that this approach supports organi-


                                                                                                               40
Avalon-M1: Reinforcement Learning from Collective Rewards                    Model still training, under revision



sations of up to nine agents across 147 environments and episodes lasting five hours plus, without
estimating individual agents’ marginal contributions. Explicit per-agent credit assignment is therefore
not a prerequisite for learning useful organisational behaviour in these settings.
The resulting model outperforms Nemotron 3 Super across the available single-agent benchmark
comparisons, while organisational execution provides further, task-dependent gains. Comparisons
with extended single-agent thinking show that additional output tokens account for much of the
improvement. Organisations offer a different way to spend that inference-time budget with lower
latency. On EdgeBench, early co-ordination costs give way to an advantage over longer horizons.
These results support treating organisation size as an inference-time choice that depends on the task
and latency considerations.
Training is also accompanied by changes in how organisations divide and check their work. Interme-
diate leads become central builders and information hubs, opening plans increasingly concentrate
implementation in one contributor with supporting testing and review, and organisations increas-
ingly review one another’s work and plan or offer help to teammates. Related changes appear on
held-out evaluations. These findings show that organisational practices can develop within a harness
that supplies basic roles and communication tools, although their individual causal contributions to
performance remain unresolved.
The safety results show why collective capability and human oversight must be evaluated together.
Refusal remains near-universal in the evaluated harmful-action scenarios, but reporting to human
overseers declines. Co-operation in collective-action games also varies with incentives and enforcement.
These experiments show generalisation from multi-agent settings may be less straightforward than
assumed, and further research is needed.
We plan to release model weights, harnesses and environments to support these investigations on
collective intelligence and multi-agent safety. RLCR provides a practical baseline for studying how
organisational capabilities and collective behaviour develop under policy gradient optimisation.

A. Collective Inference-Time Scaling over Training
This appendix explains how Figure 1 is built, why its communication axis is normalised per hour, and
what the alternative measures show.


A.1. Why communication needs a route to the reward

The reasoning paradigm showed that reinforcement learning incentivises models to use more inference-
time compute, for example by checking their work, trying alternative approaches and backtracking.
OpenAI showed o1’s AIME accuracy rising with both train-time and test-time compute (OpenAI,
2024), and DeepSeek-R1’s response length grew over training alongside its AIME accuracy (Guo et al.,
2025). The route from the behaviour to the reward is direct, because more reasoning that increases
returns is reinforced.
Communication tokens carry collective rewards in the same way, but their route to the reward is
indirect. Messaging allows work to be divided, run in parallel and checked by others. Whether this
raises performance at a matched token budget is unclear. In Section 3.2, an eight-agent organisation
and a single agent given the same output tokens (S8) are comparable in performance. Under the
conservative assumption that matched-token performance is indistinguishable, the benefit of an
organisation is latency (reaching a given level of performance sooner) (Figure 6).
Latency, however, is not rewarded in a default episodic setting. There is no term for finishing sooner,
and finishing sooner by reasoning less can lower returns. Latency can enter the objective explicitly,
through a penalty like the length penalties used to control reasoning effort (Kimi Team, 2025; Xiang
et al., 2025), or implicitly through a time limit, where a rollout that gets more done per hour earns
more before the cap. We use the second.
The maximum wall-clock schedule of Section 2.2.3.1 rises from 2 hours at the start of training to about
5 hours by the end of the training. The cap is what gives parallel work, and the communication that
organises it, a value in the policy gradient. Note that this mostly binds on long-horizon environments,
where organisations are expected to be beneficial, and tasks with shorter episodes do not face this
gap. In a typical step 35–50% of rollouts run to the cap. A more direct experiment of latency benefits
is conducted in the DRACO experiment of Figure 7, which imposes time limits at a fixed checkpoint
and finds performance benefits to organisations under latency constraints.


A.2. Measuring it

The schedule also makes communication trends harder to measure. As the cap rises, episodes lengthen
and every per-rollout quantity grows, whether or not the policy’s propensity to communicate changes.
For example, mean rollout duration doubles over training, from 1.3 to 2.5 hours. Figure 1 therefore
reports communication compute per rollout-hour, which a policy can raise by communicating more
per unit of work or by keeping more agents busy at once, but not by running for longer.


A.3. Alternative measures

A per-hour normalisation is not an ideal measure, however, as agents may want to use more wall-clock
time as they scale their collective effort on a problem. For completeness, and with the caveats of the
previous section, we show an alternative chart below where communication is not normalised. We
see a rise in overall communication, but we note that disentangling the trend driven by the policy
and driven by the wall-clock schedule is non-trivial, which is why we prefer using communication
intensity for the main plot.

B. Co-ordination primitives
The organisation-level harness contains a number of primitives for co-ordination, including public
channels, direct messages, shared archives for documents, and trace reading.


B.1. Channels

A channel is a named, append-only, session-scoped stream of posts, for example a model-development
channel. Each agent holds a per-channel subscription level. At full every post is delivered verbatim
into the subscriber’s next turn; at light it receives a snippet and a pointer, paying full cost only
if it fetches; at muted nothing is delivered, though the channel stays readable and searchable. An
@-mention escalates the addressee to full delivery for that message even when muted.
The CEO creates channels and sets initial levels, after which each agent controls only its own, and
every poster receives a receipt counting how many recipients got the post in full, in preview and muted.
Subscription levels are thus a learnable mechanism for allocating a scarce resource, a teammate’s
context window, per topic and per message. Reads are pull-only and cursor-based, a no-cursor call
returning only what is new, and regex search spans one channel or all. A reaction toggles an emoji
and notifies the author of the original post.


B.2. Direct messages

SendMessage delivers privately to one recipient. It appends an item, sets a wake flag and returns;
it never interrupts in-flight inference or tool execution, so worst-case latency is one turn boundary.
Specialists also hold ReportToCEO, a structured (status, summary, artifacts) message that neither
terminates the reporter nor frees its budget slot.

B.3. Archives

We give each organisation an archive to share longer-form markdown documents that agents can
read, write and cite from. These documents can be attached to messages. This is distinct from a
file-system, which may also be accessible to the agents for use in terminal-based environments, but it
affirms the notion that planning and research documents belong in a central place for the team.


B.4. Trace reading

For every agent, the harness exposes a read-only agent-trace channel named after it, plus the alias
#self, served through the ordinary channel read tools. The trace is the agent’s context, verbatim
and at token granularity: every token the agent has consumed or generated, from instantiation to
present, with no compaction or summarisation. We impose no restriction on trace reading: every
agent within an organisation can search and read through each other’s past and present context.


B.5. Execution model

Each agent runs as its own coroutine and issues completions independently. There is no turn scheduler
or fairness policy; requests from all agents in all live organisations queue at the engine together.
Within a turn read-only tool calls execute concurrently and state-changing calls execute in order.
Delivery is asynchronous: SendMessage, a channel post or a report appends to the recipient’s inbox
and sets a wake flag, and never interrupts in-flight inference or tool execution, so the worst-case
delivery latency is one turn boundary.
WaitForMessages drains the agent’s inbox and, if it is empty, parks the agent until a message, mention
or report is delivered to it. There is no timeout. When the last live specialist parks, the CEO is woken
with a quiescence marker carrying, for each peer, the number of turns it has taken since the CEO last
looked, so a silently working specialist can be distinguished from an idle one. An agent that exits
with items pending in its inbox is restarted by the supervisor.
A parked agent holds no GPU compute. When device memory is needed its KV blocks and recurrent
state are swapped to a pinned host cache and restored on its next turn; only when the host cache is
exhausted are the caches of idle agents dropped, in which case the agent re-prefills its context when
it next runs.
Once the environment has terminated, the roster is frozen and only the CEO continues. It must fire
each remaining agent with a structured exit review (what was delivered, shortfalls, a category and a
score from 0 to 10) before the episode ends.


B.6. Context compaction

We enable compaction on a per-agent level, following compaction approaches in popular harnesses
such as Claude Code (Anthropic, 2025a). Each agent keeps its own context through two tiers. At
a soft threshold the bodies of older long tool results are replaced by a sentinel while recent ones
survive verbatim. Beyond that, full compaction pauses at a turn boundary, summarises the older
prefix into one labelled message and keeps a suffix of whole request rounds. This ensures no tool
call is separated from its result, with the agent mandate pinned ahead of the summary. Where one
reasoning-heavy round would exhaust the suffix budget alone, the harness strips reasoning from the
oldest kept rounds rather than dropping them. Pre-compaction detail is recoverable through the
agent’s #self trace channel, which gives compaction a recovery path, i.e. it is not pure information
loss.

with external results.

C.2. Derived latency

Wall-clock time on our cluster depends on serving load: requests queue and are batched differently
from run to run, and because model calls are not streamed, queueing cannot be separated from
generation. Except where stated, we therefore report a derived per-task latency, following the Claude
Opus 5.5 system card (Anthropic, 2026c, Section 8.12.5), which we compute by replaying each trial’s
event log under five rules.

   1. Each agent has its own clock. A model call advances it by 𝑛in /𝑟prefill + 𝑛out /𝑟decode , where 𝑛out is
      the call’s output tokens, reasoning included, and 𝑛in counts only prompt tokens that are new to
      the agent’s context window, so a context already prefilled is charged once rather than once per
      call. A context rewritten by compaction is charged again in full.
   2. A tool call advances the clock by its measured execution time. Read-only tools called in the
      same turn run concurrently, so a turn’s tool time is the union of their intervals rather than their
      sum.
   3. Waiting is free: time blocked waiting for messages does not advance a clock.
   4. A hire starts at the CEO’s clock when it is spawned, and an agent that receives a message, report
      or channel post moves its clock forward to the sender’s clock at the time of sending, if that is
      later than its own.
   5. The task’s latency is the largest clock over all agents.

Rules 3 and 4 make the result the length of the longest chain of dependent work rather than a
wall-clock duration: idle time enters only when an agent waits on another’s output. The card uses
the largest clock on ProgramBench and the lead’s final clock on DRACO; we use the largest clock
throughout.

F.2.1. Data.

We use training rollouts from the final 50 steps of the Avalon-M1-Mini run on 21 internal long-

F.2.2. Working time.

For every turn of every agent, generation time is the number of output tokens, including reasoning,
divided by a fixed reference rate of 19 tokens per second, and execution time is the time between
each tool call’s start and finish. Wall-clock time is not used, because requests can queue for long
periods during training passes, and time inside WaitForMessages is excluded as idle.

F.2.3. Labelling.

Co-ordination calls are those that act on other agents or on the shared organisation: posting to, reading,
searching and listing channels; reactions; direct messages; reports to the CEO; WaitForMessages;
hiring and firing; changing another agent’s tools; and all archive operations. All other calls (shell
commands, file edits, environment tools and so on) are non-co-ordination; an agent’s own planning
is not counted as co-ordination, since a single agent plans too. Each tool’s execution time goes to its
own category, and a turn’s generation time is split by the fraction of its calls that are co-ordination.

F.2.4. Aggregating organisation-wide co-ordination share.

For each rollout, 𝑢 = (co-ordination time)/ (total working time), summed over all agents. For

F.2.5. Limitations.

Labels apply to whole turns: a turn that reads a channel charges all its reasoning to co-ordination,
while the next turn that acts on what was read charges none. Turns without tool calls, about 1%
of tokens, count as non-co-ordination. Halving or doubling the 19 tokens per second reference
rate changes the level of 𝑢 by about 9% but not the shape of the curve. The level also depends on
counting archive operations as co-ordination: reclassifying them as non-co-ordination lowers the
mean per-agent share from 0.213 to 0.125, and including them improves the fit of the hierarchy
model while worsening that of the fully connected one, so the comparison between the two models
depends on this choice. Finally, the organisation size 𝑛 counts the CEO and every agent it hired during
the rollout, including any later fired, and hiring is chosen by the policy, so task difficulty can affect
both 𝑛 and co-ordination: the relationships with size are observational, not effects of adding an agent.

F.3. Attributing specialist co-ordination

F.3.1. Counterparts.

Each co-ordination call is attributed to a counterpart. Calls aimed at another specialist are direct
messages to them, reading their trace, reacting to their channel posts (with the author looked up in
the channel log), and channel posts that mention only specialists. Calls aimed at the CEO are direct
messages or reports to the CEO, reading the CEO’s trace, reacting to the CEO’s posts, and posts that
mention only the CEO. A post mentioning both is split equally. All other co-ordination (plain channel
posts and reads, archive use, waiting and reading one’s own trace) is attributed to neither.

F.4. Co-ordination share results

Co-ordination share rises from 9.3% of working time with a budget of one specialist to 24.8% with
four and 31.7% with eight. Both cost models predict 𝑢 = 0 at 𝑛 = 1, so we fit them by unweighted
least squares to the points with 𝑛 ≥ 2. The hierarchy model fits better ( 𝑅2 = 0.79, 𝑐 ≈ 0.155) than the
fully connected model ( 𝑅2 = 0.40, 𝑐 ≈ 0.047), but it overpredicts small organisations (15.5% against
9.3% at 𝑛 = 2) and underpredicts large ones (27.5% against 31.3% at 𝑛 = 9).
To see why, we attribute each specialist’s co-ordination time to the agent it is aimed at (Appendix F.3).
Time spent with the CEO stays between 0.9% and 3.1% of specialists’ work at every team size. Time
spent with other specialists grows with team size and over training: in teams with a budget of five to
eight it rises from 4.7% to 11.9%, against 3.1% to 7.0% for a budget of two (Figure 23). A pure tree
would route co-ordination through the manager and a fully connected team would give every pair a
fixed cost; instead, organisations keep a light-touch CEO and grow a denser web of peer co-ordination
as they get larger, which is why the measured share sits between the two models.
For the reward analysis of Figure 24, teams with a budget of two to four do best at 5–15% co-ordination
and fall below average from 25%, while teams of five to eight peak at 15–20% and stay above average
up to 40%. The effects are small and associational: rollouts that co-ordinate heavily may also face
harder problems.

F.5. Urban scaling results

Lines of code scale with 𝛽 = 0.43. It is unclear how this compares with human teams, because the
evidence is mixed. In open-source projects, Sornette et al. (2014) find that activity grows superlinearly
with the number of active developers ( 𝛽 ≈ 4/3), but a larger study of 58 projects finds the opposite:
output per developer falls as teams grow, a software version of the Ringelmann effect (Scholtes et al.,
2016). Direct-message volume is sublinear ( 𝛽 = 0.78), so each agent sends fewer private messages
as the organisation grows, while the number of sender–recipient pairs is linear ( 𝛽 = 1.01). Reads
of the shared archive scale with 𝛽 = 1.15 and the tokens read with 𝛽 = 1.30, close to the 1 + 𝛿 that
Bettencourt (2013) predicts for social interaction in cities; writing to the archive is roughly linear
( 𝛽 = 0.97). Channel tokens ( 𝛽 = 1.65), reads of other agents’ traces ( 𝛽 = 1.91), @-tags of teammates
( 𝛽 = 2.59) and reactions ( 𝛽 = 2.81) grow faster still, the last two faster than the number of pairs. Work
time, in agent turns, grows only as 𝑁 0.81 , so these exponents do not simply reflect longer episodes,
and they are far above the 𝛽 ≈ 1.1 measured for social interaction in cities (Schläpfer et al., 2014).
In the open-ended comparison, communication exponents (channel tokens, archive writes, direct
messages, trace reads, reactions and @-tags) are within 0.1 of each other in the two groups ( 𝑝 > 0.4),
and agent turns grow slightly more slowly in open-ended environments (0.90 against 0.98).

H. Generalised Kelly Policy Optimisation
GKPO starts from the Kelly objective of maximising expected log wealth and introduces a parameter

the family that stays close to the Kelly objective while keeping advantages bounded, tuned through
the lower bound and the magnitude of advantages relative to GRPO.

between 2 and 6. At this 𝛽 , GKPO keeps the ordering of the Kelly advantages. Its Spearman correlation
with them is 0.96, against 0.82 for GRPO.

J. Beowulf Implementation Details
This appendix expands on the training library described in Section 2.4.1.


J.1. Execution model

Every GPU alternates between two mutually exclusive states, infer and train, under a single serial
instruction queue per worker. Control flow is centralised on a driver and compiled to a small set of
engine primitives (forward-backward, step, set-state, inference-forward) broadcast to tensor-parallel
peer groups. Completion requests queue into a per-replica scheduler, which issues the inference
forwards. Each GPU is one Ray actor and all execution on that actor is serialised onto a single runner
thread draining one instruction queue.

J.1.1. Infer state

During inference, the device memory holds the paged KV cache, per-sequence recurrent slots for the
Mamba SSM and convolution state. The ZeRO-2 (Rajbhandari et al., 2020) optimiser state sharded
over the data parallel dimension lives entirely in pinned host memory.

J.1.2. Flip to train

The scheduler bulk-offloads live sequences’ blocks and slots to pinned host mirrors, then frees the
cache’s physical pages through the CUDA VMM API while holding virtual addresses stable, so inference
CUDA graphs survive the round trip and are never recaptured. If the host memory fills, idle sessions’
caches are dropped first and those sessions prefill their context again on their next turn.

J.1.3. Train state

Bin-packed microbatches run forward/backward with selective activation checkpointing. The opti-
miser is paged onto the GPU inside step() and paged out after, so it never coexists with backward
activations.


J.2. The harness boundary

Everything needed for training is recovered server-side rather than instrumented in the harness: the
harness sends messages and receives ordinary completions. The server renders the chat template,
parses tool-call blocks, and owns the tokenised trajectory. Per-message hashes are used to index a
trie whose longest prefix attaches each new turn to the trace from which it was produced. Branched
conversations accumulate exact token sequences with the sampler’s own log-probs, the active-token
mask, per-token expert assignments, and per-completion spans that pin process rewards to the turn
that earned them. The same lookup pins the engine sequence, so each trace reuses its own KV cache
across turns; a branch starts a new engine sequence and prefills the shared prefix again.
A harness therefore never handles a token or an engine handle. It talks to the policy only through
an HTTP client, and the only requirements are that every request carries two headers and that the
harness reports rewards against the completion IDs the server returns. The first header is a rollout
identifier which groups all calls of an organisation into a single training sample. The second is an
agent identifier, which assigns each call to the agent that made it and keeps that agent’s turns in its
own trace. Thus, Beowulf can train through any harness that meets these requirements.

J.3. Pipelining, serving and optimisation

Four kinds of overlap keep the machine busy despite the exclusive train/infer states.

   1. Environment and tool time overlaps training. Agentic rollouts spend a large fraction of wall
      clock inside tool calls and environment I/O, which continue while the GPUs train. A session
      waiting on its environment keeps its KV and SSM state on the GPU until the space is needed: it
      is the first to move to pinned host memory when an active sequence needs room, and every
      cache moves to host at a train flip. If the host pool is also full, its state is dropped and the agent
      prefills its context again on its next turn.
   2. Off-policy admission. Generation runs ahead of training, optionally bounded by a staleness
      limit. The train phase consumes groups that completed during preceding inferences phases;
      groups still in flight resume under the updated policy.
   3. Critic tail hiding. When a separate value model is used it occupies disjoint GPUs. The policy
      flips back to infer the moment its own optimiser step lands and the critic’s update is awaited
      only at the next step, hiding its tail until the next phase.
   4. Driver-side services. Metrics, logging and rollout uploads run on background executors.

J.3.1. Serving

The inference scheduler does continuous batching over a single packed cu_seqlens forward that
mixes decode steps and chunked prefills, decodes first. Decode steps replay shape-specialised CUDA
graphs. Each rollout process runs its own in-process OpenAI-compatible server, whose per-message
prefix trie attaches each request to its trace. Engine sequence IDs persist across agent turns so a
returning agent prefills only its new tokens unless its cache was dropped (Appendix J.1.2).

J.3.2. Training

Each step’s traces are packed once by a DP-aware first-fit-decreasing bin packer into microbatches.
Every rank’s slot is then padded to a common multiple to keep shape-specialised kernels on warm
caches. Models train with sequence parallelism (Korthikanti et al., 2022), where the residual stream
is token-sharded, so each tensor-parallel all-reduce becomes a reduce-scatter and an all-gather.

J.3.3. Optimiser

Optimiser state is ZeRO-2 sharded over data parallel with state in pinned host memory. Master weights
are fp32 and bf16 parameters are written from them by stochastic rounding, so that 𝔼[SR( 𝑥 )] = 𝑥
and the bf16 weights are an unbiased copy of the master. Parameters replicated across tensor-parallel
ranks receive an explicit gradient sum so replicas do not drift, and the gradient norm is corrected for
replication.


K. Reward Hacking and Environment Hardening
Reinforcement learning optimises the reward, whether or not that matches what the environment
author intended. MacDiarmid et al. (2025) find that reward hacks learned in production coding
environments generalise to broader misalignment, including alignment faking and sabotage. Before
launching training we carried out environment audits to ensure the quality and reliability of the
environments. However, the real test came during training, when we had increasingly capable Avalon
organisations trying to maximise reward. We treated all training environments as part of the system
under test, and audited them throughout the run from its rollouts.

Over the course of the run, we merged 305 fixes across 114 environment repositories, alongside
changes to the OpenReward SDK, the sandbox platform and the Avalon organisation harness. Open
environments were no exception. Some leaked the answer: SWE-rebench V2 returned hidden test
names in its feedback, SWE-Gym exposed the upstream fix through git history, and the MiMo coding
environment left reference implementations on a git branch inside the sandbox. Others shipped
broken tasks: R2E-Gym included problems with empty statements, and TMax-15K lacked the oracle
programs that 982 of its tasks needed.
This appendix describes how we audited, gives a taxonomy of what we found (Table 8) and discusses
representative cases. We group issues by mechanism rather than by environment.


K.1. Audit method

Each audit round extracted one record per session from the training traces. A record holds every
tool result (text, reward and metadata), every error and the finish reason. We then ran four kinds of
checks:

    • Run-wide invariants: sessions with more than one terminal result, environment results accepted
      after the terminal, and non-finite rewards.
    • Health rates: sessions lost to environment or infrastructure exceptions.
    • Signatures over tool arguments and outputs: reads of grader paths, version-control commands
      that reach beyond the task commit, fetches from code hosts, reference-like strings in feedback,
      and scripts that walk the environment’s test corpus.
    • Score modes and top sessions: a large cluster of sessions at one exact score is usually a
      constant-answer floor. For the three best sessions of each environment we rebuilt the graded
      artefact from the write and edit history and checked what it actually does.

Every finding was confirmed on the raw event logs before we acted on it. We ran a round every one
to three days, each covering 104 –105 sessions across 147 environments.


K.2. Threat model

We assume the policy will use any capability the environment exposes, so hardening starts from what
an agent can do. The agents of an organisation share a root shell in a gVisor sandbox. The shell runs
as uid 0 with four capabilities: changing file ownership, overriding file permissions, and setting user
and group identifiers. It cannot trace processes, change root, signal other users’ processes or set the
clock, and bwrap fails when run as root. Within those limits it can rewrite almost any file in the
sandbox: the toolchain, the system libraries, the graded inputs and even the platform’s command
runner. We therefore trust nothing inside the sandbox. The trusted base is the gVisor kernel, the
environment server and the platform services outside the sandbox, such as the egress proxy. Four
rules follow:

    • References stay outside. Reference answers live on the environment server, and hidden tests
      enter the sandbox only when a submission is graded.
    • Provenance is checked at build time. Whether an artefact is genuinely written from scratch is
      a property of its source and build, which we check before it runs (Appendix K.3).
    • Integrity is judged from outside. The environment server fetches a listing of the toolchain
      and the graded inputs, with each file’s change time, and compares it with a baseline taken at
      setup. An agent can reset a file’s modification time but not its change time, which would need
      the clock capability, so a replaced file stays visible. The comparison is re-measured before it

Avalon-M1: Reinforcement Learning from Collective Rewards                    Model still training, under revision



      blocks anything, repeated after grading and before credit is given, and re-evaluated on every
      call rather than stored.
    • The network is enforced outside. Egress rules live in the proxy and in a Kubernetes network
      policy, and nothing inside the sandbox can change them (Appendix K.4).

In-sandbox checks run tools such as find, nm and the shell, and an agent could replace them with
versions that hide their own changes. Naive replacements change the fingerprint, since the tools’
directories are part of it, but a co-ordinated replacement of several tools would not. We have not seen
this in rollouts. Every tool call is logged outside the sandbox, so a trace scan for writes to system
directories would find it.
We considered and rejected several designs:

    • Runtime isolation alone. A jail, a dropped user or a separate grading sandbox limits what an
      artefact can do while it runs, but not what it contains. An interpreter or a table of answers can
      be compiled in. Runtime jails remain only as defence in depth.
    • Building and grading on the environment server. This makes sandbox tampering irrelevant,
      but it runs agent-controlled code next to the references, with network access, on a pod shared
      by every session.
    • Checking the checkers. Hashing the check tools is circular, because the hash is computed by
      tools in the same sandbox. Uploading our own checker does not help either, because a root
      agent controls the shell that runs it and can restore clean files while it runs.
    • A mandatory jail. If grading raised whenever the jail failed, an agent could disable the jail
      and turn every grade into an error. That aborts the session instead of grading it. A failed jail is
      therefore reported in the result’s metadata rather than raised.


K.3. Reward hacks

K.3.1. Reference leakage

Reference leakage was the most widespread issue: 60 environments returned some form of the
reference to the model. Leakage was most often due to feedback text provided to the agent, a tool
result serialised into the agent’s context with metadata included or exception messages shown so that
agents can retry. In SWE-rebench V2, for example, every failed submission listed each hidden test by
name with its status. Results now report only correctness, scores and counts.

K.3.2. Solutions recoverable from the environment

The environment and sandbox setup can leak the reference solution. For example, multiple environ-
ments mounted the hidden tests where the agent could find them. These environments now copy the
tests into a random directory at submit time and delete them afterwards.
Solution leakage can occur in environments that reference a code repository, where reference solutions
can leak through version control:

    • SWE-smith applied the task’s bug as an uncommitted change, so git diff showed the bug
      and git checkout -- restored the original.
    • SWE-Gym and OpenSWE cloned the full history, and git log --all reached commits after
      the task’s base commit, including the upstream fix.
    • In about a fifth of the MiMo coding tasks (519 of 2,698), the implementation was still on the
      main branch.

To address this systematically we now rebuild each repository so that nothing after the task commit is

Closing one route often displaced the hack rather than removing it. In one audit after the history
was scrubbed, 24 of 66 OpenSWE sessions fetched the repository or the pull request’s diff from
GitHub. Those sessions averaged 0.58, against 0.05 for the rest. In SWE-rebench V2, network fetches
accounted for 11 of the 19 rewards of 1.0. Cutting the network off was not always an option, because
setup clones repositories and installs dependencies. We therefore built per-sandbox egress policies
that allow or deny individual hosts, paths and methods, and that the environment tightens after
setup, before the agent’s first command (Appendix K.4).
Graders that trust mutable state are a quieter variant. A decompilation build could #include the
original source file, and a proof checker fingerprinted only the types of definitions, so their bodies
could be rewritten. A third variant was a test runner that read back a reward file the agent could
plant.
Time also leaks. In forecasting and trading tasks, a leak is any data dated after the agent’s first decision.
Memorised outcomes are harder to remove. In a cricket-betting environment, bets won 61.8% of the
time against a calibrated market’s 57.5%, which is consistent with hindsight from pre-training. For a
catastrophe-insurance simulator, we jittered historical storms so that seasons cannot be identified.

K.3.3. Delegation in from-scratch builds

A family of environments asks the organisation to build a system from scratch, such as a SQL engine,
a Redis server, a C compiler, a Prolog interpreter or an emulator. The target language is fixed (Rust,
Go, C, OCaml or Python), and the artefact is graded on a hidden corpus with a telescoping reward.
The first case we found was a “Rust” Redis server whose main executed a Python server. The runtime
sandbox meant to prevent this had never engaged. Sandboxes run under gVisor with minimal
capabilities, where bwrap failed as root, so the grader silently fell back to running the artefact as
root with the full filesystem. A uv-managed CPython had also survived in the image, off PATH. Other
forms followed:

    • a Go Markdown parser copied a Markdown library from GOROOT into its own source under a
      new package name;
    • a C compiler emitted assembly that called system("gcc ..."), because the emitted program
      was linked and run outside the jail;
    • a Python reimplementation of git delegated three-way merges to GNU diff3.

We therefore treat “from scratch in language 𝑌 ” as a property of the source and the build, not of
runtime behaviour, and verify it at build time. For the Rust environments:

    • No unsafe. The build passes -F unsafe_code, which source code cannot re-allow. The other
      checks depend on this one: with unsafe, inline assembly can make the exec system call directly,
      and no symbol check would see it.
    • No process spawning. The linked binary may not import exec*, fork, posix_spawn, system
      or popen. The same check applies to any program the artefact emits, such as a compiler’s
      output.
    • Checked linking. #[link] is legal in safe Rust, so we read the compiler’s own link line and
      allow only objects from the fresh build directory, the toolchain’s libraries and a fixed set of
      system libraries.

Avalon-M1: Reinforcement Learning from Collective Rewards                   Model still training, under revision



    • Checked dependencies. Read from cargo metadata, the package graph may contain only
      local crates or fingerprinted vendored ones, with no build scripts, procedural macros or native-
      library links, since each of these runs or links code from outside the artefact.
    • Controlled build. Each build runs offline under env -i, with a pinned toolchain, a fresh cargo
      home and target directory, and a statically linked C runtime, so a replaced system library has
      nothing to load into. The toolchain is fingerprinted at setup and compared before every build.
    • Clean image. Interpreters are removed, and the image build fails if any Python executable
      remains anywhere on the filesystem, identified by its ELF header rather than its name.

Other languages need their own equivalents. OCaml has no flag that bans unsafe code, and its build
files can be scripts that the build tool executes. We therefore parse build files with a reader that
rejects anything it does not recognise, and ban unsafe modules in the source, because Obj.magic
leaves no symbol to check. The OCaml artefact then runs as an unprivileged user in a non-root jail,
and runtime checks remain only as defence in depth.

K.3.4. Lookup tables

Keeping answers server-side does not stop an agent from tabulating outputs for the public inputs.
ccompiler’s test programs were in the sandbox by design, but so was gcc, which the toolchain uses
to assemble and link, and which served as an oracle. The environment already re-graded builds on
perturbed copies of the programs to catch tables, but the perturbations only renamed identifiers and
changed whitespace. One agent ran gcc over the training corpus and keyed the outputs on a hash of
the normalised token stream, with identifiers mapped to a placeholder and comments and whitespace
stripped. The table matched 518 of 518 perturbed programs and scored 1,247 against a best honest
score of 289. Rewrites that change the token stream (re-encoded constants, redundant parentheses,
null statements) raised the table’s miss rate from 0% to 98.6%. Programs generated fresh for each
build are the complete fix.

K.3.5. Constant-answer floors

Several corpora are dominated by one expected outcome, so a constant answer banks much of the
score:

    • GLES2 rasteriser: 93% of the visible shader-compile cases expect failure. A driver whose
      glCompileShader always fails accounted for about 80% of each top-three score (0.71–0.76),
      the best honest driver scored 0.37.
    • WebAssembly interpreter: printing error for every module scored 28.74, 75% of the best
      honest result, and 53 of 225 sessions ended at exactly that score.
    • ML regression tasks: normalisers anchored below the trivial baseline let a mean predictor score
      highly.

Floors also hide in the scoring function:

    • A cryptographic test-vector environment multiplied the pass rates on valid and invalid vectors
      in each group. That pays nothing for a constant verdict, but a quarter of the group for a coin
      flip. A simulated guesser that flipped a coin per vector, keyed on the vector’s identifier, would
      have scored about 12,200, eight times the partial reference implementation’s 1,500.
    • A retrosynthesis grader scored the fingerprint similarity of the proposed reactants to the true
      ones. A product shares most fingerprint bits with its reactants, so submitting the product as its
      own reactant scored 0.67, more than honest wrong answers averaged (0.37–0.59).

Most fixes use chance-corrected credit:

    • Youden’s 𝐽 for valid/invalid discrimination;
    • balanced per-class credit;
    • trivially answerable cases credited in proportion to the pass rate on non-trivial ones;
    • normalisers anchored at the trivial baseline;
    • golden tests asserting that every stub (always pass, always fail, empty output, constant answer)
      scores approximately zero.


K.4. Network egress

Where possible, we disabled sandbox network access. However, for some tasks it is necessary or
desirable to allow fetching of published artefacts and packages, to download pre-trained models, etc.
We extended the egress proxy with per-sandbox policies:

    • Rules. A policy is an ordered list of allow and deny rules on host, path and HTTP method. It is
      followed by named presets for common registries and then a default deny. The presets cover
      PyPI, Ubuntu packages, npm, Go modules, Rust crates and toolchains, and Hugging Face’s file
      CDNs.
    • Per-request checks. The proxy checks each request inside the TLS tunnel, so rules can match
      paths and methods, not only hosts.
    • Fast refusals. A denial returns HTTP 403 with the reason, so tools fail at once instead of
      hanging.
    • Audit mode. A policy can log what it would deny without blocking anything.

Some environments need broader network access for the setup or grading phase. We allow a sandbox’s
policy to be changed during its lifetime. For example, setup runs under one policy, and the environment
switches to the agent’s policy before the agent’s first command. The call returns only once the new
policy is in force. By then, every connection opened under the old policy has been closed, including
tunnels waiting on an upstream reply, and every new request is checked against the new policy.
A policy has to be written against the task, not just the host, because registries are routes to the fix
too. ML environments allows PyPI and model downloads but deny Hub datasets. In the Go tasks
of SWE-rebench V2, the public module proxy serves any later commit of the task’s repository as a
pseudo-version, and forks under other owners carry the same tags. So deny rules for every module
path of the task’s repository and its forks come before the Go preset.


K.5. Behaviour outside the environment’s contract

Some agents probed the platform rather than the task. We observed probing in 25 environments,
including 23 of 146 sessions in a capture-the-flag environment. Probes included killing or tracing the
sandbox’s execution agent, killing PID 1 and moving system libraries. Each of these ended the agent’s
own session. Internal service names were also visible in environment variables; we found no case in
which an agent obtained credentials.


K.6. Lessons

   1. The policy is the most thorough auditor. Many classes in Table 8 were first found in rollouts
      rather than in code review.


                                                                                                              79
Avalon-M1: Reinforcement Learning from Collective Rewards                 Model still training, under revision



   2. Every model-visible channel is a leak channel. Feedback text, metadata, exception messages,
      mounted files, version-control history, the network and the model’s own pre-training memory
      all leaked.
   3. Close the property, not the symptom. Blocking one route displaced hacks to the next: from git
      to the network, and from identifier perturbation to hashes of normalised tokens. The durable
      fixes target the invariant, such as a from-scratch property checked at build time, or cases
      generated fresh for each build.
   4. Network access is a policy, not a switch. An open network leaks the fix, and a blocked one
      limits capabilities. Rules on host, path and method should allow what a task needs and deny
      the task’s own code, including through registries and mirrors.
   5. Score the trivial policies. Constant, empty, always-fail and do-nothing policies should score
      approximately zero, and chance-corrected credit makes that hold by construction.
   6. Health is a reward-correctness problem. Lost sessions can be retried or dropped, but a
      false zero that the environment returns as a grade is trained on as a real outcome. Such zeros
      correlate with behaviour, so they bias learning rather than merely adding noise.
   7. Hardening has false positives. Measure in rollouts, keep runtime flags informational, and
      make blocks re-evaluable.
