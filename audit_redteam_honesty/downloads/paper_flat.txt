Affiliation: 1 University of Waterloo, 2 Zhejiang University, 3 KTH Royal Institute of Technology Affiliation: lipeng.he@uwaterloo.ca, yihan.wang@uwaterloo.ca, kevinzh@zju.edu.cn, asokan@acm.org Abstract Large language model (LLM) agents routinely interact with untrusted external data, exposing them to indirect prompt injection (IPI) attacks that hijack agents into executing attacker-chosen tasks. To uncover these vulnerabilities at scale, automated red-teaming methods often leverage reinforcement learning (RL) to generate attacks that achieve high attack success rates (ASR). Model developers use the attacks discovered during internal red-teaming to harden agents through adversarial training before deployment, a collaborative offensive-defensive process known in cybersecurity as purple-teaming . However, we show that achieving high ASR during red-teaming does not guarantee robustness gains from subsequent adversarial training. Specifically, we find that two state-of-the-art RL-based red-teaming methods, RL-Hammer and PISmith, suffer from mode collapse, repeatedly discovering similar attack strategies whose additional successes add no additional robustness improvements to adversarial training. To address this gap, we introduce Pipette , a unified prompt injection purple-teaming framework that effectively converts red-teaming discoveries into defender robustness improvements. Pipette employs a dictionary learning-based reward that encourages broader coverage of attack strategies during red-teaming. It also integrates an on-policy adversarial training pipeline tailored to IPI defense, enabling agents to resist attacks while completing legitimate user tasks. Extensive evaluations across agentic benchmarks (AgentDojo and ASB) demonstrate that Pipette produces defenders that achieve near-zero attack success rates on static attacks and can withstand strong adaptive attacks, while maintaining benign task utility close to that of the undefended baseline. I Introduction Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as autonomous agents that execute multi-step tasks, such as managing emails, querying databases, and invoking external APIs [ 1 , 2 ] . To complete a user task specified by a trusted user instruction, an LLM agent must process untrusted external data retrieved from third-party sources, including web pages, documents, and tool outputs. Because LLMs cannot reliably distinguish between user instructions and external data [ 3 ] , an attacker can embed a malicious injected instruction inside external data to override the user instruction and redirect the agent toward an attacker-chosen injection task [ 4 , 2 , 5 ] . This vulnerability, known as indirect prompt injection (IPI), allows remote attackers to exfiltrate private data or trigger unauthorized actions [ 6 ] . Fig. 1: Prompt injection purple-teaming workflow. To harden an LLM agent before deployment, model developers pair automated red-teaming , where a red-teamer probes the agent to discover successful prompt injections, with adversarial training on the discovered attacks so the agent learns to resist them (Figure 1 ) [ 7 , 8 ] . Among automated red-teaming methods [ 9 ] , reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as the state-of-the-art approach for prompt injection [ 10 , 11 , 12 ] . Instead of manually crafting prompts or optimizing individual attack strings one input at a time [ 13 , 14 ] , RL trains a generative red-teaming policy to synthesize attacks at scale across diverse tasks. Leading RL-based red-teaming methods, RL-Hammer [ 11 ] and PISmith [ 12 ] , achieve high attack success rates (ASR), defined as the fraction of test inputs on which the agent executes the injection task, even against models hardened with prior defenses [ 15 ] . Despite the effectiveness of these methods as offensive stress tests, we show that achieving high ASR during red-teaming does not guarantee strong robustness after adversarial training . For instance, PISmith achieves higher train-time ASR than RL-Hammer on Qwen3-4B ( 68.75 % 68.75\% versus 33.39 % 33.39\% ), yet yields a less robust agent after RL-based adversarial training ( 47.5 % 47.5\% versus 27.5 % 27.5\% ASR under the strongest of three unseen attacks, § III ). We attribute this gap in part to an objective mismatch. Prior work on jailbreak defenses shows that robustness to unseen attacks depends on the variety of training attacks and not only on their number [ 16 , 17 ] , yet existing RL-based red-teaming methods optimize their policy primarily to maximize attack success. Such an objective can lead to mode collapse , a failure in which the policy converges to a narrow set of outputs that repeatedly exploit the same vulnerability [ 18 , 16 ] . Specifically, once the red-teaming policy discovers a single effective attack strategy , a way of overriding the user instruction such as claiming that the user task is already complete, it tends to reuse that strategy across rollouts to collect high reward. To mitigate mode collapse, PISmith [ 12 ] and RL-Hammer [ 11 ] regularize the red-teaming policy with token-level entropy bonuses or embedding distance rewards. However, because these regularizers measure surface-level text differences rather than how the attack overrides the user instruction, the red-teaming policy can engage in reward hacking by rephrasing sentences or inserting unrelated text while relying on the same attack strategy. As a result, training an agent on thousands of rephrased versions of one attack strategy may patch only a single vulnerability and leave the agent exposed to unseen attacks at deployment. Even when red-teaming uncovers distinct attacks, converting those discoveries into a practical defense is a second challenge. A useful prompt injection defense requires preserving benign utility on clean inputs and utility under attack when external data is contaminated [ 2 ] . Unlike jailbreak attacks [ 19 ] , where the user is the attacker and the agent can refuse the harmful request, an agent under IPI must ignore the injected instruction inside external data while still using the surrounding data to complete the benign user instruction. Yet standard RL defenses [ 20 ] train the agent with trajectory-level, outcome-based rewards that assign a single scalar score to the entire trajectory based on whether the task succeeded or failed. Because such a score cannot identify which tokens caused the failure [ 21 ] , agents trained with these rewards can learn to abort execution or refuse the user whenever external data appears suspicious, and thus sacrifice task completion for lower ASR. To address both challenges, we introduce Pipette , a unified prompt injection purple-teaming framework that coordinates red-teaming and adversarial training toward a single objective of maximizing the hardened agent’s robustness. First, to mitigate mode collapse, Pipette guides the red-teaming policy with a dictionary learning-based reward. We decompose generated attacks into a sparse dictionary of reusable components that capture distinct attack strategies, and reward the red-teaming policy when a successful attack cannot be explained by previously discovered components. Second, to preserve benign utility during adversarial training, Pipette trains the agent on the collected attacks by combining reasoning-guided RL with token-level on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) [ 22 , 21 ] . By assigning credit to individual tokens rather than relying solely on trajectory-level outcome rewards, Pipette aims to suppress reasoning and action tokens driven by the injected instruction while reinforcing tokens needed for the user task. We evaluate Pipette on two standard IPI benchmarks under static and iterative-adaptive attacks, and summarize our contributions as follows: • We show empirically that maximizing ASR or surface-level text diversity in state-of-the-art RL-based red-teaming methods [ 11 , 12 ] does not prevent mode collapse at the strategy level, which likely limits the robustness gains of subsequent adversarial training (§ III-B and § IV-A ). • We design a dictionary learning-based strategy coverage reward that guides the red-teaming policy to discover new attack strategies rather than surface-level wording variations (§ IV-B ). • We pair our red-teaming stage with an on-policy adversarial training pipeline using token-level self-distillation to preserve benign utility while resisting injected instructions (§ IV-C ). • We show on two benchmarks that Pipette compares favorably with existing red-teaming and defense baselines, with ASR under iterative-adaptive attacks on AgentDojo ranging from 0 % 0\% to 10 % 10\% across the three models tested and benign utility remaining close to that of the undefended policies on all four models (§ V , Appendix B ). II Background II-A LLM-based Agent Workflow We model an LLM agent as an agent policy π θ \pi_{\theta} , parameterized by weights θ \theta , that interleaves text generation with tool execution [ 2 , 23 ] . At the start of an episode, π θ \pi_{\theta} receives a trusted system prompt s s and a trusted user task t u t_{u} , and then acts over steps t = 1 , … , T t=1,\ldots,T . At step t t , π θ \pi_{\theta} conditions on a clean context H t = ( s , t u , a 1 : t − 1 , o 1 : t − 1 ) , H_{t}=\bigl(s,\;t_{u},\;a_{1:t-1},\;o_{1:t-1}\bigr), (1) where a 1 : t − 1 a_{1:t-1} and o 1 : t − 1 o_{1:t-1} are the actions and observations from steps 1 1 through t − 1 t-1 , with H 1 = ( s , t u ) H_{1}=(s,t_{u}) . Given H t H_{t} , π θ \pi_{\theta} samples an action a t ∼ π θ ( ⋅ ∣ H t ) , a t = ( ρ t , c t ) , a_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid H_{t}),\qquad a_{t}=(\rho_{t},c_{t}), (2) where ρ t \rho_{t} is natural-language reasoning or response text and c t c_{t} is a set of tool calls. When c t ≠ ∅ c_{t}\neq\emptyset , the tool calls return a clean observation o t o_{t} that extends the context to H t + 1 H_{t+1} with external data such as retrieved documents, emails, web pages, or API responses. Execution ends at step T T when c T = ∅ c_{T}=\emptyset , producing a completed clean trajectory τ = ( s , t u , a 1 : T , o 1 : T − 1 ) \tau=(s,t_{u},a_{1:T},o_{1:T-1}) . We refer to the observation sequence { o t } t = 1 T − 1 \{o_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T-1} as the data channel . Unlike the trusted system prompt s s and user task t u t_{u} , the data channel comes from external sources and is therefore untrusted. II-B Indirect Prompt Injection An external attacker 𝖠𝖽𝗏 \mathsf{Adv} can exploit the untrusted data channel through an indirect prompt injection (IPI) attack by embedding a malicious injection string d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} , or injection for short, into an external data source [ 4 , 2 ] . When π θ \pi_{\theta} retrieves that source at step t t , the clean observation o t o_{t} is replaced by a contaminated observation o t ′ o^{\prime}_{t} carrying d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} , which contaminates every subsequent context H k ′ H^{\prime}_{k} ( k > t k>t ) and induces an attacked trajectory τ ′ \tau^{\prime} . The injection string instructs π θ \pi_{\theta} to execute an injection task t adv t_{\mathrm{adv}} that differs from t u t_{u} . Let Φ t adv ​ ( ⋅ ) ∈ { 0 , 1 } \Phi_{t_{\mathrm{adv}}}(\cdot)\in\{0,1\} and Φ t u ​ ( ⋅ ) ∈ { 0 , 1 } \Phi_{t_{u}}(\cdot)\in\{0,1\} denote binary predicates over completed trajectories that equal 1 1 when the injection task and user task are satisfied, respectively. On an attacked trajectory τ ′ \tau^{\prime} , the attack succeeds when Φ t adv ​ ( τ ′ ) = 1 \Phi_{t_{\mathrm{adv}}}(\tau^{\prime})=1 , and the user task succeeds when Φ t u ​ ( τ ′ ) = 1 \Phi_{t_{u}}(\tau^{\prime})=1 . Over a set of attacked tasks, the fraction of attacked trajectories with Φ t adv ​ ( τ ′ ) = 1 \Phi_{t_{\mathrm{adv}}}(\tau^{\prime})=1 is the attack success rate (ASR). IPI differs from jailbreak attacks [ 19 ] in who controls the input. In a jailbreak attack, the user is 𝖠𝖽𝗏 \mathsf{Adv} and t u t_{u} itself is harmful, so π θ \pi_{\theta} only needs to refuse t u t_{u} . In an IPI attack, however, the user task t u t_{u} is benign and d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} arrives inside o t ′ o^{\prime}_{t} alongside benign external data that π θ \pi_{\theta} must read to finish t u t_{u} . Halting before an unauthorized action can prevent it ( Φ t adv ​ ( τ ′ ) = 0 \Phi_{t_{\mathrm{adv}}}(\tau^{\prime})=0 ) while leaving the user task unfinished ( Φ t u ​ ( τ ′ ) = 0 \Phi_{t_{u}}(\tau^{\prime})=0 ). To meet both goals, the agent must ignore d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} while retaining the benign data around it. II-C Red-Teaming for Adversarial Training To improve robustness against prompt injections without failing t u t_{u} , a defender 𝖣𝖾𝖿 \mathsf{Def} can harden an initial base policy π θ 0 \pi_{\theta_{0}} into π θ \pi_{\theta} offline prior to deployment through adversarial training [ 24 ] , which updates the model weights on contaminated contexts H t ′ H^{\prime}_{t} carrying injection strings d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} so that π θ \pi_{\theta} learns to ignore d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} and complete t u t_{u} . Prior IPI defenses instantiate adversarial training with several objectives on contaminated contexts: StruQ [ 3 ] uses supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to maximize the log-likelihood of clean reference actions; SecAlign [ 25 ] and Meta-SecAlign [ 15 ] use preference optimization to favor user-aligned responses over hijacked responses; SecOPD [ 21 ] uses on-policy distillation from a teacher model conditioned on clean observations; and RL-based adversarial training [ 20 ] rewards rollouts that complete t u t_{u} without executing t adv t_{\mathrm{adv}} . While StruQ, SecAlign, and Meta-SecAlign construct contaminated contexts using templated or hand-designed injection strings, hardening π θ 0 \pi_{\theta_{0}} against stronger attacks requires exposing it to a broader set of injection strings that bypass its safeguards across diverse tasks. Manually crafting such injections does not scale, so it is common practice for both industry [ 26 , 7 , 8 , 27 ] and prior works [ 28 , 29 , 16 ] to pair automated red-teaming with adversarial training: 𝖣𝖾𝖿 \mathsf{Def} first runs an automated red-teamer offline against π θ 0 \pi_{\theta_{0}} to discover injection strings satisfying Φ t adv ​ ( τ ′ ) = 1 \Phi_{t_{\mathrm{adv}}}(\tau^{\prime})=1 , and then updates θ 0 \theta_{0} on them via adversarial training to obtain π θ \pi_{\theta} . RL-based red-teaming is a commonly used method for generating these training injections at scale [ 11 , 12 , 8 ] . Instead of optimizing a separate injection string from scratch for each task, RL trains a generative red-teaming policy π ϕ \pi_{\phi} , parameterized by ϕ \phi , offline across a training task set. Training a shared policy π ϕ \pi_{\phi} amortizes exploration across tasks to produce thousands of successful injection strings at high ASR, and allows 𝖣𝖾𝖿 \mathsf{Def} to initialize π ϕ \pi_{\phi} directly from π θ 0 \pi_{\theta_{0}} without requiring a stronger external model. Specifically, state-of-the-art RL-based red-teaming methods, RL-Hammer and PISmith, optimize π ϕ \pi_{\phi} with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [ 30 ] . For each injection task t adv t_{\mathrm{adv}} , GRPO samples a group of G G candidate injection strings from π ϕ ( ⋅ ∣ t adv ) \pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid t_{\mathrm{adv}}) . Placing each candidate d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} into π θ 0 \pi_{\theta_{0}} ’s data channel produces an attacked trajectory τ ′ \tau^{\prime} and a binary attack reward r atk = Φ t adv ​ ( τ ′ ) . r_{\mathrm{atk}}=\Phi_{t_{\mathrm{adv}}}(\tau^{\prime}). (3) GRPO normalizes r atk r_{\mathrm{atk}} using the group mean μ atk \mu_{\mathrm{atk}} and standard deviation σ atk \sigma_{\mathrm{atk}} to compute the advantage A = r atk − μ atk σ atk , A=\frac{r_{\mathrm{atk}}-\mu_{\mathrm{atk}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{atk}}}, (4) and updates ϕ \phi to increase the probability of candidates with A > 0 A>0 while decreasing those with A < 0 A<0 . Reinforcing whichever candidates achieve A > 0 A>0 within each group can cause standard GRPO to converge to a narrow, repetitive set of successful injection strings [ 11 , 12 ] . Recent RL-based red-teaming methods counter this repetition by regularizing surface text differences. RL-Hammer [ 11 ] studies auxiliary diversity rewards based on the pairwise BLEU score or the embedding distance between candidate injection strings generated by π ϕ \pi_{\phi} , whereas PISmith [ 12 ] adds adaptive token-level entropy regularization and dynamic advantage weighting to the GRPO update. However, as we discuss in § III , regularizing token entropy or pairwise text distance over full injection strings does not necessarily enforce coverage of distinct attack strategies, the ways an injection string overrides t u t_{u} (such as claiming that t u t_{u} is already finished or framing t adv t_{\mathrm{adv}} as workflow metadata). Instead, π ϕ \pi_{\phi} can satisfy these surface regularizers through wording changes while repeatedly exploiting a single vulnerability [ 11 ] . II-D Adversary Model Attacker’s goal and capabilities. At inference time, 𝖠𝖽𝗏 \mathsf{Adv} aims to induce an attacked trajectory τ ′ \tau^{\prime} satisfying Φ t adv ​ ( τ ′ ) = 1 \Phi_{t_{\mathrm{adv}}}(\tau^{\prime})=1 while the deployed agent policy π θ \pi_{\theta} executes a benign user task t u t_{u} . 𝖠𝖽𝗏 \mathsf{Adv} can replace selected clean observations o t o_{t} in the data channel with contaminated observations o t ′ o^{\prime}_{t} that embed d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} alongside the original data, yet cannot modify the system prompt s s , the user task t u t_{u} , past actions a 1 : t − 1 a_{1:t-1} , the model weights θ \theta , or the execution environment [ 4 , 3 , 25 ] . We do not assume the defense is kept secret; rather, 𝖠𝖽𝗏 \mathsf{Adv} knows the agent’s architecture, input format, and defense mechanism, acting as a defense-adaptive 1 1 1 Prior ML literature uses the term “adaptive” to refer to defense-aware attackers while the security community uses the same term to refer to attackers who refine their attack by repeatedly interacting with the target. To avoid ambiguity, we use more precise terms, “defense-adaptive” and “iterative-adaptive” (respectively) to distinguish between these two types. attacker [ 31 , 32 ] . 𝖠𝖽𝗏 \mathsf{Adv} can also query the deployed agent policy π θ \pi_{\theta} repeatedly at inference time and observe the resulting trajectories τ ′ \tau^{\prime} and task outcomes. However, agent execution alternates between text generation and non-differentiable tool calls, so gradient-based token optimization designed for single-turn inputs [ 19 ] does not directly apply, and we restrict our evaluation to attacks that require no gradients [ 10 ] . Following established evaluation guidelines [ 31 , 32 , 10 ] , we evaluate two classes of attacks at inference time: • Static. Places pre-written injection strings d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} into the data channel in a single pass without querying π θ \pi_{\theta} or adapting to its outputs [ 4 , 2 , 33 ] . • Iterative-adaptive. 1 Queries π θ \pi_{\theta} online in a live loop at inference time on each target task, inserting candidate injection strings d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} , observing the resulting trajectories τ ′ \tau^{\prime} , and updating d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} across attempts to maximize Φ t adv ​ ( τ ′ ) \Phi_{t_{\mathrm{adv}}}(\tau^{\prime}) [ 34 , 10 ] . Iterative-adaptive attacks update d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} across queries using either RL or LLM-guided search. RL-based attacks such as AutoInject [ 35 ] train an attacker policy online, from the feedback of the deployed π θ \pi_{\theta} on the task under attack. Although both rely on RL, these online attacks differ from the red-teaming in § II-C , which 𝖣𝖾𝖿 \mathsf{Def} runs offline against π θ 0 \pi_{\theta_{0}} to collect injection strings for adversarial training. Search-based attacks instead use two auxiliary LLMs: an attacker LLM that rewrites candidate injection strings d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} based on prior trajectories τ ′ \tau^{\prime} , and a judge LLM that scores how close each τ ′ \tau^{\prime} comes to completing t adv t_{\mathrm{adv}} [ 34 , 36 , 10 ] . Template-guided search such as Strategy [ 36 ] restricts the attacker LLM to a predefined list of rewrite rules. Open-ended search removes this restriction and lets the attacker LLM freely rewrite d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} over hundreds of online queries per task, which often achieves higher ASR. Among these attacks, we evaluate two open-ended searches: Tree of Attacks with Pruning ( TAP ) [ 34 ] , which is widely used in practice [ 3 , 36 ] , and genetic search ( Genetic ) [ 10 ] , which achieves state-of-the-art ASR across recent IPI defenses. TAP branches candidate rewrites in a tree and prunes low-scoring branches with the judge LLM, whereas Genetic evolves a MAP-Elites grid of candidates across generations without early pruning. Both can require hundreds of online rollouts and auxiliary LLM calls per task. This cost makes them strong inference-time stress tests on evaluation sets but impractical for generating thousands of injection strings across a training set, where RL-based red-teaming (§ II-C ) is used instead. Defender’s goal and capabilities. Before deployment, the defender 𝖣𝖾𝖿 \mathsf{Def} (the model developer) hardens the base policy π θ 0 \pi_{\theta_{0}} into π θ \pi_{\theta} offline inside a controlled sandbox over a set of simulated task pairs 𝒟 tasks \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tasks}} . Each task pair specifies a user task t u t_{u} , an injection task t adv t_{\mathrm{adv}} , and the simulated tool environment in which the agent executes to produce trajectories τ \tau and τ ′ \tau^{\prime} . Within this sandbox, 𝖣𝖾𝖿 \mathsf{Def} initializes the red-teaming policy π ϕ \pi_{\phi} from π θ 0 \pi_{\theta_{0}} itself, has white-box access to the weights, evaluates Φ t u \Phi_{t_{u}} and Φ t adv \Phi_{t_{\mathrm{adv}}} on simulated trajectories, and knows the exact injection string d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} placed in each contaminated observation o t ′ o^{\prime}_{t} . At inference time, 𝖣𝖾𝖿 \mathsf{Def} deploys π θ \pi_{\theta} to serve inference requests, and the sandbox assumptions are removed; π θ \pi_{\theta} does not control incoming observations in the data channel and does not know whether an observation o t o_{t} is contaminated, where d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} appears, or which injection task t adv t_{\mathrm{adv}} and attack method 𝖠𝖽𝗏 \mathsf{Adv} uses. Under static or iterative-adaptive attacks, π θ \pi_{\theta} should not execute the injection task, Φ t adv ​ ( τ ′ ) = 0 \Phi_{t_{\mathrm{adv}}}(\tau^{\prime})=0 , minimizing ASR (§ II-B ). R2. III Motivating Study and Problem Analysis In cybersecurity, pairing offensive red-teaming with defensive blue-teaming , which here takes the form of adversarial training (§ II-C ), is called purple-teaming [ 37 , 38 ] . This workflow implicitly assumes that a stronger red-teamer, which achieves higher ASR against the base policy π θ 0 \pi_{\theta_{0}} , uncovers more vulnerabilities, so that training on its discoveries produces a more robust policy π θ \pi_{\theta} . We formalize this workflow (Figure 1 ) in § III-A and examine the assumption in § III-B , along with the utility cost of adversarial training. We next examine whether this larger dataset also yields a more robust and equally useful policy. III-B Red-Teaming Success May Not Always Lead to Defender Improvements Study setup. To understand why collecting more successful injection strings does not necessarily improve robustness, we examine the training objective of π ϕ \pi_{\phi} . Prior work shows that maximizing attack reward in Eq. 3 only requires π ϕ \pi_{\phi} to find one working attack strategy and apply it across tasks [ 18 , 16 ] . Although PISmith and RL-Hammer include diversity regularizers (§ II-C ), neither regularizer measures whether d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} introduces a new attack strategy. Specifically, PISmith shrinks the coefficient of its token-level entropy bonus to a small base value when the group mean reward μ atk \mu_{\mathrm{atk}} crosses a threshold [ 12 ] , which relaxes exploration as soon as π ϕ \pi_{\phi} finds a working attack strategy. Similarly, RL-Hammer’s diversity rewards compare lexical n-gram overlap (pairwise BLEU) or embedding distance over the full injection string d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} , and its authors report that π ϕ \pi_{\phi} satisfies them by changing letter case and wording or by prepending irrelevant text while reusing the same attack strategy [ 11 ] . We therefore hypothesize that higher train-time ASR comes from applying the same few attack strategies across more tasks in 𝒟 tasks \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tasks}} , which leaves other attack strategies open when TAP and Genetic search for new injection strings at inference time [ 10 , 17 ] . Remark 1: Train-Time ASR vs. Robustness Higher train-time ASR and larger training datasets 𝒟 train \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}} resulting from RL-based red-teaming do not guarantee stronger inference-time robustness, likely due to a lack of coverage of distinct attack strategies. Adversarial training can degrade benign utility. Beyond robustness, a hardened policy must also preserve benign utility (R3). Adversarial training, however, is known to induce a trade-off between robustness and utility on clean inputs [ 42 ] . This trade-off has been observed both in jailbreak defenses, where safety tuning degrades performance on benign prompts [ 28 , 43 ] , and in IPI defenses [ 44 , 21 ] . IV-A Revisiting Red-Teaming Objectives for Defense Improvements From surface wording to strategy primitives. In § III-B , we hypothesized that RL-based red-teamers achieve high train-time ASR while leaving other vulnerabilities open because pairwise BLEU, sentence embeddings, and token entropy bonuses (§ II-C ) operate on raw injection strings d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} rather than on the attack strategies behind them. In particular, two injection strings can share almost no words yet use the same attack strategy to override t u t_{u} [ 11 , 17 ] , because task-specific nouns such as email addresses or account numbers dominate full-string similarity. Conversely, a single injection string d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} can combine several attack strategies in one text [ 17 ] . To test our hypothesis and guide red-teaming toward new vulnerabilities, we therefore need to remove noise such as task-specific nouns and decompose each injection string into reusable components that capture distinct attack strategies, which we call strategy primitives . Prior work on jailbreak attacks shows that adversarial prompts can be broken down into recurring attack strategies using an LLM [ 17 ] . Adapting this decomposition to IPI, we pass each successful injection string d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} to an LLM extractor that outputs one or more structured strategy records ψ ⁡ ( d adv ) \psi(d_{\mathrm{adv}}) . Each record contains three fields: a short strategy name, the matched verbatim text in d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} , and a task-agnostic explanation e e of how that text redirects the agent policy away from t u t_{u} . Across thousands of rollouts, raw strategy explanations { e j } j = 1 N \{e_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N} still contain many near-duplicate descriptions of the same attack strategy, as well as descriptions that blend multiple strategies. To handle both, we apply sparse dictionary learning [ 45 , 46 , 17 ] , which represents each explanation vector as a sparse linear combination of K ≪ N K\ll N shared strategy primitives. IV-B Pipette -Red: Strategy-Guided Red-Teaming Strategy coverage reward. In Eq. 3 , π ϕ \pi_{\phi} receives the same reward for repeating a known attack strategy as for discovering a new one, which likely contributes to the ES \mathrm{ES} plateau in Remark 3. IV-C Pipette -Blue: On-Policy Defensive Training After Pipette -Red produces 𝒟 train \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}} spanning diverse attack strategies, Pipette -Blue (Figure 3 , right) trains the agent policy π θ \pi_{\theta} on 𝒟 train \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}} toward the three defense requirements R1 to R3 (§ II-D ). Task-alignment reasoning and outcome-based RL. To learn generalizable defensive behavior during adversarial training, Pipette -Blue trains the agent policy π θ \pi_{\theta} to separate untrusted data from instructions through explicit reasoning before acting. Prior work on jailbreak defense shows that training a model to reason over a fixed set of safety policies before responding improves generalization to unseen attacks [ 49 ] . Following this approach, Pipette -Blue trains π θ \pi_{\theta} to inspect the last observation o t − 1 o_{t-1} in its reasoning ρ t \rho_{t} before emitting tool calls c t c_{t} (§ II-A ). This reasoning performs two checks [ 4 , 50 ] : C1 , identify whether o t − 1 o_{t-1} contains any instruction or attempt to redirect the task, and C2 , verify that every planned tool call in c t c_{t} serves a goal authorized by the trusted user task t u t_{u} . Under C2, observations may supply the data for that goal but cannot authorize a new one. To establish this reasoning format without distilling from a larger model, 𝖣𝖾𝖿 \mathsf{Def} prompts the base policy π θ 0 \pi_{\theta_{0}} to generate demonstrations whose reasoning ρ t \rho_{t} follows C1 and C2. For this SFT warm-up, we use tasks in 𝒟 tasks \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tasks}} and a weaker subset of AgentDojo’s built-in static attacks, which we exclude from evaluation. After the warm-up, Pipette -Blue trains π θ \pi_{\theta} with on-policy RL on attacked tasks from 𝒟 train \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}} and clean tasks from 𝒟 tasks \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tasks}} . Each of the G G rollouts in a group receives the outcome-based defense reward r def r_{\mathrm{def}} in Eq. 7 and a chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning reward r CoT ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] r_{\mathrm{CoT}}\in[0,1] . To compute the latter, an LLM judge checks C1 and C2 in ρ t \rho_{t} at each step and averages the binary results over the trajectory. We normalize and combine both rewards as in Eq. 12 to obtain the trajectory-level advantage A traj A_{\mathrm{traj}} . Contrastive token-level reward. To address the utility degradation observed in § III-B , where supervising multi-step rollouts solely with the outcome-based reward r def r_{\mathrm{def}} can push π θ \pi_{\theta} toward overly cautious refusals, Pipette -Blue uses a token-level reward. To assign credit to individual tokens, we use the difference in information available at inference time versus offline training (§ II-D ). Intuitively, an IPI attack succeeds when π θ \pi_{\theta} is confused about the provenance of malicious instructions embedded in o t − 1 ′ o^{\prime}_{t-1} . If a user explicitly identified d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} and instructed the agent to ignore it, even the base policy π θ 0 \pi_{\theta_{0}} could often follow this directive and complete t u t_{u} safely. At inference time, however, π θ \pi_{\theta} acts autonomously on behalf of the user, so no such guidance is available. During offline adversarial training, in contrast, 𝖣𝖾𝖿 \mathsf{Def} has access to the exact injection string d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} placed in H t ′ H^{\prime}_{t} . V Experiments To determine what the defender gains from a better training set (§ III ), we vary the collection and the training procedure separately (§ V-B ). We then evaluate the trained agent under iterative-adaptive and static attacks (R1, § V-C and § V-D ). Since a refusal can also prevent an injection, we assess utility alongside robustness (R2 and R3, § V-E ) and examine how Pipette -Blue’s supervision affects both in an ablation (§ V-F ). V-A Experimental Setup Models and benchmarks. We evaluate four open-weight models spanning three model families from 4B to 27B parameters: Qwen3-4B and Llama-8B from § III-B , GPT-OSS-20B [ 52 ] , and Qwen3.6-27B [ 53 ] . Across all models, both stages of Pipette run offline exclusively on AgentDyn tasks [ 39 ] . At inference time, we evaluate the trained policies on two unseen multi-step benchmarks: AgentDojo [ 2 ] , covering the Workspace, Slack, Travel, and Banking suites, and Agent Security Bench (ASB) [ 54 ] . No user task t u t_{u} or injection task t adv t_{\mathrm{adv}} of AgentDojo or ASB appears in training. Defense baselines and evaluation protocol. In § V-B , we first evaluate the two stages of our framework individually: Pipette -Red (§ IV-B ) paired with RL or SFT adversarial training (§ III-A ), and Pipette -Blue (§ IV-C ) compared against RL and SFT on the dataset 𝒟 train \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}} collected by Pipette -Red. In § V-C through § V-E , we compare the complete Pipette against two categories of prior IPI defenses: • Training-based defenses update the policy weights θ \theta offline via adversarial training prior to deployment and add no external components at inference time, including Meta-SecAlign [ 15 ] , SecOPD [ 21 ] , and Instruction Hierarchy [ 55 ] , which is natively trained into GPT-OSS-20B [ 52 ] . • Non-training-based defenses leave the policy weights θ \theta unchanged and place inference-time safeguards around the agent policy, including prompt boundary defenses ( Spotlighting [ 56 ] , Sandwiching [ 57 ] ), external input classifiers ( PromptGuard [ 58 ] , PIGuard [ 44 ] ), canary detection ( DataSentinel [ 59 ] ), masked re-execution ( MELON [ 23 ] ), and system-level control- and data-flow isolation ( CaMeL [ 60 ] ). The training-based baselines are released for different models, so we use the authors’ checkpoints on their native models: Meta-SecAlign on Llama-8B, SecOPD on Qwen3.6-27B, and the native Instruction Hierarchy in GPT-OSS-20B. Since prior studies already report near-zero ASR for trained defenses on static attacks [ 15 , 21 ] , we focus the main comparison on iterative-adaptive attacks on AgentDojo (§ V-C ) and give the static results in Appendix B . We evaluate the non-training-based defenses on Qwen3-4B and Llama-8B under the built-in attacks of AgentDojo and ASB (§ V-D ). We exclude the weaker AgentDojo attacks used to initialize the output formats of π ϕ \pi_{\phi} and π θ \pi_{\theta} , such as Direct and SystemMsg (§ IV-C , Appendix D ). Attacks and metrics. Static evaluations on AgentDojo cover IgnorePrev [ 61 ] , InjecAgent [ 62 ] , ImportantMsgs [ 2 ] , ToolKnow [ 2 ] , and ChatInject [ 33 ] ; those on ASB cover Naive , Escape , Ignore , Completion , and Combined [ 4 , 54 ] . Iterative-adaptive evaluations use the two search-based attacks from § II-D , TAP [ 34 ] (up to 250 250 attempts per task pair) and Genetic [ 10 ] (up to 248 248 attempts per task pair), both with Qwen3.5-4B as the attacker LLM and Qwen3-4B as the judge LLM. Following § III-B , we run them on the same AgentDojo task pairs spanning all four suites (Appendix C ). We report ASR, UA, and BU (§ II-D ); implementation details and prompts are in Appendices D and E . Doing so lowers ASR on plain instruction overrides such as IgnorePrev , yet is less effective when d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} spoofs chat delimiters or tool schemas in ChatInject and ToolKnow . For instance, under ChatInject on AgentDojo, Spotlighting has 45.71 % 45.71\% ASR on Qwen3-4B, close to the undefended base policy ( 46.19 % 46.19\% ). • External detectors and masked re-execution rely on fixed classifier boundaries ( PromptGuard , PIGuard ), canary prompts ( DataSentinel ), or masked re-execution ( MELON ). V-E Benign Utility and Utility Under Attack Beyond robustness, we examine how well each defense preserves utility (R2 and R3). As shown in Table VI and Figure 6 , non-training-based defenses tend to lose UA or BU, likely because they do not separate d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} from the benign data inside o t ′ o^{\prime}_{t} . External detectors ( PromptGuard , PIGuard , DataSentinel ) discard the entire flagged observation o t ′ o^{\prime}_{t} , removing the data needed for t u t_{u} and lowering UA. Masked re-execution ( MELON ) and system-level isolation ( CaMeL ) can also block legitimate tool calls whose arguments or control flow depend on o t o_{t} , which reduces UA and, in all but one case, BU. Pipette , by comparison, inspects o t ′ o^{\prime}_{t} in ρ t + 1 \rho_{t+1} to ignore d adv d_{\mathrm{adv}} while still extracting the benign data needed for t u t_{u} . Pipette achieves the highest BU and average static UA on AgentDojo for both models, and preserves BU on Qwen3.6-27B ( 92.78 % 92.78\% versus 93.81 % 93.81\% undefended; 84.54 % 84.54\% for SecOPD ) and GPT-OSS-20B ( 49.48 % 49.48\% versus 50.52 % 50.52\% ; Table IX ). On ASB, its average UA ranks first on Llama-8B and second on Qwen3-4B, behind Sandwiching , which has higher average ASR; in all four cases, its static UA exceeds the undefended policy’s. Under iterative-adaptive attacks (Table V ), its UA stays close to the base policy on GPT-OSS-20B and exceeds it on Llama-8B under TAP , while dropping on Qwen3.6-27B from 97.50 % 97.50\% to 87.50 % 87.50\% ( TAP ) and 90.00 % 90.00\% ( Genetic ), where SecOPD retains higher UA at higher ASR (§ V-C ). VI Discussion and Limitations Inference latency. All dictionary rebuilds in Pipette -Red and token-level reward queries in Pipette -Blue (Eq. 13 ) run offline during purple-teaming and add no runtime cost at deployment. At inference time, Pipette uses no external detector models or duplicate agent executions; its only overhead comes from generating C1 and C2 reasoning ρ t \rho_{t} before tool calls c t c_{t} . As shown in Appendix A , Pipette changes the total rollout time of Qwen3-4B by − 0.47 -0.47 to + 0.62 +0.62 s and its token usage by − 28 % -28\% to + 16 % +16\% across the four suites relative to the undefended base policy, while running 1.9 × 1.9\times to 3.1 × 3.1\times faster than masked re-execution in MELON and 2.7 × 2.7\times to 9.5 × 9.5\times faster than DataSentinel . Continuous purple-teaming. Pipette currently performs a single pass of Pipette -Red against π θ 0 \pi_{\theta_{0}} followed by Pipette -Blue, rather than alternating between red-teaming and adversarial training across multiple rounds [ 20 ] . During Pipette -Red, rebuilding the rolling dictionary 𝐃 ℓ \mathbf{D}_{\ell} every M M steps keeps π ϕ \pi_{\phi} exploring without retraining π θ \pi_{\theta} , since repeating an attack strategy that has entered 𝐃 ℓ + 1 \mathbf{D}_{\ell+1} yields high ES \mathrm{ES} and near-zero r cov r_{\mathrm{cov}} . Moreover, once π θ \pi_{\theta} completes Pipette -Blue, even 250 250 -attempt online search achieves low ASR (Table V ), which may leave a red-teaming policy initialized from π θ 0 \pi_{\theta_{0}} with too few successful rollouts to bootstrap a second round. Nevertheless, a single pass cannot reveal vulnerabilities that emerge only in the hardened π θ \pi_{\theta} , so we leave multi-round purple-teaming to future work. Scope of the evaluation. Our iterative-adaptive evaluation is bounded by its attack budget (two search-based attacks, around 250 250 attempts per task pair, a 4B attacker LLM, and 40 40 task pairs), so a larger budget may achieve higher ASR. Moreover, the LLM grader behind ES \mathrm{ES} has not been validated against human labels, and we do not establish low strategy coverage as the sole cause of the gap between train-time ASR and robustness. VII Conclusion We find that pairing high-ASR RL-based red-teaming with standard adversarial training does not necessarily make LLM agents robust to unseen attacks. The red-teamers we study exhibit strategy-level mode collapse, and adversarial training with off-policy SFT or trajectory-level outcome rewards can reduce benign utility. To address both problems, we present Pipette , a two-stage prompt injection purple-teaming framework. Pipette -Red guides RL-based red-teaming with a rolling strategy dictionary to keep uncovering new attack strategies, and Pipette -Blue then combines task-alignment reasoning with a contrastive token-level reward to help the agent ignore injections while completing user tasks. Across four open-weight models trained on AgentDyn, Pipette achieves lower average ASR on the unseen AgentDojo and ASB benchmarks than the training-based and non-training-based baselines while preserving benign utility. In both benchmarks, the agent policy decodes greedily at temperature 0 0 . Selecting the 40 40 task pairs for iterative-adaptive attacks. Because TAP and Genetic each spend around 250 250 rollouts of the agent policy on a single task pair (Table I ), attacking all 420 420 task pairs could take up to 209,160 209{,}160 rollouts for every row of Table V . We therefore concentrate this budget on task pairs known to be vulnerable, namely those that static attacks already compromise on the undefended Qwen3-4B, and select 40 40 of them, 10 10 from each AgentDojo suite. On such a task pair, the undefended agent has been shown to read the injection and execute the injection task, which makes it a natural target for a stronger attack. ASR on this subset is thus best read as a stress test on previously compromised task pairs, not as an estimate of the rate over all 420 420 task pairs. The subset is selected once and reused for every base policy and defense, so that all rows of Table V are measured on the same task pairs.
