                  Abstract
                  Recursive self-improvement (RSI) studies how AI systems modify themselves and carry those changes
                  into the generation of subsequent improvements. LLM agents can pursue this process by using in-
                  teraction experience to revise Prompt, Memory, Skill, and Harness in some cases while keeping the
                  task-execution model’s weights fixed. However, execution feedback does not directly specify which
                  component should change, and a locally successful revision may not remain useful across tasks or
                  subsequent improvement cycles. Understanding how experience becomes persistent agent structure
                  therefore requires examining when evidence justifies a change, where that change should act, and how
                  it is constructed, verified, and retained. This survey connects four components–Prompt, Memory, Skill,
                  and Harness–with seven analytical functions: Collect, Evaluate, Diagnose, Abstract, Update, Verify,
                  and Consolidate. The resulting framework supports comparisons of instruction revision, memory
                  management, skill construction and maintenance, and Harness evolution through their update loops.
                  These comparisons examine how mechanisms use observations to construct candidate changes, distin-
                  guishing feedback that triggers revision from evidence that supports retention. The analysis further
                  addresses coordinated updates across components and the revision or retirement of retained compo-
                  nents as tasks, tools, and models change. Finally, we identify cross-component attribution, long-term
                  effectiveness, and independent validation as key challenges, and outline controlled and longitudinal
                  evaluations of task-performance gains, the contribution of inherited state, and the effectiveness of
                  subsequent updates.

                  1. Introduction
                      Recursive self-improvement (RSI) refers to the process by which AI systems learn from their
                  experience to improve themselves, with each improvement potentially enabling further improve-
                  ments (M. Chen, Wang, & Qu, 2026; Duan et al., 2026; H. Wu et al., 2026). For large language model

              (LLM) agents, this process can operate through changes to Prompt, Memory, Skill, and Harness while
              the task-execution model’s weights remain fixed. Retained changes can influence both subsequent
              task execution and the diagnosis, generation, and selection of further updates. Some studies also
              combine external component updates with model training. However, interaction outcomes often
              provide incomplete evidence about what should change and whether a proposed modification will
              remain useful across tasks and environments.
                    Experience-driven changes can act on several parts of an agent system (Silver & Sutton, 2025).
              Automatic prompt optimization revises instructions and demonstrations that directly condition model
              behavior (Schulhoff et al., 2024). Memory mechanisms extract, organize, retrieve, revise, and discard
              information acquired through previous interactions (Y. Hu et al., 2025). Skill mechanisms construct
              reusable procedures and maintain the libraries through which these procedures are selected and
              invoked (R. Xu & Yan, 2026). Harness mechanisms govern context assembly, tool mediation, execution
              orchestration, and behavioral checks (J. Li, Xiao, et al., 2026). We refer to these components as Prompt,
              Memory, Skill, and Harness. These components interact within a system: a harness may retrieve
              memories and invoke skills, while prompts guide their use. The four categories represent functional
              roles of editable agent state, distinguished by how retained content affects execution rather than by
              mutually exclusive software modules or storage formats.
                    Existing surveys organize these advances from several complementary perspectives. Surveys of
              self-evolving agents connect component optimization to adaptive and lifelong systems, while surveys
              of agent adaptation classify approaches by their optimization targets and feedback sources (Fang, Peng,
              et al., 2025; P. Jiang et al., 2025). Self-improving agents in the era of experience examines experience-driven
              improvement through runtime infrastructure, update destinations, and meta-evolution (C. Jiang et al.,
              2026). Building on these perspectives, this survey examines the operations that connect interaction
              evidence to an accepted component update. Methods targeting the same component may differ
              substantially in how they identify failures, extract reusable information, construct revisions, and verify
              their effects. Conversely, methods modifying different components may share update procedures
              while producing different execution-time behaviors. We therefore take the update loop as a common
              unit of comparison, connecting the evidence that motivates a change, the component on which it acts,
              and the state inherited by subsequent cycles.
                    Three connected questions organize our analysis. When should interaction evidence trigger an
              update, justify its retention, or warrant revision or retirement? Where should an intervention act—on
              Prompt, Memory, Skill, Harness, or multiple components in coordination? How can evidence be
              transformed into reusable modifications, verified against potential regressions, and integrated for
              subsequent use? These questions are interdependent. The choice of update target determines how a
              correction affects execution and what evidence is needed to assess it; verification may, in turn, motivate
              a different intervention or limit the scope of retention. Figure 1 summarizes these relationships and
              distinguishes the reuse of retained state during task execution from its role in generating subsequent
              updates.
                    Consider an agent whose requests fail after an API schema change. Failed calls and validation
              responses provide evidence of an incompatibility, but the appropriate intervention depends on how
              requests are produced. A Prompt update can direct the model to use the revised field name; a Memory
              update can retain version-specific schema information for retrieval; a Skill update can revise a reusable
              request-construction procedure; and a Harness update can enforce parameter validation before execu-
              tion. These interventions differ in their scope, invocation conditions, and maintenance requirements,
              and may be combined. Verification must therefore examine both whether the intervention repairs the
              failed requests and whether it preserves valid behavior for other supported versions. Once retained,
              the correction can support later execution and provide information for diagnosing or constructing
              further updates.
                    To compare such interventions systematically, we introduce a lifecycle comprising seven analytical
              functions. Collect acquires interaction traces and feedback; Evaluate assesses observed execution

                    Across update loops, inherited state can influence how later changes are diagnosed, generated,
              evaluated, and selected. Recent systems retain information about previous update attempts or modify
              the procedures used to produce future changes (Kim et al., 2026; S. Wu et al., 2026; Xia, Han, et al., 2026;
              J. Zhang et al., 2026). These mechanisms connect component evolution to the improvement process
              itself. They also motivate an evaluation question: does inherited state help produce more effective
              future updates under comparable experience, feedback, and computational budgets? Emerging
              benchmarks provide settings for examining this question across different mutable components and
              feedback conditions (Meng et al., 2026; Y. Wu, Zhang, Shi, Zhang, et al., 2026). We therefore trace both
              how retained artifacts enter subsequent improvement and how their contribution to future updates
              can be evaluated.
                    This survey makes four contributions:
              •     A shared analytical framework for experience-driven updates. We connect seven analytical
                    functions with four execution roles and apply this framework to 20 update loops from 20 studies,
                    tracing how evidence becomes candidate changes and retained state.
              •     A mechanism-level synthesis across agent components. We compare how Prompt, Memory,
                    Skill, and Harness form, revise, verify, and maintain reusable state, relating differences in update
                    procedures to their effects during execution.

              2. Background, Scope, and Survey Organization
              2.1. From Feedback to Recursive Self-Improvement
                   Research on improving agents through interaction experience connects to several established
              fields. Continual learning asks how a model can learn new tasks without forgetting previous ones
              (De Lange et al., 2019). Meta-learning uses experience from earlier learning episodes to improve how a
              model learns new tasks (Hospedales et al., 2020). Reinforcement learning (RL) can train an agent to
              manage Memory, for example by deciding which records to add, update, or delete (G. Zhang et al.,
              2025). Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) provides access to stored information (Y. Li et al., 2026),
              while prompt optimization provides methods for revising a Prompt.
                   The component being updated and the process that updates it are distinct. A learned selector can
              operate on Memory; an LLM can revise a Prompt or construct a Skill; and a fixed gate can accept or
              reject a proposed update. These processes can change different components even when they use the
              same update algorithm.
                   LLM agents can use experience to update a Prompt, Memory, Skill, or Harness. This creates
              several parallel routes for retaining the result of feedback.
                   Automatic prompt optimization makes behavioral specification searchable. APE generates and
              selects instructions for a Prompt, OPRO conditions proposals on scored candidates, and ProTeGi uses
              error-derived critiques to guide edits (Pryzant et al., 2023; C. Yang et al., 2023; Y. Zhou et al., 2022).
              These methods can support cumulative improvement when the updated Prompt is retained for later
              interactions.
                   Agents can also retain experience in Memory and Skills. Reflexion carries verbal feedback into
              subsequent attempts; ExpeL extracts reusable insights and recalls past experience; MemGPT manages

              stores executable programs in a reusable skill library (G. Wang et al., 2023). These systems expose a
              distinction between recalling evidence and invoking a procedure: the same successful trajectory can
              support either product, with different selection and execution requirements.
                    As interactions grow longer, Harness also becomes a target for improvement. SWE-agent studies
              agent–computer interfaces; AutoHarness synthesizes action-checking code from environmental feed-
              back; Meta-Harness searches over complete Harness implementations (Y. Lee et al., 2026; Lou et al.,

              predictions about their effects (Lin et al., 2026). Editable content includes how an agent observes state,
              invokes tools, checks actions, and recovers.
                    Together, these routes connect local feedback to persistent behavioral and system changes. Surveys
              of learning from experience, agent adaptation, and long-horizon execution examine this shift through
              retained state, feedback, and interactions between models and harnesses (Dong et al., 2026; C. Jiang
              et al., 2026; P. Jiang et al., 2025).

              2.2. Scope and Comparison Criteria
                    This survey examines how LLM agents use interaction experience to improve Prompt, Memory,
              Skill, and Harness. The title uses structure to refer to these components and their retained contents. We
              focus on updates that leave the task executor’s model weights fixed, though the updater itself may be
              trained (Kong et al., 2025).
                    For Prompt, experience can guide revisions to instructions or demonstrations. For Memory, agents
              can add, revise, or remove records based on previous interactions. For Skill, experience can support
              the construction or revision of reusable procedures. For Harness, feedback can inform changes to how
              Harness manages tool use or action checks.
                    This survey examines methods that retain component updates for later tasks. It also examines
              whether a retained component or update process enters a later improvement cycle and helps produce
              further updates. Reuse in later tasks alone does not establish that recursive dependency. Figure 3
              illustrates the distinction between a revised answer, a component retained for later tasks, and a change
              to the process that produces updates.

              2.3. Relation to Existing Surveys
                    Self-improving agents in the era of experience examines how deployed agents accumulate and reuse
              experience through execution infrastructure, external components, and model updates (C. Jiang et al.,
              2026). The agent adaptation survey compares methods by optimization target and feedback source
              (P. Jiang et al., 2025), while a survey of self-evolving agents considers component optimization in
              lifelong agent systems (Fang, Peng, et al., 2025). Building on these perspectives, we compare the
              operations within component update loops.
                    A survey of self-evolving agents organizes evolution by what changes, when it changes, how it
              changes, and where it is applied (H.-a. Gao et al., 2025). Its timing categories include adaptation within
              and between test-time episodes, while its application categories distinguish general and specialized
              domains. In our survey, when concerns the evidence for updating, retaining, revising, or removing
              a component; where concerns which component to update; and how concerns how an update is
              constructed, verified, and maintained.
                    Broader RSI surveys organize methods by engineering goal, autonomy over improvement de-
              cisions, or loop closure (M. Chen, Wang, & Qu, 2026; Duan et al., 2026; H. Wu et al., 2026). Coupled
              model–scaffold and co-evolution surveys examine which interacting components can change (Ren
              et al., 2026; Zong et al., 2026). Component and domain surveys provide the corresponding accounts of
              memory, skills, harnesses, coding agents, executable interfaces, and environments (Y. Hu et al., 2025;
              J. Li, Jin, et al., 2026; J. Li, Xiao, et al., 2026; Ning et al., 2026; R. Xu & Yan, 2026; H. Zhou, Hu, et al.,
              2026). These axes locate an update within a system or improvement process. Our comparison follows
              the operations within that update: which observations support a diagnosis, how reusable content is
              constructed, and which tests determine retention.

              3. Where Experience Becomes Structure: Four Components
                  Prompt, Memory, Skill, and Harness identify the components on which the analytical lifecycle
              operates. Their functions during execution influence the evidence available for diagnosis, the form of
              an update, and the behavior that verification must test.

              3.1. Operational definitions
                  Choosing where to retain a correction requires identifying how it will reach and shape later
              execution. For example, TPTU and TPTU-v2 study task planning and tool use in large language model
              agents (Kong et al., 2024; Ruan et al., 2023). Table 1 compares the four components by their use in

              3.2. Complementary Comparison Dimensions
                   We compare the components along five dimensions:
              •    Persistence: how long a change remains available without being re-derived.
              •    Proceduralization: whether the retained content describes experience or provides a reusable
                   process.
              •    Enforcement: whether use depends on model compliance or is imposed by the surrounding
                   system.
              •    Editability: how easily the retained content can be inspected, revised, versioned, and rolled back.
              •    Governance burden: verification, oversight, and maintenance work associated with a change.
                    Deployment policy determines persistence: a versioned system prompt may outlast a temporary
              memory entry. The relative costs of editing and governance can also reverse. A small declarative guard
              may be easier to inspect and verify than a large skill library with many dependencies. Comparisons
              therefore need measurements of retention, verification effort, and dependencies in the system under
              study.
                    These dimensions also interact. Broader enforcement can require more testing; a Memory can
              remain stored after losing its value; and a short configuration change can affect every task.
                    Classification. First identify the content that changes, then determine how it affects later execution.
              This survey classifies content directly included in context as Prompt, records retrieved under a
              condition as Memory, and procedures selected and executed by the agent as Skill. Changes to
              mechanisms such as context management, tool dispatch, or verification are classified as Harness. A
              system can use several components, so each change should be identified separately.
                    Three repair examples. These cases illustrate how components can address different problems.
              In the API case, an agent keeps using an obsolete field after a schema change. An instruction, retrieved
              schema, callable wrapper, and Harness validator can each address part of the failure. The comparison
              must distinguish knowledge of the new field from correct invocation and rejection of invalid calls.
                    In the fact revision case, a user supplies a new delivery address. The repair must answer current
              queries correctly while preserving the old address for explicitly historical queries. A global instruction
              can supply the correction, selective recall can restrict it to the relevant user and time, and a controller
              can enforce which version a transaction uses. The question is whether scope and validity survive
              reuse.
                    In the procedure case, an agent repeatedly carries out a repository check involving several com-
              mands and a stop condition. A prompt can describe the steps, a memory can recall an earlier execution,
              a skill can expose the procedure for invocation, and a harness can require the checks before accepting a
              result. The comparison asks whether errors concern missing knowledge, unreliable sequencing, or a
              requirement that must hold even when the model omits a step.
                    These cases show that changes to different components have different requirements for use and
              testing.

              3.3. Prompt and Memory: Specification Versus Selective Recall
                    Prompt and Memory both influence the model through context. For example, PET-SQL uses a
              Prompt for a Text-to-SQL task (Z. Li et al., 2026; B. Zhang, Ye, et al., 2026). This survey distinguishes
              them by use: content directly included in context is analyzed as Prompt, while records selected and
              retrieved by a query or another condition are analyzed as Memory. Text, storage medium, and retention
              duration do not determine the classification. Moving an instruction from unconditional injection to
              Memory adds a retrieval step: the instruction must be selected before it enters the context. Moving a
              retrieved case into the Prompt removes that retrieval step; the case is then included directly within the
              relevant configuration.

              3.4. Memory and Skill: Experience Versus Callable Procedure
                    Memory and Skill both retain content for reuse: Memory may record procedures, and Skills may
              be retrieved as text. This survey distinguishes content used as an example from a reusable procedure
              selected and executed by the agent. Applicability conditions, execution steps, and outcome checks
              help describe how a procedure is used.
                    MemSkill represents operations for managing memory as reusable skills that can evolve (H. Zhang,
              Long, et al., 2026). Its controller selects routines for extracting, consolidating, revising, and pruning
              information; an executor applies them to produce memories, and a designer improves the routines
              using difficult cases. Here, Skill operates on Memory by providing procedures for managing records.
              The two components call for different evaluations. For Memory, we can test whether retrieving a
              record helps the agent make a better decision. For Skill, we can test whether the agent can carry out
              the procedure and whether using it improves the result. The way content is used also matters: a record
              retrieved as an example can serve as Memory even if it describes a sequence of actions. If those actions
              are organized as a reusable procedure for the agent to select and follow, they can serve as a Skill.

              3.5. Skill and Harness: Procedures and Execution Management
                   A Skill packages a procedure for invocation; Harness manages model and tool calls, including
              context assembly, state management, retries, verification, and permissions. Changing a procedure’s
              steps updates Skill state; changing its dispatcher, context manager, tracing, or admission mechanism
              updates Harness state. These changes can improve information availability or coordination as well as
              impose constraints. SkillHone combines the two components: the harness tracks skill provenance and
              evolution, while skills provide execution units (Z. Li & Hu, 2026).

              4. How Experience Becomes Structure: An Analytical Lifecycle
                 The seven-stage lifecycle analyzes how agents use interaction experience to update Prompt,
              Memory, Skill, and Harness.

              4.1. Functions of the Seven Stages
                   The stages describe the operations that can turn experience into a reusable component update.
              Collect gathers interaction records and feedback. Evaluate assesses what happened during execution.
              Diagnose identifies a problem or a target for change. Abstract derives reusable information from the
              experience, such as a fact, rule, or procedure. Update constructs or revises a component. Verify tests
              the effects of the proposed change. Consolidate selects and retains content for later use, including
              possible use in subsequent updates.
                   Evaluate and Verify serve different purposes. Evaluate examines an existing execution to provide
              evidence for an update; Verify tests a candidate change. For example, a failed API call can help identify
              a problem, while testing a revised request procedure can show whether the proposed change works.
                   The stages are analytical tools, not mandatory steps in a fixed order. A method may combine
              several stages, omit some, or test a change after it has been put into use.

              5.1. What Experience Informs Prompt Updates
                   Prompt updates can use task scores, incorrect examples, or execution traces (Agrawal et al., 2025;
              Pryzant et al., 2023; C. Yang et al., 2023). Scores allow different Prompts to be compared; errors and
              traces also provide information about how a failure occurred. What the optimizer can observe affects
              how it proposes a change.

              5.2. Generating Prompt Candidates from Experience
                    OPRO generates instructions from earlier instructions and task scores. The optimizer receives a
              task description, examples, and the scores of previous instructions on training examples, then proposes
              candidates. Once scored, the candidates and their scores can be used in later generations (C. Yang et al.,
              2023). The optimizer can compare instructions using these scores, but the loop does not separately
              analyze why an instruction failed.
                    ProTeGi runs the current Prompt on a batch of training examples and collects incorrect cases
              and outputs. A model identifies possible problems in the instruction, uses these written critiques to
              propose revisions, and produces further candidates through paraphrasing. The system scores the
              candidates on the task and retains a set of Prompts for the next search round (Pryzant et al., 2023).
              Unlike instruction–score pairs alone, incorrect cases allow the optimizer to critique particular outputs;
              the critique and proposed revision may arise from the same analysis.
                    TextGrad also uses written feedback to revise a Prompt. It represents an application as connected
              steps and asks a model to provide feedback on how an earlier component contributed to the result. In
              its Prompt optimization experiments, this feedback guides changes to a system Prompt using training
              examples (Yuksekgonul et al., 2024). The approach extends written critique to systems in which the
              Prompt is one part of a larger computation.
                    GEPA uses execution traces and textual feedback to revise a program module’s Prompt. It selects
              an existing candidate, runs it on a training minibatch, and gives a reflection model the selected

              5.3. Testing and Retaining Prompt Changes
                   Methods differ in how they test candidates and decide what to retain. APE ranks generated
              instructions using a chosen scoring function and selects the highest-scoring candidate (Y. Zhou et al.,
              2022). ProTeGi retains a set of candidates during search, while GEPA maintains a candidate pool
              and uses performance on individual task examples to select what to revise next. Retaining multiple
              candidates lets a search continue exploring different instructions instead of keeping only one version
              at every round.
                   Candidate selection should also be distinguished from final testing. For example, GEPA’s mini-
              batch comparisons and candidate-pool scores participate in search; reporting the selected Prompt’s
              performance on unseen task examples requires test data that did not participate in those choices
              (Agrawal et al., 2025). Metrics such as accuracy or execution outcomes also determine what a task
              score measures.

              5.4. How Retained Content Supports Later Updates
                    Retained candidates and scores can participate in the next round of Prompt search; other methods
              also revise the instructions used to generate new Prompts. Promptbreeder maintains task Prompts
              together with the Prompts that guide their mutation. It evaluates task Prompts on training examples,
              selects high-performing candidates with their associated mutation Prompts, and uses them to generate
              the next generation; the mutation Prompts can themselves change during evolution (Fernando et al.,
              2023).
                    SePO also treats the Prompt that proposes revisions as an optimization target, but uses two stages.
              It first evolves the Prompt Agent’s own system Prompt across a task pool. It then fixes the evolved

              6.1. Forming Memories from Interactions
                   Memory can be formed from conversations or from trajectories generated as agents perform
              tasks. A system may retain a successful execution as an example, or extract facts or strategies from
              interactions; these contents require different ways of organizing and retrieving them.
                   A-MEM turns new interaction content into notes and uses a model to generate keywords, tags,
              and contextual descriptions. It retrieves related existing notes, then asks a model whether the new and
              existing content should be linked; a new note can also lead to revisions of earlier descriptions and tags
              (W. Xu et al., 2025). These updates are triggered by new interactions and do not require a task to be
              judged successful or unsuccessful. Later queries can retrieve relevant notes and their linked content.
                   H-EPM organizes successful tool-use trajectories differently. It builds a graph in which tools are
              nodes and successive calls form edges. The edges retain summaries of the circumstances around a
              transition as well as weights derived from successful executions. For a later decision, the agent can
              use a summary of the current situation to find relevant past transitions, or use the weights to suggest

              likely next tools (S. Li et al., 2025). Thus, it can reuse part of an earlier trajectory without retrieving the
              entire case.
                    ExpeL collects successful and failed trajectories on training tasks. After a failure, the agent can
              reflect and retry. Once trajectories have been gathered across tasks, a model compares success and
              failure on the same task or summarizes several successful cases to propose reusable insights. The
              system can add or edit insights, adjust their counts, and remove an insight when its count reaches
              zero (Zhao et al., 2024). On a new task, ExpeL adds the extracted insights to the task instruction and
              retrieves similar successful trajectories as examples. Under our classification, the former directly enters
              the Prompt, while the latter is used as retrievable Memory.
                    ReasoningBank constructs memories as tasks continue to arrive. After a task, an LLM judges
              success or failure from the task and trajectory, then extracts strategies from successful behavior and
              lessons from failures. It forms memory items with titles, descriptions, and content and adds them to
              the bank. On the next task, the agent retrieves relevant memories and constructs new records after
              execution (Ouyang et al., 2025). The outcome judgment does not use ground-truth answers; the basic
              writing process does not report a separate task test for each new item before insertion.
                    RSIAgent instead builds Memory through practice in a new environment. One agent proposes
              exploration tasks, another performs them, and a verifier checks outcomes against environmental
              feedback. The system uses verified experience to add or revise knowledge, and accumulated Memory
              helps guide subsequent exploration. After this preparation, Memory is frozen for downstream
              evaluation (Zhu et al., 2026). Unlike a system that continues writing after each deployed task, its
              reported evaluation tests reuse of the Memory built during exploration.

              6.2. Updating Memories with New Experience
                    New information can lead a system to add a record or to revise or delete an existing one (Fan
              et al., 2025). Mem0 extracts facts from a new exchange and its context, retrieves similar memories, and
              uses a model to compare the new and existing content and choose whether to add, update, delete, or
              leave it unchanged (Chhikara et al., 2025). Judging whether content is redundant or conflicting helps
              select the write operation; it does not test task performance after the change.
                    Zep also handles the periods during which facts are valid. It extracts facts and temporal informa-
              tion from conversations and other inputs and stores relationships in a knowledge graph. When a new
              fact conflicts with an existing relationship in time, a model compares the relevant relationships, and
              the system adjusts the validity period of the old one while retaining its history (Rasmussen et al., 2025).
              Information that no longer applies to the present can thus still answer questions about the past.

              6.3. Retrieving and Testing Updated Memories
                   Once written, records must also be found and used in later tasks. MemGPT provides retrieval
              and read–write operations that let an agent access historical information across its context and external
              storage (Packer et al., 2023). The basis for retrieval can also change in response to feedback from later
              tasks.
                   MemRL first filters memories by their similarity to the current task, then uses each record’s utility
              value to select among them. After the agent uses the selected memories to perform the task, the system
              updates their utility values using environmental rewards. On later similar tasks, those values influence
              memory selection (S. Zhang et al., 2026). This loop changes the estimated utility of memories without
              requiring their text to be rewritten each time; the task model’s weights remain fixed.
                   When to supply a record also matters during a long task. The Proactive Memory Agent periodi-
              cally reads recent execution steps and updates a separate memory bank. It then decides whether to
              provide a memory-based reminder for the next action or remain silent (Y. Wu, Zhang, Zhou, et al.,
              2026). This separates updating the bank from deciding whether its contents should enter the next
              model call. The reported comparisons include always providing memory and passive retrieval.
                   Experiments on memory systems often examine overall performance on later tasks. For example,
              Zep evaluates memory retrieval using long-term conversational question answering (Rasmussen et al.,
              2025), while MemRL compares memory methods on task performance (S. Zhang et al., 2026). Such
              system-level results can show whether the tested method helps overall, but do not directly establish
              that every record was individually verified before writing.

              6.4. Improving Memory Management from Later Experience
                   Later experience can change memory records and also improve how memories are constructed.
              MemSkill divides interaction trajectories into spans, selects Skills using the current span and retrieved
              memories, and uses those Skills to generate memory updates. The system uses task performance to
              improve these Skills, which then help construct subsequent memories (H. Zhang, Long, et al., 2026).
              Memory updates can therefore be affected by changes to another component.
                   Memory-R1 likewise learns memory management from downstream task results: a manager
              learns to add, update, delete, or leave records unchanged, while an answering agent selects information
              from retrieved results and answers. The paper uses reinforcement learning to fine-tune both agents
              (Yan et al., 2025).
                   Recuris connects Memory use during a task with later changes to its management. A working
              state records task progress and helps select relevant Skills. Execution traces then let a separate meta-
              agent identify where a failure may be addressed: a Skill, the working-state specification, the selection
              rule, or a state checker. A proposed change is accepted only if it repairs the failure and satisfies a
              regression check on development tasks (Yu et al., 2026). The next round uses the accepted version,
              while the outer process that proposes and validates changes remains fixed.

              7.1. Forming Skills from Interaction Experience

              can therefore complement each other: a procedure derived from documentation can later be revised
              using task feedback.

              then decides whether to add a candidate, merge it with an existing Skill, or discard it. Relevant Skills
              can be retrieved for later responses (Y. Yang, Li, et al., 2026). Here the input for Skill extraction is the
              user’s request, rather than a success or failure judgment on an execution trajectory.

              7.2. Revising Skills from Feedback
                   A poor outcome does not always mean that the Skill itself is wrong. The agent may have selected
              an unsuitable Skill, followed an appropriate Skill incorrectly, or encountered a missing or incorrect
              step. These possibilities lead to different updates. When evidence points to a defect in the Skill, the
              system can use the failure to identify what should be changed.
                   SkillForge first builds an initial Skill from domain documents and historical tickets for cloud
              technical support. After the agent handles later tickets, an LLM compares its responses with expert
              references. For low-consistency cases, the system analyzes problems such as missing knowledge or
              incorrect tool use, aggregates recurring failures, and locates the relevant parts of the Skill. It then edits
              those parts and commits a new version (X. Liu et al., 2026). The study replays historical tickets in
              chronological batches for three evolution rounds and evaluates on a later held-out batch. The held-out
              result assesses the final version, not each change before it is committed.
                   SkillOpt collects execution trajectories and scores on training tasks, proposes edits from successes
              and failures, and groups, deduplicates, and merges related suggestions into additions, deletions, or
              replacements in a procedural document. It compares candidates with the current version on a separate
              selection set and reserves a test set for the final result (Y. Yang, Gong, et al., 2026). In its direct-chat
              setting, the document is placed in system or developer instructions; SkillForge instead loads a Skill
              package. Both revise procedural content, but introduce it into execution differently.
                   MemSkill uses performance on memory-dependent tasks to improve the Skills used to construct
              memories. During training, a designer model analyzes representative hard cases to revise existing
              Skills or propose new ones. The system tracks better-performing Skill banks and can roll back to an

              7.3. Selecting and Maintaining a Skill Library
                    When an agent has many Skills, improvement depends on more than adding new ones. It must
              find a suitable Skill for the current task, and the library can contain overlapping Skills or leave some
              tasks uncovered. Maintenance can therefore change the library as well as the content of an individual
              Skill.
                    SkillNet connects Skills through relationships and supports their creation and evaluation (Liang
              et al., 2026). These relationships help organize related Skills, whereas SkillX also uses execution
              feedback to refine Skills at different levels of granularity. It can merge similar Skills, decompose
              complex ones, and explore new tasks to expand coverage (C. Wang et al., 2026). The distinction matters
              for reuse: an agent may need a complete procedure for one task but only part of it for another.
                    Memento-Skills uses a router to select relevant Markdown Skills for a task and updates or expands
              the library from subsequent interaction experience (H. Zhou, Guo, et al., 2026). Selection determines
              which existing experience reaches the agent; the resulting execution can then supply evidence for
              a later update. Its work on selecting and updating Skills is distinct from changing the underlying
              language model’s parameters.
                    Maintenance can also remove content. SkillReducer shortens Skill bodies while retaining the
              descriptions used for selection (Y. Gao et al., 2026). SLIM examines Skill usage and outcomes on
              validation tasks: for a Skill under review, it compares performance with and without that Skill to
              decide whether to retain or retire it; persistently failing tasks can also prompt creation of new Skills
              (Shen et al., 2026). The removal test supports decisions about existing Skills; it does not imply that
              every new Skill receives the same test before insertion.

              7.4. From Skill Evolution to Continued Improvement
                   Skill reuse can connect multiple rounds of updates: existing Skills help with new tasks, whose
              execution may in turn provide experience for revising or adding Skills. To identify such a loop, we
              need to see whether later task experience is used for an update, not only whether a generated Skill
              runs on a new task.
                   For example, SkillForge uses chronologically arriving batches of tickets to revise an existing Skill;
              Memento-Skills uses interaction experience to update or extend its library after selecting Skills for
              tasks. These methods show how later experience enters an update, but repeated updates alone do not
              establish that the update method itself has improved.
                   SkillRL combines Skill evolution with policy training. Initial Skills are distilled from successful
              and failed trajectories. During training, the system identifies poorly performing task categories using
              validation success rates, analyzes their failed trajectories, and adds or revises Skills. The new Skills
              enter an external Skill library for later retrieval; the paper does not require a separate pre-insertion test
              for every new Skill (Xia, Chen, et al., 2026). Task rewards also train the agent’s policy, so changes to
              external Skills and changes to model parameters need to be understood separately.

              7.5. Evaluating Skill Evolution
                   Evaluation can address different questions. Comparing task results with and without a Skill tests
              whether access to it helps in a particular setting; comparing versions tests whether a revision helps
              on the selected tasks. Evaluating on new tasks or with another model examines whether the Skill
              transfers beyond the experience used to create it. A correct Skill may still fail to help if it is not selected
              or followed.
                   SkillsBench evaluates the effect of providing Skills across diverse tasks, while SkillLearnBench
              studies the generation of Skills from experience (X. Li et al., 2026; Zhong et al., 2026). The survey
              by Ding and colleagues groups evaluation work across Skill generation, utility, selection, and safety
              (Ding et al., 2026). These evaluations answer complementary questions: a useful test should match the
              change being studied, whether it creates a Skill, revises its content, or changes selection.
                   SWE-Skills-Bench compares runs with and without a Skill on software engineering tasks, using
              tests derived from task requirements and reporting token cost alongside task success (Han et al.,
              2026). In its tested setting, many Skills did not change the pass rate and some increased token use.
              These results assess the supplied Skills in that setting; they do not by themselves show whether an
              evolutionary update improves a Skill.
                   Safety and compatibility also affect continued use. A Skill may depend on tools, permissions,
              or assumptions about an environment that do not hold elsewhere. SKILL-INJECT studies attacks
              that use misleading Skill content or descriptions to redirect an agent (Schmotz et al., 2026). For Skills
              obtained from outside the system, testing task utility alone is therefore insufficient to establish that
              their instructions and required actions are safe to use.

              8.1. Starting Points and Editable Parts
                    An evolving Harness can start from a small executable system or from an existing agent product.
              The starting point affects both what is available to the agent and what an update can change. A study
              should therefore be read in light of the initial tools, control logic, and access granted to the editor,
              rather than treating every update as if it rebuilt the whole system.

              they retain.
                    HarnessDev separates initial construction from subsequent evolution. In its creation setting, a
              model builds a Harness from a weak but runnable seed and development cases. In its evolution setting,
              it revises the resulting Harness using execution feedback. The benchmark evaluates the constructed
              systems on held-out tasks and compares them with human-engineered references; gains from later
              revisions are not always stable or transferable (Y. Wu, Zhang, Shi, Lei, et al., 2026). This distinction
              separates the ability to build an initial system from the ability to improve one over repeated updates.

              (H. Zhang, Zhang, et al., 2026). HarnessPro, by contrast, reports experiments using Codex and Claude
              Code as existing agent environments (Pan et al., 2026). Its edits concern accessible instructions, tools,
              hooks, and related files; using a mature product as a starting point does not imply unrestricted changes
              to its internal implementation. Across these settings, an update can be a configuration change, a new
              check, or a code revision, with different consequences for later execution.

              8.2. From Execution Experience to a Harness Modification
                    Task failures can reveal a missing tool, an unproductive retry, or a check that the agent skipped.
              They do not by themselves identify the responsible code. Harness evolution must connect what
              happened during execution to a change the system can make. Experience may be analyzed after a
              batch of runs or used as tasks arrive; collecting a trajectory during execution does not mean that the
              Harness was modified during that same run.
                    HarnessFix aligns steps in failed trajectories with the Harness artifacts involved in those steps. It
              groups recurring diagnoses into flaw records, uses them to generate scoped patches, and evaluates the
              patches with regression-aware checks (M. Chen, Wang, Liu, et al., 2026). The important step is the link
              from a failure to an editable target: an unsuccessful tool call supplies evidence for diagnosis, while a
              test of the proposed patch addresses whether the change helps.
                    ModularRSI also limits edits to identified parts of a Harness, but compares multiple executions of
              the same task. When both successful and failed trajectories are available, it contrasts them to identify
              recurring problems; when all attempts fail, it analyzes the failures or consults earlier trajectories.
              Findings across tasks guide changes to individual modules, which are then checked and integrated
              (S. Wu et al., 2026). This comparison uses differences within a task as well as repeated evidence across
              tasks to select modification targets.
                    AHE collects multiple execution traces for each task and makes the raw records and summaries
              available to a debugger. The debugger analyzes failure causes or successful patterns; an evolver
              inspects this analysis and the editable files, proposes changes, and records their expected effects.
              Outcomes in the next round are used to inspect the changes and revert ineffective edits (Lin et al.,
              2026). Execution, debugging, and evolution have different roles, but the paper reports that they use
              the same base model. A change can be tried in the next round and checked against its results rather
              than independently verified before use.
                    AutoHarness instead uses environmental feedback on action legality to improve checking code. It
              summarizes types of illegal actions, revises the checker, and measures action legality on new execution
              traces (Lou et al., 2026). This test concerns the action checker; a higher legality rate alone does not
              establish a higher success rate on the full task.

              8.3. Who Proposes Changes and How Candidates Are Generated
                    The agent that performs tasks and the agent that proposes Harness changes can be the same
              model or different models. They may also operate with different tools and information. These roles
              matter because the editor must obtain enough evidence to understand the executor’s failures, while a
              change to the executor’s Harness need not change the editor’s own way of working.
                    Self-Harness studies whether an agent can improve its own Harness without a stronger external
              agent. It collects failed held-in trajectories and verifier feedback, groups behaviors associated with fail-
              ure, and proposes small changes targeting model-specific weaknesses. Candidates undergo regression
              checks, and accepted edits are merged into the next version (H. Zhang, Zhang, et al., 2026). Acceptance
              uses both held-in and held-out scores. Although held-out traces are hidden from the proposer, scores
              used for candidate selection cannot be treated as a fully independent final test.
                    Meta-Harness uses an outer coding agent to search over Harness code. The proposer can inspect
              earlier candidates’ source code, scores, and trajectories in a filesystem, then edit code to produce a new
              candidate. The system evaluates candidates on search tasks, saves candidates and their results, and
              maintains a Pareto frontier for later search (Y. Lee et al., 2026). Access to history does not establish that
              every record was used; the records are available to inform later proposals, while Harness code is the
              object directly revised.
                    Harness-R1 trains an engineering model to turn failures of a frozen target agent into executable
              Harness patches (Shao et al., 2026). This changes how the editor learns to propose a patch, even though
              the target agent is held fixed in the reported setting. When training is involved, identifying which
              model’s parameters change is necessary to understand which part of the system is being improved.
                    Harness Learning likewise trains a proposer to revise the code used by a fixed solver. It evaluates
              candidate edits on task questions and uses their outcomes to train the proposer; on new tasks, the
              trained proposer uses execution reports to make further revisions (A. Zhang et al., 2026). The proposer
              is trained before test-time adaptation; during adaptation, both models remain fixed while the Harness
              changes.

              8.4. Adapting to a Task and Improving Across Tasks
                    A Harness may be generated for one task, updated for future tasks, or used in both ways. These
              settings make different uses of experience. A task-specific candidate can help with the current problem
              without being retained; a retained configuration or lesson can also influence how the system handles
              later problems.
                    JIT-Agent generates a Harness conditioned on the current task and validates the result, with a
              bounded repair process for invalid candidates. The paper trains its Harness generator, while keeping
              its task executor fixed during the described training (G. Zhang et al., 2026). At inference it distinguishes
              a mode that selects a Harness for the current task from a streaming mode that can add useful designs
              to a bank for later tasks. The generator remains fixed at deployment. Thus per-task generation and
              cross-task reuse are two settings of the method, not mutually exclusive descriptions of it.
                    Continual Harness revises components during a single ongoing task. At intervals, a refiner
              examines recent interaction history for failures and changes instructions, subagents, Skills, or Memory;
              the updated contents are used as execution continues without resetting the environment (Karten et al.,
              2026). The paper also studies a separate model-training loop, whereas this refinement loop changes
              the external contents used during execution.
                    TTHE updates an executable Harness across batches of test inputs without access to gold labels or
              hidden tests during adaptation. Proposers revise candidate code using execution traces and proxy
              signals, such as execution errors and public-test results when available. A judge selects a candidate
              to carry into the next batch (Nie et al., 2026). These signals can help select a candidate but do not
              guarantee task correctness; the paper uses gold answers after selection to measure performance.
                    MemoHarness takes another approach: it stores individual execution diagnoses and patterns
              distilled across cases, then retrieves relevant experience to adapt a Harness configuration to a new

              8.5. Testing, Selecting, and Retaining Changes
                    A candidate Harness can improve one task while breaking another, or raise success at the expense
              of more calls and longer execution. Evaluation therefore needs to cover the tasks and resources relevant
              to the intended use. Held-out tasks and out-of-distribution tests assess transfer, while regression checks
              examine whether a change damages previously supported behavior. Candidate selection and final
              measurement should also be distinguished when the same feedback influences repeated edits.
                    HarnessPro analyzes repeated executions for failures and avoidable cost, then produces candidate
              changes with different performance and cost priorities. During evolution it uses model-generated tests
              rather than ground-truth solutions or official tests. It filters candidates using validation performance
              and cost, retains representative low-cost, balanced, and performance-oriented versions, and evaluates
              the final versions on a separate test set (Pan et al., 2026). Its Pareto comparison is part of selection as
              well as a way to describe trade-offs: a candidate is dominated when another is at least as good on both
              measured dimensions and better on one.
                    RRSI addresses a different selection concern: repeated changes can fit the tasks used during
              evolution. It limits the number of edits in a proposal, records earlier gains and regressions, and screens
              candidates for task-specific content. Candidate selection also considers evaluation noise and added
              complexity (Xia, Han, et al., 2026). These checks constrain the search and selection process; transfer
              still needs to be measured on other tasks.
                    JIT-Agent also measures task reward, latency, and monetary cost when assessing candidates
              (G. Zhang et al., 2026). Such measurements should state which costs are counted. The resources spent
              generating, testing, and revising Harnesses can differ from the cost of using the final Harness on later
              tasks; both affect whether an improvement is worthwhile.
                    An evaluation study compares Harness evolution with task-level approaches, including parallel
              sampling and sequential refinement, under comparable feedback and inference budgets (Y. Wang et al.,
              2026). On its tested Terminal-Bench settings, Harness evolution does not consistently outperform those
              alternatives and transfers only to a limited extent. The result motivates comparisons that separate
              gains from a reusable Harness from gains obtained by spending more search effort on the evaluated
              tasks.

              8.6. Harness Evolution and Further Self-Improvement
                   A revised Harness can change later task execution, but that alone does not show that the system
              has improved how it makes the next revision. A stronger connection to recursive self-improvement
              arises when earlier updates or their outcomes also change the evidence, tools, or procedures used
              to produce later updates. The retained object and the improvement process should be identified
              separately.
                   VERSE studies this distinction directly. An optimizer revises a task executor’s Harness and
              also updates its own prompts, Skills, tools, hooks, and notes between rounds. It can run draft edits,
              replay recorded failures, and perturb suspected steps before submission; an audit records fixes and
              regressions across rounds (Z. Wang et al., 2026). Both optimizer and executor model weights remain

              on the reviewed trajectories (Ye et al., 2026). At deployment, the specialized Harness and reviewer are
              removed, but the student still operates under a fixed target Harness.

              9. When Components Are Updated Again
                  After a component is formed, new tasks, interactions, or environmental changes can prompt an
              agent to modify it again. Studies use different triggers: task failures, new facts, and dependencies
              between components. What the system retains also affects whether the results can participate in later
              improvements.

              9.1. What Triggers Further Updates
                    Task failures or declining performance can prompt a system to inspect an existing component.
              SkillForge compares agent responses with expert references, analyzes recurring problems across
              support tickets, and revises the relevant Skill (X. Liu et al., 2026). Self-Harness instead identifies
              model-specific weaknesses from failed trajectories and proposes Harness changes (H. Zhang, Zhang,
              et al., 2026). Both use failures, but the former revises procedural content while the latter changes
              execution mechanisms. Failure alone does not specify what to edit; the system must analyze what
              happened.
                    New information can also trigger an update without a failed task. Mem0 extracts facts from
              conversations and compares them with existing memories before choosing to add, update, delete, or
              make no change (Chhikara et al., 2025). Zep adjusts the validity period of a relationship when new and
              old facts conflict in time, while retaining the historical record (Rasmussen et al., 2025). The question
              here is how new and existing information should coexist, rather than whether a task succeeded or
              failed.
                    Changes to a model, tool, or task can also make a component unsuitable. For example, a changed
              tool interface may make field information in Memory or calling steps in a Skill outdated. A system
              must identify what the change affects before deciding whether to revise a record, modify a procedure,
              or adjust a Harness check; more than one component may be involved.

              9.3. When to Retain, Revise, or Retire
                    Proposing a change and accepting it are different decisions. Self-Harness runs regression checks
              and merges a candidate into the next version only when it meets the acceptance conditions (H. Zhang,
              Zhang, et al., 2026). AHE instead allows an edit to be used in the next round, then retains or reverts it
              based on the results (Lin et al., 2026). Verification can occur before use or draw on feedback from a
              trial; the tested tasks and acceptance conditions affect what is retained.
                    Later information can also lead a system to revise content or stop using it. Mem0 can modify or
              delete records after comparing new and existing facts; Zep preserves the historical validity of outdated
              facts so they can still answer questions about the past (Chhikara et al., 2025; Rasmussen et al., 2025).
              Information that is no longer current does not always need to be deleted from the historical record.
                    For a Skill library, SLIM compares validation performance with and without a Skill and uses this
              evidence together with usage information to decide whether to retain or retire it. Persistently failing
              tasks can also prompt the addition of new Skills (Shen et al., 2026). Retirement changes what can be
              selected later, so the check needs to cover intended uses, including less frequent but still important
              tasks.
                    Who a correction applies to, how long it remains useful, and whether it needs to be enforced also
              affect its maintenance. For example, an address correction should apply to the relevant user and time,
              while preventing invalid tool calls may require a Harness check. Scope and authority depend on the
              task and available evidence.

              9.4. When the Improvement Process Itself Changes
                   Retained components can directly help with later tasks, or they can help propose or select the
              next change. The latter has a more direct connection to recursive self-improvement: earlier experience
              affects not only what the agent does but also how the system improves. Using earlier experience in
              another update does not by itself prove that the update method has become more effective.
                   Harness2 stores selected components and task experience in an improvement library that can
              inform Harness changes on later tasks. Each task still starts from a common initial execution Harness,
              and the stage instructions and decision rules remain fixed (R. Xu et al., 2026). The paper reports that
              the accumulated library helps later improvement. What changes here is the set of components and
              experience available to later edits, not every update rule.
                   VERSE also revises the Prompt, Skills, tools, hooks, and notes used by its improver while modify-
              ing the task executor’s Harness; both models’ weights remain fixed (Z. Wang et al., 2026). It combines
              execution checks available to the improver with self-modification and studies their effect on subsequent
              improvement. The modified content also participates directly in producing later edits.
                   SePO first evolves the system Prompt of a Prompt Agent that proposes changes across a set of
              tasks. It then fixes the evolved Prompt Agent and uses it to optimize a target agent’s Prompt (Tao et al.,
              2026). The guidance for generating candidates can itself be updated, but the two stages should be
              distinguished: the guidance does not continue to evolve simultaneously in the second stage.
                   Determining whether an improvement process is more effective requires examining later update
              outcomes and what else changed. Better final task performance may result from better components,

              10. Evaluating Continued Improvement
                   Evaluating component evolution involves more than measuring task scores after an update. It
              also asks what the change contributed, whether its effects last, and whether later updates benefit.
              These questions require different comparisons and evidence.

              10.1. Does the Update Improve Task Performance?
                   The most direct question is whether the agent performs better with the updated component. Its
              performance before and after the update can be compared on the same tasks and checked on tasks not
              used to produce the change. Alongside average scores, reports should identify failures or regressions
              and variation across runs.
                   When performance improves, a further comparison can examine the contribution of a particular
              component. For example, compare the same tasks with and without a Memory item or Skill. Com-
              parisons between Prompt, Memory, and Harness solutions should state the information, model, and
              budget available to each. Table 7 links common questions to corresponding comparisons.

              comparisons should also count model calls, tool use, and time spent generating and testing candidates.

              edits on a selection set and reserves a test set for its final results (Y. Yang, Gong, et al., 2026). Self-

              from the proposer, but their scores still participate in selection (H. Zhang, Zhang, et al., 2026). The
              information available to the editor differs across these settings and matters when interpreting the
              results.

              10.2. Does the Improvement Last?
                   An update can improve a current task, yet retained content may become outdated or conflict
              with new information as tasks, tools, or models change. Continued evaluation follows multiple
              rounds: what was retained, when it was later used, whether new experience led to revisions, and how
              performance and costs changed.
                   Historical facts in Memory, applicability conditions in Skills, and tool interfaces in Harness can
              change in different ways. For example, Zep retains temporal information to support questions about
              current and past facts (Rasmussen et al., 2025); SLIM uses Skill usage and contribution to decide what
              to retain or retire (Shen et al., 2026). Alongside task scores, evaluations can record retrieval errors,
              failed calls, update costs, and recovery time.
                   Reuse across cycles and improvement of the update method are also separate questions. Harness2
              retains components and experience in an improvement library for later task-specific changes; VERSE
              additionally modifies some of the content used by its improver (Z. Wang et al., 2026; R. Xu et al., 2026).

              HarnessPro considers both task performance and cost and retains candidate versions with different
              trade-offs (Pan et al., 2026). Reporting these costs alongside task gains shows what improvement
              requires during later use.

              10.3. Are the Evaluation Results Reliable?
                    Evaluation depends on the feedback and checks used. Environmental rewards, test programs,
              LLM judgments, and human review observe different information: automated tests check behaviors
              they cover, while an LLM can supply textual feedback but may also make mistakes. Reports should
              state which feedback generates edits, which selects candidates, and which data and evaluation method
              produce the final results.
                    When an evaluator changes, scores can change with the standard itself. Comparisons should
              record evaluator versions and the information available to them; when acceptance criteria change, the
              same tasks can be used to recheck old and new versions. Multiple model judges can share errors, so
              their number alone does not ensure independent judgments.
                    Experience carried into later updates also raises security questions. Malicious content can enter
              Memory or Skills and later be retrieved, invoked, or used to generate new changes. SKILL-INJECT
              and SkillGuardBench study Skill-related attacks and defenses (Lv et al., 2026; Schmotz et al., 2026).
              Evaluation can examine whether later tasks use malicious content and whether source records, permis-
              sion checks, and rollback limit its effects. Protection mechanisms differ across systems; reporting the
              checks actually performed is more informative than a single aggregate safety score.

              10.5. Reporting and Reproducibility
                   Reproducing continued improvement requires records of the final system and of the update
              process. Studies should describe the models, components, tools, and tasks; sources of experience and
              feedback; edits made in each round; candidate selection; and data available at each stage. Component
              versions and edit histories help readers determine what later tasks used.
                   Reports should also include update and verification costs, variation in results, failures, and the
              recovery or rollback methods actually used. When data or components cannot be released, their
              roles in the experiment should be stated so readers can identify which results can be independently
              reproduced.

              10.6. Limitations of the Survey and Framework
                   This survey selects studies purposefully to analyze reported components and update methods. It
              supports qualitative comparison, but does not estimate the prevalence of designs across the field. A
              system can combine several components, and its classification depends on the particular use analyzed;
              these classifications have not been independently double-coded.
                   The seven stages are an analytical tool for the selected update cycles. One operation can serve
              multiple functions, and an unreported operation does not establish that a system never uses it.
              Component classifications and stage mappings need to be examined on further systems and revised as
              evidence warrants.

              11. Conclusion
                    This survey compares methods that use interaction experience to evolve Prompt, Memory, Skill,
              and Harness in LLM agents. Studies differ in the experience they collect, the components they modify,
              and how they test and retain changes. The seven stages provide a shared perspective for comparing
              these update processes while revealing differences in practice: some operations are combined, and
              some methods examine a change only after it has been used.
                    The studies also connect to recursive self-improvement in different ways. Updated components
              can help agents perform later tasks; in some methods, retained experience or components also par-
              ticipate in producing the next change. Harness2 uses accumulated components and experience to
              guide later Harness improvements, VERSE also modifies components used by its improver, and SePO
              first evolves guidance for proposing Prompt changes before using it to optimize task Prompts. These
              approaches show how experience can influence later improvement and raise an evaluation question:
              how much does existing experience help a system produce more effective changes?
                    Future research can examine which experiences support later updates and how to reduce inter-
              ference from erroneous or irrelevant experience. When components depend on one another, it is also
              important to understand when they should change together and how each affects the result. Long-term
              evaluation needs to consider later task performance, the effects of repeated updates, and the costs of
              verification and maintenance as tasks, tools, and models change.
