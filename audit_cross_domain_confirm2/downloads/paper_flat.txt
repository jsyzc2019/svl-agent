Abstract

While the ability of video generation models to accurately depict scientific
phenomena has received growing attention, determining whether these models
genuinely understand the underlying principles remains a
critical challenge. Existing benchmarks typically evaluate video samples in
isolation, making it difficult to expose models that simply fit superficial
visual priors. In scientific experiments, a small change in one condition
produces a markedly different outcome even when the rest of the setup remains
fixed. Capitalizing on this sensitivity, we introduce
SciWorldBench, a benchmark comprising paired scientific experiments. Each pair
varies a single condition in an otherwise fixed setup, yielding two mutually
exclusive expected outcomes. Each generation prompt specifies the condition
but omits the expected outcome. We evaluate
ten text-and-image-to-video (TI2V) models using an independent vision-language
model (VLM) that assesses each video without access to the condition text or
the label identifying the correct outcome. A pair is scored as correct only
when both videos depict their respective scientifically correct outcomes.
Across all ten models, we find that the two videos in an experimental pair
frequently depict the same outcome even though the conditions require different
results. This recurring failure suggests that current video world models
struggle to apply the underlying scientific principles reliably during
generation.

with realistic appearance and smooth motion from a first frame and a text
prompt. These models are increasingly used as video world models to simulate
scene evolution. In scientific experiments, changing a single condition can lead to a
markedly different outcome while the rest of the setup remains unchanged. Consider an orange released into
water: air in the intact peel lowers the fruit’s average density, allowing it
to float, whereas a peeled orange sinks. The two experiments otherwise share
the same setup. In the example shown in 
Fig.
 
1

in which the orange floats under both conditions, although the peeled orange
should sink. Generating the correct outcome under one condition therefore does not
establish that the model understands how the experimental condition affects
the outcome. This failure motivates our central question: do video world
models really understand scientific experiments?

Existing benchmarks broaden task coverage and refine evaluation
criteria 
[
2
, 
11

 to assess whether models can generate
physically correct videos from prompts specifying scenes, events, and
outcomes 
[
3

. However, generating the correct outcome
does not establish that the model derived it from the given conditions
through causal reasoning. A complementary line of work examines responses
to controlled changes in autonomous driving and robotic
manipulation 
[
7

 and simulated physical
scenes 
[
4
, 
20
, 
19

. Their focus on embodied interactions
and mechanical events provides limited evidence of whether models understand
condition–outcome relationships across a broader range of scientific
experiments.

We introduce SciWorldBench, a benchmark of 222 experiment pairs across
eight categories. Each pair holds the setup fixed and varies one condition,
with mutually exclusive expected outcomes. Generation prompts specify the
condition but omit the outcome. Half of the pairs use an identical first
frame, so the condition is specified only in text. In the other half, the
condition is visible in the first frame. The two videos are generated
in separate requests, and a pair passes only when both show their respective
scientifically correct outcomes.
A VLM uses visual evidence to check experimental prerequisites and
separately assess whether each candidate outcome occurs. We score a video
as correct only when all prerequisite checks pass and the judgments confirm
the expected outcome while ruling out the alternative.

Across ten TI2V models, Paired Accuracy (PA) ranges from 1.4% to 24.8%,
and the highest Scientific Outcome Accuracy (SOA) is 53.8%.
A recurring failure is that models generate the same outcome despite the
contrasting conditions: among pairs in which both videos pass the prerequisite
checks and each shows exactly one outcome, the Collapse Rate (CR) is
55.7%–91.7%. Providing the correct outcome in the prompt
improves generation for all six tested open-weight models, but
most still tend to generate the same outcome under both conditions.
The highest PA is 33.3%, and CR exceeds 50% for five of the six models.
Both Cosmos models combine understanding and generation, yet their
ability to infer experimental outcomes in text does not reliably carry over
to video generation.
Cosmos3-Super, for example, predicts both outcomes correctly in text
for 63.1% of pairs but generates both correctly in only 9.0%.

Our contributions are as follows.

1.

We introduce SciWorldBench, a benchmark of 222 experiment pairs across
eight categories for evaluating whether video world models understand
scientific experiments. Each pair specifies contrasting experimental
conditions and their distinct, visually verifiable outcomes.

2.

We establish an evaluation protocol that distinguishes unmet experimental
prerequisites from incorrect outcomes and assesses correctness under each
condition. The VLM identifies the outcome shown in a video without receiving
the condition text or the correct-outcome label, and we validate its
judgments against human consensus.

3.

We evaluated 10 mainstream TI2V models on our benchmark. Across all the
models, the highest PA was 24.8%, and paired videos frequently showed the
same outcome even though the two conditions required different results.
These findings show that current video world models lack reliable scientific
understanding in generation.

3.2 
Benchmark Construction

We use GPT-5.6-sol 
[
29
]
 to generate 600 candidate pairs of 8-second
video experiments, following the pipeline in 
Fig.
 
1
. Each
candidate defines a shared experimental setup and two alternative conditions,
which are combined to form a pair of video generation prompts. Each candidate
also specifies the expected outcome under each condition and a brief
explanation of the causal relationship between the condition and its expected
outcome. We then conduct expert review to assess the candidates along three
dimensions. (1) For 
clarity of the experimental description
, reviewers
check that each candidate specifies the shared setup and how the two conditions
differ. (2) For 
scientific validity
, they verify that each condition
leads to its stated outcome under that setup. (3) To assess

suitability for video evaluation
, they check whether each outcome is
observable within eight seconds and distinguishable from the alternative using
only the video frames. After review and refinement, 222 pairs
are retained. We then use GPT-6 Astra 
[
30
]
 to reformulate the video
generation prompts into a structured format with four labeled fields: scene,
condition, procedure, and camera requirements. The two video generation
prompts in each pair differ only in the condition field, and neither
explicitly specifies the expected outcome or its explanation.

In the TI2V setting, each experimental condition is associated with a first
frame. We generate and edit these reference frames using Qwen-Image
2.1 
[
31
]
. The side-A frame is generated from the shared
experimental setup and its corresponding condition. When the condition is
visually distinguishable, the side-B frame is edited from the side-A frame
while preserving the scene layout, camera viewpoint, and unrelated objects.
When the condition is not visually distinguishable in a static image, as in
the ice cube experiments with water and alcohol (
Fig.
 
2
), the same
first frame is used for both sides, with the difference specified only in the
generation prompts. All reference frames are initially created at

2048
×
2048
2048\times 2048

before video generation.

Finally, we organize the 222 pairs into eight categories based on the
underlying physical mechanisms (
Fig.
 
3
). We also divide them
into two equally sized subsets according to whether the paired videos share
the same first frame: 111 same-first-frame pairs and 111 different-first-frame
pairs.

3.3 
Evaluation Protocol

We use doubao-seed-2-1-pro 
[
5
]
 at temperature 0 to evaluate the
two videos in each experimental pair separately (
Fig.
 
1
). For
each video, the VLM receives 49 full-resolution frames sampled uniformly
over time, their timestamps, and a description of the shared experimental
setup. We withhold the condition-specific text, the name of the generating
model, the A/B side label, and the label identifying the expected outcome.

We use the same five questions to score both videos in a pair. We shuffle the question order
for each video using a fixed seed. Three questions check whether the
target’s behavior is visible, whether it remains the same object throughout
the video, and whether the experimental preconditions are met. The other two
questions each ask whether the frames show a particular candidate outcome.
We describe each outcome in terms of observable behavior, removing
condition-specific attributes and referring to the target in neutral terms.
For example, we rewrite “The peeled orange sinks to the bottom” as “The round
fruit sinks through the water and settles on the bottom of the tank.”

For each question, the VLM returns an answer, a brief observation, and a
rationale in a fixed JSON format.
Both 
yes
 and 
no

indices of at least two supporting frames and the time interval they span.
When the visual evidence is insufficient, the VLM answers 
unknown
.
We score a video as correct only if all three prerequisite checks pass and
the VLM answers 
yes
 for the outcome expected under that condition
and 
no
 for the alternative.

4.3 
Outcome Stated in the Prompt

To test whether stating the expected outcome reduces this repetition,
we append the correct outcome to each condition’s original prompt and regenerate
the videos with the six open-weight models. We score the new videos using the
same protocol.

With the outcome given, all six models achieve higher SOA and PA, and
all six show lower CR (
Table
 
5
). Averaged across models, PA rises from 5.6% to 19.5%.
MiniMax-H3 shows the largest improvement. Its PA rises from 7.2% to 33.3%,
the highest among the six models, and its CR falls from 84.3% to 40.2%.
Wan2.2-TI2V-5B shows the smallest reduction in CR, from 91.7% to 81.1%. Even
with explicit outcome guidance, five of the six models still generate the same
outcome under both conditions in more than half of their decided pairs.

4.4 
Predicting Experimental Outcomes in Text

We return to the original prompts, which omit the expected outcome,
and compare how often Cosmos3-Super and Cosmos3-Nano predict both outcomes
correctly in text with how often they depict both correctly in video.
For each condition, we provide the full original generation prompt, which
specifies the scene, condition, procedure, and camera requirements. We omit the first frame and ask what happens.
The question is identical for both conditions of a pair. We manually
assess each answer against the expected outcome for its condition.
Both models predict the two outcomes correctly in text far more often
than they generate them correctly in video (
Table
 
5
).
Cosmos3-Super gives correct text answers for both conditions in 63.1% of
pairs, compared with a video PA of 9.0%. For Cosmos3-Nano, the corresponding
rates are 44.6% and 5.4%.
Even when these models correctly infer both outcomes in text,
they often fail to depict those outcomes in their respective videos.

Results by First-Frame Group

5

original generation setting, with 111 experimental pairs in each group. When
the condition is visible in the first frame, PA ranges from 2.7% to 30.6%.
When the two conditions share a first frame and differ only in text, PA ranges

This pattern suggests that models are less responsive to a change
in experimental conditions when the prompt provides the only cue to that change.

Each of the eight scientific categories contains 16 to 34 experimental

5 
Discussion

Our empirical findings expose a sharp divide between recognizing what should happen
and synthesizing how it unfolds. In this section, we trace this failure to two underlying
bottlenecks—outcome-repetition bias stemming from generation without reasoning,
and a modality disconnect between reasoning and generation.

Outcome-repetition bias stems from generation without reasoning.

Because this bias is systematic, persists under explicit instruction,
and occurs across independent generation requests, it reflects a shared prior
rather than cross-run copying. We attribute this bias to visual associations
that text-and-image-to-video (TI2V) models learn from observational videos.
During generation, the models rely more heavily on the association between a
scene’s initial appearance and a familiar outcome than on the experimental
condition specified in the prompt. As a result, they favor the same outcome
in both videos of a pair even when the two conditions require different results.
Current models behave more like 
renderers
 than world simulators,
producing visually realistic videos that often fail to show how changing an
experimental condition alters the outcome. Even when the expected outcome is explicitly stated in
the prompt, they merely translate that label into surface rendering, accounting
for their partial compliance without true reasoning.

The text–video gap reflects a modality disconnect.
 Both unified Cosmos
models predict the correct outcomes for the two experimental conditions far
more often in text than they depict them in video
(
Tabs.
 
5
 and 
4.4
). This gap may partly reflect an intrinsic
difference in task demands. In the text task, the model makes a low-dimensional
judgment about which outcome follows from the stated experimental condition.
Video generation poses a higher-dimensional reasoning problem because the model
must map that same condition to a visual trajectory that captures how the scene
evolves across space and time to produce the corresponding outcome. Even when
the prompt supplies the correct outcome, removing the need to infer it, the best
video PA among the six tested open-weight models is 33.3% (
Tab.
 
5
).
In the original setting, all ten models show higher CR for pairs distinguished
only by text than for pairs in which the condition difference is also visible
in the first frame (
Tab.
 
5
). Together, these findings suggest
that textual information sufficient to identify an outcome does not reliably
guide its realization in video. Whether this difficulty arises from a mismatch
between the representations used for inference and generation remains open.

6 
Conclusion

We introduced SciWorldBench to test whether video world models understand
scientific experiments. In each of its 222 pairs, two versions of the same
experiment differ in one condition and have mutually exclusive expected outcomes.
A VLM assesses each video without access to the condition text or the
expected-outcome label. A pair passes only if both videos depict the
scientifically correct outcomes for their respective conditions. Across ten
TI2V models, paired accuracy (PA) ranges from 1.4% to 24.8%. Models often
repeat the same outcome under both conditions, particularly when the first
frame is shared and only the text distinguishes the two inputs. Stating the
correct outcome improves generation for all six tested open-weight models,
yet the highest PA is 33.3%. The two Cosmos models predict both outcomes
correctly in text far more often than they depict them correctly in video.
Together, these results suggest that current video world models have a limited
understanding of how experimental conditions determine outcomes.

Future work could use paired training videos to help models learn how a change
in one experimental condition affects the outcome. When both videos start from
the same frame, the model must use the condition stated in the prompt to infer
which outcome should occur. A latent or symbolic representation of that
prediction could guide video synthesis and provide a reference for checking the
generated result. Beyond checking whether an individual video matches that
prediction and appears plausible, benchmarks should test whether each video in
a controlled pair depicts the scientifically correct outcome for its condition.

Limitations.

Two limitations warrant consideration. First, our
mechanistic interpretation is derived from behavioral outputs and text
predictions rather than from probing internal representations; the deeper
mechanisms through which video generation models produce these phenomena remain
to be investigated. Second, the accuracy of our evaluation depends heavily on
the VLM judge: even with a state-of-the-art model, its agreement with human
annotators remains imperfect, which can introduce scoring errors.
